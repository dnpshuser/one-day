# Election Commission of India (ECI)

### Additional Focus Areas: The ECI Appointment and Removal Mechanics

**1. How are the CEC and ECs appointed?**
The appointment process is currently governed by the *Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act, 2023*. The process operates in a two-tier mechanism:

* **The Search Committee:** Headed by the Cabinet Secretary (and comprising two other members of Secretary rank), this committee shortlists a panel of five eligible names. Eligibility requires candidates to be persons of integrity with experience in election management who have held a post equivalent to Secretary to the central government.
* **The Selection Committee:** The final recommendation to the President is made by a three-member Selection Committee consisting of the Prime Minister (Chairperson), a Union Cabinet Minister (nominated by the PM), and the Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha (or the leader of the single largest opposition party).
* **Presidential Appointment:** The President formally appoints the CEC and ECs based on this binding recommendation. The Act also clarifies that the Selection Committee can validly make recommendations even if there is a vacancy in the committee, and it can consider candidates outside the Search Committee's shortlist.

**2. Why did the Supreme Court intervene in appointments?**
The Supreme Court intervened through a Constitution Bench in the *Anoop Baranwal v. Union of India (2023)* case to cure a 73-year-old constitutional vacuum.

* Article 324(2) of the Constitution states that the appointment of the CEC and ECs shall be made by the President, *"subject to the provisions of any law made in that behalf by Parliament"*.
* For over seven decades, successive Parliaments failed to enact such a law. Consequently, appointments were made entirely at the sole discretion of the Executive (the Prime Minister advising the President).
* The Court ruled that leaving the appointment of an election watchdog entirely to the executive threatens the perception of neutrality and could affect public confidence. The intervention was strictly to insulate the Election Commission from executive interference, as a truly independent ECI is a prerequisite for free and fair elections, which is a basic structure of the Constitution.

**3. What did the Anoop Baranwal judgment prescribe?**
The *Anoop Baranwal* judgment did not attempt to permanently rewrite the Constitution. Instead, it prescribed an **interim arrangement** to fill the constitutional gap.

* The Court issued a writ of mandamus establishing a temporary, independent collegium to advise the President on ECI appointments.
* The judgment explicitly stated that this judicial mechanism would only operate *until* Parliament fulfilled its constitutional duty under Article 324(2) to enact a dedicated law governing the appointment process. (This ultimately prompted Parliament to pass the 2023 Act, superseding the Court's interim mechanism).

**4. Who was on the appointment panel prescribed by the Supreme Court?**
To ensure a balance of power and strip the executive of its monopoly, the Supreme Court prescribed a three-member neutral selection committee comprising:

1. **The Prime Minister**
2. **The Leader of the Opposition in the Lok Sabha** (or the leader of the single largest opposition party)
3. **The Chief Justice of India (CJI)**

*(Note: The subsequent 2023 Act passed by Parliament replaced the CJI with a Union Cabinet Minister, shifting the balance back to a 2:1 executive majority, which remains a subject of intense political and legal scrutiny.)*

**5. What protection does a CEC have against removal?**
To shield the institution from political pressure, the Constitution deliberately sets an exceptionally high bar for removing a Chief Election Commissioner.

* **Constitutional Shield (Article 324(5)):** The CEC can only be removed from office *"in like manner and on the like grounds as a Judge of the Supreme Court"*. This provision is explicitly retained in Section 11(2) of the 2023 Act.
* **The Impeachment Process:** Removal can only occur on the grounds of "proved misbehaviour or incapacity". It requires an impeachment motion to be admitted (signed by 100 MPs in Lok Sabha or 50 in Rajya Sabha), followed by an independent inquiry.
* **Special Majority:** The motion must then secure a special majority—support of a majority of the total membership of each House *and* a two-thirds majority of members present and voting in both Houses of Parliament.
* **Vulnerability of other ECs:** In stark contrast, the other Election Commissioners do not possess this robust constitutional shield. They can be removed from office simply on the recommendation of the CEC.

## 1. Intro

* The Election Commission of India (ECI) is a permanent, autonomous constitutional body established on January 25, 1950, under Article 324 of the Constitution.
* It is vested with the absolute power of superintendence, direction, and control over the entire process of conducting elections to Parliament, State Legislatures, and the offices of the President and Vice-President of India, serving as the institutional guarantor of the world's largest democracy.

## 2. Sub-components of the topic [most imp]

* **Composition (Article 324(2)):** The ECI consists of a Chief Election Commissioner (CEC) and such number of other Election Commissioners (ECs) as the President may fix. Since 1993, it functions as a multi-member body comprising one CEC and two ECs, sharing equal powers and salary.
* **How are the CEC and ECs appointed?** Constitutionally, they are appointed by the President. Historically, this was done purely on the advice of the Prime Minister (Executive discretion). Currently, under the *CEC and Other ECs (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act, 2023*, they are appointed by the President on the recommendation of a Selection Committee consisting of the Prime Minister (Chairperson), the Leader of the Opposition (or leader of the single largest opposition party), and a Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the PM.
* **Protection of the CEC against Removal (Article 324(5)):** The CEC enjoys supreme security of tenure. They cannot be removed from office except in "like manner and on like grounds as a Judge of the Supreme Court" (i.e., a resolution passed by a special majority in both Houses of Parliament on grounds of proved misbehavior or incapacity).
* **The Vulnerability of ECs:** Unlike the CEC, the other two Election Commissioners do not enjoy this constitutional protection. They can be removed from office simply on the recommendation of the CEC, creating a structural imbalance in independence.
* **Functions:** Delimitation of constituencies, preparation of electoral rolls, recognition of political parties, allotment of symbols, and enforcement of the Model Code of Conduct (MCC).

## 3. Evolution timeline

* **1950-1989 (Single-Member Body):** The ECI functioned as a single-member body consisting only of the CEC.
* **1989 (The Voting Age Shift):** Following the 61st Amendment (reducing voting age from 21 to 18), the workload increased. The President appointed two more ECs, making it a multi-member body briefly before reverting back in 1990.
* **1993 (Permanent Multi-Member Status):** The President promulgated an ordinance permanently fixing the composition to one CEC and two ECs. The Supreme Court upheld this in the *T.N. Seshan case (1995)*, confirming equal powers among all three.
* **March 2023 (Anoop Baranwal Judgment):** A landmark Supreme Court Constitution Bench struck down the executive's absolute monopoly on ECI appointments, prescribing a temporary neutral collegium.
* **December 2023 (The New Act):** Parliament passed the *Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners (Appointment, Conditions of Service and Term of Office) Act, 2023*, establishing a statutory selection committee that replaced the CJI with a Union Minister, sparking intense ongoing constitutional debates.

## 5. Committee or reports [latest is best or some report in the past with very large impact]

* **Law Commission 255th Report (2015):** Strongly recommended elevating the legal protection of the two ECs to match the CEC (requiring impeachment for removal). It also explicitly recommended an independent collegium for appointments comprising the PM, LoP, and CJI.
* **Tarkunde Committee (1975) & Goswami Committee (1990):** Both historical committees flagged the danger of leaving ECI appointments entirely to executive discretion, arguing it compromises the perception of neutrality.
* **Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2nd ARC - Ethics in Governance):** Recommended a collegium system for appointing the CEC and ECs to insulate the institution from political patronage.

## 6. Indices [better which shows India's ranking]

* **Democracy Index (Economist Intelligence Unit):** India’s score heavily relies on the "Electoral Process and Pluralism" parameter. The ECI's ability to conduct largely violence-free, universal adult suffrage elections is the primary reason India retains its democratic status despite other systemic downgrades.
* **V-Dem Electoral Democracy Index:** Flags concerns over the "level playing field," pointing out that while the ECI executes elections flawlessly, its ability to curb money power and ruling-party media dominance remains severely constrained.

## 7. Global comparison

* **United States (Federal Election Commission - FEC):** The US FEC is heavily bipartisan by design, consisting of six members appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate, with no more than three allowed from the same political party. It frequently deadlocks, unlike the Indian ECI which operates efficiently on majority rule.
* **United Kingdom (Electoral Commission):** An independent body that answers directly to the UK Parliament, not the government. Commissioners are appointed by the King upon the recommendation of the House of Commons, heavily involving opposition consensus.
* **South Africa (Independent Electoral Commission):** The constitution mandates a deeply transparent, multi-layered appointment process involving a panel headed by the President of the Constitutional Court, ensuring absolute executive distance.

2. **March 2023 (SC Intervention - Anoop Baranwal):** Supreme Court strikes down executive monopoly -> Prescribes temporary Collegium (PM + LoP + CJI) to balance power.

## 10. Recent occurrence - debate

* **Why did the Supreme Court intervene in appointments?** In the *Anoop Baranwal v. Union of India (2023)* case, the SC intervened because Article 324(2) states that appointments shall be made "subject to the provisions of any law made in that behalf by Parliament." For 73 years, Parliament failed to enact such a law, leaving a massive constitutional vacuum. The executive exploited this silence, maintaining absolute, unbridled discretion over appointments. The SC ruled that this executive monopoly threatened the ECI's institutional independence and violated the basic structure of democracy.
* **What did the Anoop Baranwal judgment prescribe?** The Supreme Court prescribed that until Parliament enacts a specific law, a neutral, high-powered collegium must make the appointments to ensure non-partisanship.
* **Who was on the appointment panel prescribed by the Supreme Court?** The SC prescribed a three-member panel consisting of: (1) The Prime Minister, (2) The Leader of the Opposition (or leader of the single largest opposition party in Lok Sabha), and (3) The Chief Justice of India (CJI).
* **The 2023 Act Debate:** In December 2023, Parliament enacted a law replacing the CJI with a Union Cabinet Minister. Critics and constitutional experts heavily debate this, arguing it defeats the entire purpose of the SC judgment by restoring a 2:1 absolute majority to the ruling executive, rendering the Leader of the Opposition's vote mathematically meaningless and compromising institutional neutrality.

## 11. Advantage/Merits/Role

* **Seamless Transfer of Power:** The ECI is globally revered for conducting peaceful transitions of power in a hyper-diverse, heavily populated nation, a feat few post-colonial democracies have achieved.
* **Logistical Marvel:** Successfully manages over 900+ million voters, setting up polling booths so that no voter has to travel more than 2 kilometers, including extreme terrains (like the single-voter booth in Gir Forest).
* **The Model Code of Conduct (MCC):** A unique, consensus-based moral code (though lacking statutory backing) that the ECI enforces to prevent the ruling party from misusing the official state machinery for campaigning.
* **Voter Inclusivity:** Aggressive initiatives like SVEEP (Systematic Voters’ Education and Electoral Participation) have led to record-breaking voter turnouts, successfully bridging the gender gap in voting percentages.

## 12. Disadvantage/De-Merits (Systemic Limitations)

* **Inability to Deregister Parties:** The ECI has the power to register political parties (under Section 29A of the RPA), but it fundamentally lacks the legal power to deregister them for constitutional violations or non-compliance, leaving the system cluttered with shell parties.
* **Toothless MCC Penalties:** Violations of the MCC (like hate speech) often only result in a temporary campaign ban (e.g., 48 hours). The ECI lacks the power to immediately disqualify a candidate for egregious ethical violations.
* **Dependence on the Executive for Staff:** The ECI does not have its own independent, permanent workforce. During elections, it relies entirely on drafting teachers and state bureaucrats, who often retain loyalties to their local political bosses.
* **Financial Dependence:** The ECI's expenditure is not charged on the Consolidated Fund of India (unlike the UPSC or CAG), making it technically dependent on the Union budget voting process.

## 13. Challenges

* **EVM Skepticism:** A continuous, highly politicized challenge where losing parties perpetually allege EVM hacking. The ECI struggles to maintain public trust despite introducing VVPATs (Voter Verifiable Paper Audit Trails).
* **Money Power and Criminalization:** The ECI has completely failed to curb the influx of black money and the rising criminalization of politics (43% of MPs in the 17th Lok Sabha had criminal cases), as statutory expenditure caps apply only to candidates, not political parties.
* **Deepfakes and Digital Subversion:** The rapid rise of AI-generated deepfakes and targeted social media disinformation campaigns threatens the integrity of the ballot. The ECI's technological and legal capacity to penalize algorithmic manipulation is currently severely lagging.
* **Perception of Partisanship:** The new appointment mechanism (2023 Act) and recent delays in taking action against high-profile MCC violations by ruling party leaders have deeply damaged the ECI's historical reputation of fierce, blind neutrality.

## 14. Way forward / solution / positive outlook

* **Restore the Neutral Collegium:** Parliament should amend the 2023 Act to replace the Union Cabinet Minister with a neutral constitutional functionary (like the CJI or a consensus civil society representative) to prevent the executive from steamrolling ECI appointments.
* **Elevate EC Protection:** Article 324 must be amended to provide the two Election Commissioners with the exact same security of tenure (impeachment process) as the Chief Election Commissioner, preventing them from feeling subordinate or vulnerable.
* **Statutory Backing for Deregistration:** The Representation of the People Act (RPA) must be urgently amended to explicitly empower the ECI to deregister political parties that violate constitutional principles or operate as tax-evasion fronts.
* **Regulate Party Expenditure:** The RPA must be amended to impose an absolute financial cap on the total expenditure a *political party* can incur during an election, closing the massive loophole that currently allows infinite corporate spending.

## 15. Best practices

* **India (The cVIGIL App):** An internationally recognized technological best practice deployed by the ECI. It empowers ordinary citizens to instantly report MCC violations (like cash or liquor distribution) using geo-tagged photos, enabling a rapid response from flying squads within 100 minutes.
* **South Africa (Transparent Appointments):** The appointment of electoral commissioners is open to public scrutiny and driven by a completely independent judiciary-led panel, ensuring zero political debt for the appointees.
* **Costa Rica (The Supreme Electoral Tribunal):** A brilliant institutional model where the electoral commission actually assumes command of the national police force during election periods, granting it absolute, unchallengeable authority to ensure fair play.
* **India (SVEEP):** The ECI's Systematic Voters’ Education and Electoral Participation program is a global best practice in targeted civic engagement, effectively utilizing local icons and tailored messaging to boost voter turnout in historically apathetic urban and remote tribal areas.

## 16. Conclusion

* The Election Commission of India is not merely an administrative body; it is the constitutional referee that ensures the democratic legitimacy of the Indian State.
* However, its institutional capital rests entirely on public trust. To combat modern challenges of digital subversion, dark money, and creeping executive dominance, the ECI urgently requires structural fortification—specifically through neutral appointments and parity in the security of tenure for all its commissioners—ensuring it remains a fierce, independent guardian of the people's mandate.
* The ECI is central to free and fair elections. Strengthening its
structural autonomy, financial independence and collegial
