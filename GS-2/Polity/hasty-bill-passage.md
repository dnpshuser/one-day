# Parliament's Hasty Bill Passage Without Much Deliberation

## 1. Intro

* Parliamentary democracy relies on rigorous debate and committee scrutiny to ensure that legislation is constitutionally sound, widely accepted, and free of drafting errors.
* However, recent data from the 17th and 18th Lok Sabhas reveals a concerning trend of "legislative railroading," where Bills are introduced and passed in the same session, often within hours or minutes, bypassing the crucial mechanism of parliamentary standing committees.

## 2. Sub-components of the topic [most imp]

* **Decline in Committee Scrutiny:** Departmentally Related Standing Committees (DRSCs) act as mini-parliaments for in-depth, non-partisan review. Their usage has plummeted—from 71% in the 15th Lok Sabha to just 16% in the 17th Lok Sabha.
* **Same-Session Passage:** A growing trend where Bills are passed in the very session they are introduced, leaving MPs with no time to read the drafts, consult stakeholders, or propose meaningful amendments.
* **Passing in the Din:** The practice of pushing through major legislation (like the Farm Laws or Criminal Codes) while the opposition is protesting in the Well of the House or after mass suspensions, removing the primary check on executive overreach.
* **Guillotine on the Budget:** The Budget process involves Demands for Grants from various ministries. Due to lack of time or disruptions, these demands are grouped together and passed without any debate via a process called the "guillotine," severely compromising financial accountability.
* **Ordinance Route Bypass:** The government often promulgates Ordinances first and then introduces Bills merely to ratify the ordinance *fait accompli*, shutting down any scope for structural changes through debate.
* **Voice Vote Abuse:** Complex Bills are frequently passed via voice vote rather than recorded division (only 9% of bills in the 17th LS saw recorded voting), making it impossible to hold individual MPs accountable for their stance.

## 3. Evolution timeline

* 1993: Creation of DRSCs
The Departmentally Related Standing Committee system was formally established to ensure every major Bill receives detailed, specialized scrutiny away from the political grandstanding of the main chamber.


* 2009-2014: 15th Lok Sabha (High Scrutiny)
71% of all Bills introduced were referred to parliamentary committees for detailed examination before passage.


* 2014-2019: 16th Lok Sabha (Decline Begins)
The referral rate to parliamentary standing committees dropped sharply from 71% to just 25%.


* 2019-2024: 17th Lok Sabha (The Railroading Peak)
Only 16% of Bills were referred to committees. 58% of Bills were passed within two weeks of introduction. Notably, 35% of Bills in the LS were passed with less than *one hour* of discussion.


* Dec 2023: Criminal Codes Passed Amidst Purge
Three foundational laws (replacing the IPC, CrPC, and Evidence Act) were passed while 146 opposition MPs were suspended, stripping the debate of any substantial counter-view.


* August 2026: 18th Lok Sabha (Monsoon Session)
The trend accelerates. According to PRS data, 11 out of 12 Bills introduced in the Monsoon Session were passed before the session ended. Alarmingly, 7 of these 11 Bills were passed in *five minutes or less*, with no MP speaking other than the minister piloting them.

## 5. Committee or Reports

* **PRS Legislative Research (17th & 18th Lok Sabha Analysis):** The definitive data source. The latest 2026 reports show that 53% of the 64 Bills introduced since the 18th Lok Sabha began were passed in the very session of their introduction. In the recent Monsoon session, Lok Sabha functioned for only 15% of its scheduled time, yet cleared 11 bills.
* **National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (NCRWC, 2002):** Explicitly warned against the declining standards of debate. It recommended making committee referrals mandatory for all important legislation to prevent the executive from turning Parliament into a mere rubber stamp.
* **Vice President's Observations (Jagdeep Dhankhar):** Has repeatedly expressed anguish over Parliament being reduced to a "notice board" where passing bills in the din has become the new normal, eroding the moral authority of the legislature.

## 6. Indices

*(Note: Hasty legislation impacts global governance metrics indirectly through the quality of laws produced)*

* **Democracy Index (Economist Intelligence Unit):** India's score on "Functioning of Government" is directly depressed when the executive totally dominates the legislature, rendering opposition scrutiny mathematically irrelevant.
* **Rule of Law Index (World Justice Project):** The "Open Government" and "Regulatory Enforcement" parameters drop when laws are drafted opaquely and passed without stakeholder consultation (which DRSCs usually facilitate).
* **Ease of Doing Business / Investor Confidence:** Hasty laws often contain drafting errors or unintended consequences (e.g., retrospective taxation issues in the past), leading to policy unpredictability which repels long-term foreign investment.

## 7. Global comparison

* **United Kingdom:** Uses the "Green Paper" (consultation document) and "White Paper" (firm proposals) system before a Bill is even introduced. Once introduced, it goes through rigorous readings and mandatory committee stages, making "same-session" railroading practically impossible for substantive laws.
* **United States:** The Senate operates on a deeply deliberative model. The committee system in Congress is exceptionally powerful; committees can completely rewrite, stall, or kill executive bills before they ever reach the floor for a vote.
* **Switzerland:** Employs a pre-parliamentary consultation phase where cantons, political parties, and interest groups are invited to comment on major legislative drafts before they are formalized, ensuring massive democratic consensus before the parliament even votes.

## 9. Flow chart

**The Ideal Legislative Process vs. The Short-Circuit**

1. **Bill Drafted:** Executive finalizes the draft.
2. **Introduction in House:** Minister introduces the Bill.
3. **The Crucial Juncture (Referral):**
* *Ideal Route:* Speaker refers Bill to a Standing Committee (DRSC).
* *Short-Circuit Route:* Government requests immediate consideration; Speaker agrees.


4. **Scrutiny Phase:**
* *Ideal Route:* DRSC consults experts, seeks public feedback, identifies flaws, and reports back.
* *Short-Circuit Route:* Skipped entirely.


5. **Floor Debate:**
* *Ideal Route:* Detailed clause-by-clause debate based on DRSC recommendations.
* *Short-Circuit Route:* Passed via voice vote in the din, often under 5 minutes.


6. **Outcome:**
* *Ideal:* Robust, error-free law.
* *Reality:* Flawed law requiring immediate ordinances/amendments or facing SC challenges.



## 10. Recent occurrence - debate

* **The 2026 Monsoon Session Speed-Run:** The PRS report (August 2026) highlighted that in the recent Monsoon session, 7 out of 11 Bills were passed in five minutes or less. Nine bills were cleared without *any* MP speaking other than the minister. This has sparked a fierce debate about the complete irrelevance of the opposition in the 18th Lok Sabha.
* **The Telecommunications Act, 2023:** Passed in the Winter Session amidst the suspension of 146 MPs. Experts debated that the Act grants sweeping powers to the government for internet suspension and surveillance, provisions that desperately needed rigorous DRSC scrutiny but received none.
* **The Missing Deputy Speaker (Again):** Just like the 17th Lok Sabha, the 18th Lok Sabha has also not elected a Deputy Speaker so far, keeping control strictly within the ruling party and stifling opposition voices in managing the House's time.

## 11. Challenges

* **Executive Hegemony:** A massive absolute majority allows the ruling party to treat Parliament as an extension of the executive, using it merely to rubber-stamp decisions already made in the PMO/Cabinet.
* **Erosion of Judicial Time:** Poorly drafted, un-debated laws inevitably lead to massive litigation. The Supreme Court has to step in to interpret ambiguous clauses or strike down unconstitutional provisions, wasting precious judicial time.
* **Lack of Public Trust:** When laws directly affecting millions (like agriculture or labor codes) are passed without consulting stakeholders via committees, it breeds deep suspicion, leading to street protests and the eventual repeal of the laws.
* **The Speaker's Bias:** The absolute discretion of the Speaker/Chairman to refer (or not refer) a Bill to a committee is frequently exercised in favor of the government's demand for speed over the opposition's demand for scrutiny.

## 12. Way forward / Solution / Positive Outlook

* **Mandatory Committee Referral (NCRWC/Rules Amendment):** The Rules of Procedure must be amended to make it mandatory for every Bill (excluding Money Bills or genuine emergencies) to be referred to a DRSC. Any exemption should require a special 2/3rd majority vote in the House, not just the Speaker's discretion.
* **Minimum Debate Time:** Introduce a rule stipulating a mandatory minimum number of hours for debating specific categories of Bills, preventing the passage of complex laws in under an hour.
* **Pre-Legislative Consultation Policy (PLCP):** Strictly enforce the 2014 PLCP mandate, requiring all draft Bills to be placed in the public domain for at least 30 days for citizen feedback *before* introduction in Parliament.
* **Calendar of Sittings:** Adopt a fixed annual calendar for Parliament with a constitutionally mandated minimum of 100-120 sittings. More time available reduces the government's excuse that they must rush Bills to beat the session clock.

## 13. Best practices

* **United Kingdom (Pre-Legislative Scrutiny):** The UK extensively utilizes ad-hoc Joint Committees to examine draft Bills *before* they are formally introduced into Parliament. This separates technical drafting issues from political debate.
* **Australia (Senate Scrutiny of Bills Committee):** An independent committee that evaluates every single Bill against specific principles (e.g., does it trespass on personal rights? does it delegate legislative power inappropriately?) regardless of the politics behind the Bill.
* **India (The Data Protection Bill Process):** A rare positive example from the 17th Lok Sabha. The Personal Data Protection Bill (2019) was sent to a Joint Parliamentary Committee, which spent over two years consulting stakeholders. Although the original bill was withdrawn, the scrutiny process itself was a textbook example of how complex legislation should be handled.

## 14. Conclusion

* The primary function of Parliament is not to agree with the government, but to hold it accountable. Passing laws in minutes without debate or committee scrutiny reduces the world's largest democracy to a procedural formality.
* To restore the sanctity of the legislature, structural reforms mandating committee referrals and ensuring adequate debate time are imperative. Without this, the laws governing the nation will reflect executive convenience rather than democratic consensus.
