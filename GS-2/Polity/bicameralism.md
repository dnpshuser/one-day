# Bicameralism

## 1. Intro

* Bicameralism is the constitutional system of having a legislature divided into two separate assemblies, chambers, or houses. In India, it forms the bedrock of deliberative democracy and federalism.
* At the Union level, Parliament consists of the Lok Sabha (House of the People) and the Rajya Sabha (Council of States) under Article 79. At the state level, Article 168 allows for a bicameral legislature comprising the Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha) and the Legislative Council (Vidhan Parishad), though currently, only six states have opted for the latter.

## 2. Sub-components of the topic [most imp]

* **The Federal Chamber (Rajya Sabha):** Designed as the "House of Elders" and the guardian of state rights. Members are indirectly elected by state MLAs via a single transferable vote, ensuring regional voices are heard in national law-making.
* **Asymmetrical Representation:** Unlike the US Senate where every state has equal seats, Rajya Sabha seats are allocated based on population (Fourth Schedule). Thus, UP has 31 seats, while Northeastern states have just 1, heavily skewing federal parity.
* **Permanent House:** The Rajya Sabha is a continuous chamber that is never dissolved. One-third of its members retire every second year (Article 83), ensuring policy continuity even when the Lok Sabha is dissolved.
* **Special Powers of Rajya Sabha:** It has exclusive powers to authorize Parliament to make laws on State List subjects (Article 249) and to create new All India Services (Article 312), reinforcing its federal mandate.
* **State Legislative Councils (Vidhan Parishads):** The upper house in states. Their composition includes members elected by local bodies, graduates, teachers, MLAs, and members nominated by the Governor, aiming to bring in intellectuals and experts.
* **Creation and Abolition (Article 169):** Parliament can create or abolish a State Legislative Council *only* if the concerned State Assembly passes a resolution to that effect by a special majority. However, the final power rests with Parliament (via simple majority).
* **Legislative Asymmetry (Money Bills):** A severe limitation of the upper houses. Money Bills can only originate in the lower house. The Rajya Sabha can only delay a Money Bill for 14 days, and State Legislative Councils have similarly castrated financial powers.

## 3. Evolution timeline

* **1918 (Montagu-Chelmsford Report):** First recommended a bicameral legislature at the central level to ensure proper representation and checking of hasty laws in British India.
* **1919 (Government of India Act):** Formally established the bicameral legislature at the Centre, creating the Council of State and the Central Legislative Assembly.
* **1935 (Government of India Act):** Introduced bicameralism in the provinces, laying the historical framework for today’s State Legislative Councils.
* **1950 (Constitutional Adoption):** The Constituent Assembly robustly debated the necessity of a Second Chamber, ultimately adopting it for the Centre but leaving it optional for the States (Article 169) due to cost and utility concerns.
* **2003 (RPA Amendment):** Parliament amended the Representation of the People Act, removing the requirement that a Rajya Sabha candidate must be a domicile/registered voter of the state they represent, fundamentally altering the chamber's federal nature.
* **2006 (Kuldip Nayar v. Union of India):** The Supreme Court upheld the 2003 amendment and the introduction of open voting in Rajya Sabha elections (to curb cross-voting), stating it did not violate the basic structure of federalism.
* **2026 (Current State Setup):** Only six states currently operate a bicameral legislature: Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, Karnataka, Maharashtra, Telangana, and Uttar Pradesh.

## 5. Committee or reports [latest is best or some report in the past with very large impact]

* **National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (NCRWC, 2002):** Debated the asymmetry of the Rajya Sabha. While some members argued for equal representation for all states (like the US), the Commission did not recommend it but strongly defended the continuing necessity of the Upper House to check executive tyranny.
* **Punchhi Commission (2010):** Recommended an amendment to ensure equal representation for states in the Rajya Sabha, irrespective of their population, to protect smaller states and strengthen cooperative federalism.
* **Law Commission of India (255th Report):** Noted the politicization of Article 169, pointing out that State Legislative Councils are often created or abolished based purely on the whims of the ruling party to accommodate defeated politicians, rather than for structural governance.
* **Parliamentary Standing Committee on Law and Justice:** Has periodically highlighted that the original justification for Legislative Councils (to bring in the "intelligentsia") has faded, as rising educational levels mean experts can now directly contest lower house elections.

## 6. Indices [better which shows India's ranking]

* **Democracy Index (Economist Intelligence Unit):** A strong, functioning bicameral system improves the "Checks and Balances" and "Functioning of Government" metrics. When the upper house is bypassed (e.g., via the Money Bill route), India's score is negatively impacted.
* **V-Dem (Varieties of Democracy) Deliberative Component Index:** Measures the depth of legislative debate. A robust upper house that actively reviews and sends bills to committees boosts this index.
* **Rule of Law Index (World Justice Project):** The "Constraints on Government Powers" parameter relies heavily on the upper house checking the executive, especially when the ruling party has an absolute majority in the lower house.

## 7. Global comparison

* **United States (The Strongest Upper House):** The US Senate is the most powerful upper chamber globally. It operates on the principle of absolute federal equality (2 Senators per state). It has co-equal legislative powers and exclusive powers to ratify treaties and confirm presidential appointments.
* **United Kingdom (The Weakest Upper House):** The House of Lords is an unelected body (comprising hereditary peers and appointed life peers). Under the Parliament Acts, it can only delay legislation for up to a year and cannot touch Money Bills, making it subordinate to the House of Commons.
* **Australia (Symmetrical and Powerful):** The Australian Senate ensures equal representation for all states (12 each). Crucially, the Constitution explicitly bans "tacking" (sneaking non-financial provisions into money bills), ensuring the Senate cannot be bypassed procedurally.
* **India (The Hybrid):** The Rajya Sabha is elected but asymmetrical (population-based), making it stronger than the House of Lords but much weaker than the US Senate, particularly concerning financial legislation and the removal of the government.

## 9. Flow chart

**The Process of Creation/Abolition of a State Legislative Council (Article 169)**

1. **Political Motivation:** State government decides to create or abolish a Legislative Council.
2. **State Assembly Action:** A resolution is introduced in the State Legislative Assembly (Vidhan Sabha).
3. **Special Majority Requirement:** The resolution must be passed by:
* A majority of the total membership of the Assembly, AND
* A majority of not less than 2/3rds of the members present and voting.


4. **Transmission:** Passed resolution is sent to the Union Parliament.
5. **Parliamentary Action:** Parliament introduces a Bill to give effect to the resolution.
6. **Simple Majority:** The Bill only requires a Simple Majority in both Houses of Parliament (It is NOT considered a Constitutional Amendment under Article 368).
7. **Presidential Assent:** The Bill becomes an Act, officially creating or abolishing the Council.

## 10. Recent occurrence - debate

* **The Delimitation Crisis (Post-2026):** Southern states are intensely debating bicameral reform. Fearing a massive loss of seats in the Lok Sabha due to upcoming delimitation (because of their successful population control), they are demanding that Rajya Sabha seat allocation be changed to an equal-seat-per-state model (like the US) to protect federal equilibrium.
* **Politicization of Article 169:** The provision has become a political football. West Bengal passed a resolution to create a Legislative Council, but the Union Government has simply sat on it. Andhra Pradesh passed a resolution to abolish its Council when the opposition controlled it, and then withdrew the resolution years later when political fortunes changed.
* **The Money Bill Subterfuge:** The debate rages over the Centre increasingly passing complex laws (Aadhaar, Tribunal Rules, PMLA amendments) as Money Bills. This deliberately cuts the Rajya Sabha out of the legislative process, rendering bicameralism useless.
* **Nominated Members' Apathy:** The poor attendance and zero legislative contribution of high-profile nominated members (celebrities, sportspersons) in the Rajya Sabha has sparked debates on whether the nomination quota is failing its intellectual purpose.

## 11. Advantage/Merits/Role

* **Check on Hasty Legislation:** In a democracy, the lower house can often pass laws based on the "passions of the moment" or populist frenzy. The second chamber provides a cooling-off period for sober, second thought.
* **Voice of the States (Federalism):** The Rajya Sabha structurally forces the Union government to negotiate with regional parties and state governments, acting as a bulwark against centralizing tendencies.
* **Platform for Experts:** Through nominations (12 in RS, 1/6th in State LCs), bicameralism allows apolitical experts, scientists, artists, and economists to contribute to national policy without braving the toxic electoral grind.
* **Sharing Legislative Workload:** With the Lok Sabha perpetually burdened, non-controversial or highly technical bills can be initiated in the upper houses, easing the legislative traffic.
* **Continuity of Governance:** Because the Rajya Sabha and Legislative Councils are never dissolved, they ensure that the legislative branch is always active, even during national or state elections.

## 12. Disadvantage/De-Merits

* **Redundancy and Delay:** Critics argue that if the upper house agrees with the lower house, it is superfluous; if it disagrees, it is mischievous. It often serves merely to delay the government's mandate.
* **Parking Lot for the Defeated:** The most severe criticism is that political parties use upper houses to rehabilitate powerful politicians who were rejected by the public in direct elections, defeating the spirit of democracy.
* **Dilution of Federal Character:** By removing the domicile requirement (2003) for the Rajya Sabha, parties now routinely parachute "outsiders" into states they have no connection with, destroying the chamber's identity as a Council of *States*.
* **Expensive Replicas (State LCs):** At the state level, Legislative Councils are heavily criticized as massive drains on the exchequer. They lack real power (they can only delay ordinary bills by 4 months) and often just duplicate the debates of the Assembly.
* **Asymmetrical Power:** The overwhelming dominance of populous states (like UP) in the Rajya Sabha means that smaller states are easily marginalized, defeating the federal purpose of the upper house.

## 13. Challenges

* **The Money Bill Loophole:** The absolute discretion of the Speaker to certify an ordinary bill as a Money Bill (which the RS cannot touch) poses an existential threat to the relevance of the Upper House.
* **Loss of Deliberative Quality:** With increasing partisanship, the Rajya Sabha often mirrors the chaotic disruptions of the Lok Sabha rather than acting as a dignified, intellectual chamber of review.
* **The "Whims and Fancies" of State LCs:** Article 169 leaves the creation/abolition of State Legislative Councils entirely to political expediency. There is no objective national policy on which states actually *need* a second chamber based on size or population.
* **Executive Dominance:** When a ruling party eventually secures an absolute majority in *both* houses (which usually takes a few election cycles), the second chamber is reduced to a rubber stamp, losing its friction-creating utility.

## 14. Way forward / solution / positive outlook

* **Symmetrical Federalism (Punchhi Commission):** The Constitution should be amended to allocate an equal number of seats to all states in the Rajya Sabha (e.g., 5 seats per state), ensuring that large states do not dominate and true federalism is achieved.
* **National Policy on State LCs:** Instead of leaving Article 169 to state-level political whims, Parliament must enact a uniform national policy—perhaps mandating that only states above a certain population/size threshold can have Legislative Councils.
* **Restoring the Domicile Rule:** To revive the Rajya Sabha's federal essence, the 2003 amendment must be reversed. Only genuine residents who understand the local socio-political issues should represent a state in the Council of States.
* **Judicial Check on Money Bills:** The Supreme Court (pending 7-judge bench) must strictly define the boundaries of Article 110 (Money Bills) to prevent the executive from procedurally bypassing the Rajya Sabha on substantive administrative laws.
* **Strengthening Committee Referrals:** To improve deliberative quality, it should be made mandatory for all bills introduced in either house to be referred to the respective Departmentally Related Standing Committees before floor debates.

## 15. Best practices

* **United States (Federal Equality):** The absolute equality of states in the US Senate (2 per state, regardless of population) is the gold standard for protecting regional parity in a massive, diverse federation.
* **Australia (Anti-Tacking Rule):** Section 54 of the Australian Constitution strictly prohibits the government from sneaking ordinary policy matters into financial bills. If they do, the High Court invalidates it, perfectly protecting their Senate from the "Money Bill bypass."
* **United Kingdom (Salisbury Convention):** By convention, the House of Lords does not oppose the second or third reading of any government legislation promised in its election manifesto. This is a brilliant behavioral practice that balances the upper house's right to review with the lower house's democratic mandate.
* **Ireland (Vocational Representation):** The Irish Senate (Seanad Éireann) includes members specifically elected from vocational panels (Agriculture, Labour, Industry, Culture, etc.), ensuring structured, institutionalized expert representation rather than random political nominations.

## 16. Conclusion

* Bicameralism was not designed to be a procedural speed-breaker, but rather the constitutional crucible where raw majoritarian mandates are tested, cooled, and refined into enduring national laws.
* To ensure the "House of Elders" does not decay into a mere echo chamber of the lower house, urgent structural reforms—particularly enforcing symmetrical state representation and plugging the Money Bill loophole—are essential for the survival of India's cooperative federalism.
