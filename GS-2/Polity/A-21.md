Here are comprehensive UPSC Mains notes on Article 21 (Right to Life and Personal Liberty), structured across your required headings.

## 1. Intro

* Article 21 declares that "No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law." It is available to both citizens and non-citizens.
* Often described as the "heart of the fundamental rights," it is a dynamic, ever-expanding constitutional guarantee that protects individuals against arbitrary state action and ensures existence with human dignity.

## 2. Sub-components of the topic [most imp]

* **Right to live with human dignity:** The Supreme Court (in *Francis Coralie vs. UT of Delhi*) established that life is not mere animal existence but includes the bare necessaries of life such as adequate nutrition, clothing, and shelter.
* **Right to Livelihood:** Recognized in the *Olga Tellis (1985)* case, stating that no person can live without the means of living, making livelihood an integral part of the right to life.
* **Right to Privacy:** Formally recognized as an intrinsic part of Article 21 in the landmark *K.S. Puttaswamy (2017)* judgment, protecting an individual's autonomy over personal decisions and data.
* **Right to Health and Clean Environment:** Expanding jurisprudence has included the right to a pollution-free environment, access to medical care (even for convicts), and hazardous industries regulation under this umbrella (*M.C. Mehta* cases).
* **Rights of the Accused and Prisoners:** Includes the right to free legal aid (*Khatri v. State of Bihar*), right to a speedy trial (*Hussainara Khatoon*), and protection against solitary confinement and custodial violence.
* **Right to Sleep and Privacy from State Surveillance:** Specifically noted in the *Ramlila Maidan* case, recognizing sleep as a fundamental biological necessity.
* **Right to Die with Dignity (Passive Euthanasia):** Recognized in the *Common Cause (2018)* case, allowing the execution of 'Living Wills' for terminally ill patients, drawing a line between passive euthanasia and active suicide.
* **Due Process of Law:** Though the text says "procedure established by law", judicial interpretation post-1978 requires that any law depriving liberty must be "just, fair, and reasonable."

## 3. Evolution timeline

* 1950: A.K. Gopalan vs. State of Madras
The Supreme Court took a narrow view, ruling that "procedure established by law" meant the state could deprive liberty as long as a validly enacted law existed, refusing to read American "due process" into it.


* 1962: Kharak Singh vs. State of UP
While dealing with police surveillance, the Court slightly expanded 'personal liberty', acknowledging it as a compendious term including various rights, though the Right to Privacy was not yet fully recognized.


* 1978: Maneka Gandhi vs. Union of India
The watershed moment. The Court overruled *A.K. Gopalan*, holding that the procedure depriving liberty must be "just, fair, and reasonable." It established the interlinkage of Articles 14, 19, and 21 (The Golden Triangle).


* 1985: Olga Tellis vs. Bombay Municipal Corporation
The "Pavement Dwellers Case" expanded Article 21 to include the Right to Livelihood, as depriving a person of livelihood amounts to depriving them of life.


* 1993: Unni Krishnan vs. State of Andhra Pradesh
The Court elevated the Right to Education up to the age of 14 to the status of a fundamental right flowing from Article 21, later leading to the insertion of Article 21A.


* 2017: Justice K.S. Puttaswamy vs. Union of India
A nine-judge bench unanimously ruled that the Right to Privacy is a fundamental right, intrinsic to life and liberty under Article 21.


* 2018: Navtej Singh Johar & Common Cause cases
Decriminalized homosexuality (affirming bodily autonomy) and legalized passive euthanasia (right to die with dignity), cementing personal choice as a core tenet of Article 21.

* **Chanakya (Arthashastra):** Emphasized state protection of individuals.

* **Justice P.N. Bhagwati (in Maneka Gandhi Case):** "Article 21 is the very heart of the fundamental rights... 'Personal liberty' is of the widest amplitude and it covers a variety of rights which go to constitute the personal liberty of man."
* **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar:** While defending the Draft Constitution, he stressed that the ultimate goal of the state is to maintain conditions where the individual can develop their personality—a philosophy now entirely absorbed by the expanded Article 21.

## 5. Reports

* **Law Commission of India (277th Report):** Titled "Wrongful Prosecution (Miscarriage of Justice): Legal Remedies," it heavily relied on Article 21 to demand compensation for innocents incarcerated due to state apathy, highlighting the sanctity of personal liberty.
* **Law Commission of India (262nd Report):** Recommended the abolition of the death penalty for all crimes (except terrorism-related offenses), arguing that capital punishment inherently conflicts with the evolving standards of dignity under Article 21.
* **National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) Annual Reports:** Consistently document custodial deaths and fake encounters as the gravest violations of Article 21, pushing for stricter enforcement of DK Basu guidelines.
* **NITI Aayog Health Index Reports:** Assess the performance of states in public healthcare delivery. Poor healthcare infrastructure is increasingly viewed as a failure of the state to fulfill the implicit Right to Health under Article 21.

## 7. Global comparison

* **United States:** The 5th and 14th Amendments explicitly use the phrase "Due Process of Law." While India originally rejected this phrase for "procedure established by law," the Supreme Court has practically imported American Due Process jurisprudence via Article 21.
* **United Kingdom:** Does not have a written constitution, but the principles of the *Magna Carta* (1215) and the *Human Rights Act 1998* (incorporating the ECHR) provide robust common law protection against arbitrary deprivation of liberty.
* **Japan:** Article 31 of the Japanese Constitution states, "No person shall be deprived of life or liberty, nor shall any other criminal penalty be imposed, except according to procedure established by law." India borrowed this exact phrasing from Japan.
* **International Frameworks:** Article 3 of the **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR)** and Article 6 of the **International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR)** strongly parallel Article 21, affirming that the inherent right to life shall be protected by law.
* **South Africa:** Section 11 of their Constitution explicitly states, "Everyone has the right to life," and Section 12 guarantees freedom and security of the person, which has been interpreted to completely abolish the death penalty.

## 8. Committee reports

* **Justice J.S. Verma Committee (2013):** Emphasized that bodily autonomy and protection against sexual violence are intrinsic to a woman's Right to Life and Dignity under Article 21, leading to major criminal law amendments.
* **Justice B.N. Srikrishna Committee (2018):** Formed following the *Puttaswamy* judgment, this committee drafted the framework for data protection, translating the fundamental Right to Privacy into actionable statutory safeguards against digital surveillance.
* **Malimath Committee on Criminal Justice (2003):** Recommended balancing the rights of the accused (traditionally heavily protected under Art 21) with the rights of the victims, proposing a more inquisitorial system of justice.
* **Swaminathan Commission (National Commission on Farmers):** While economic in nature, its recommendations on Minimum Support Price (MSP) are often argued by activists as essential to secure the "Right to Livelihood" (Article 21) for the agrarian distress population.

## 10. Flow chart

**The Judicial Test for Deprivation of Liberty (Post-Maneka Gandhi)**

1. **State Action:** The State attempts to deprive an individual of life or personal liberty.
2. **First Test (Legality):** Is there a valid law authorizing this deprivation?
* *If NO:* Action is unconstitutional.
* *If YES:* Proceed to the next test.


3. **Second Test (Legislative Competence):** Was the law passed by a competent legislature (Union/State)?
* *If NO:* Law is void.
* *If YES:* Proceed to the next test.


4. **Third Test (The Golden Triangle - Arts 14, 19, 21):** Is the prescribed procedure "Just, Fair, and Reasonable"?
* Does it violate equality/non-arbitrariness (Art 14)?
* Does it unreasonably restrict fundamental freedoms (Art 19)?


5. **Final Outcome:**
* *If the procedure is arbitrary/oppressive:* The law is struck down as unconstitutional.
* *If the procedure is just/fair:* The deprivation of liberty is legally valid.



## 11. Recent context/issues/debate

* **Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023:** Translating the *Puttaswamy* Right to Privacy into statutory law. Debates focus on the wide exemptions granted to the government (under section 18) for national security, raising concerns about state surveillance overriding Article 21 protections.
* **Climate Change as a Fundamental Right:** In April 2024 (*M.K. Ranjitsinh vs. Union of India*), the Supreme Court expanded Article 21 to explicitly include the "Right to be free from the adverse impacts of Climate Change," linking ecological balance to the right to life.
* **Medical Termination of Pregnancy (MTP) and Bodily Autonomy:** Recent Supreme Court judgments have struggled to balance the reproductive rights/bodily autonomy of women (Article 21) against the life of the fetus, especially in cases of late-stage pregnancies exceeding the 24-week statutory limit.
* **The Right to be Forgotten:** Emerging jurisprudence where individuals seek to have their past criminal records or personal data erased from search engines to live with dignity, pitting Article 21 (Privacy) against Article 19(1)(a) (Public's Right to Information).
* **Preventive Detention (UAPA/PMLA):** Severe debate exists over strict bail conditions under laws like PMLA and UAPA. Critics argue that "bail is the rule, jail is the exception" is being inverted, leading to prolonged incarceration without trial, fundamentally undermining Article 21.

## 12. Conclusion

* Article 21 has undergone a remarkable metamorphosis from a restrictive procedural clause into the most vibrant and expansive fundamental right in the Indian Constitution.
* Through judicial activism, it has become a reservoir of implicit human rights, ensuring that the Constitution is not merely a legal text, but a living document that continually adapts to protect the dignity and liberties of the most vulnerable sections of society.
