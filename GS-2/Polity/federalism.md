## 1. Intro

* Indian federalism is a unique constitutional arrangement characterized as "quasi-federal" by K.C. Wheare, possessing a strong unitary bias (a strong Centre) to ensure national integration.
* It is detailed primarily in Part XI (Centre-State Relations) and the 7th Schedule of the Constitution, functioning on the principle of cooperative federalism, where the Union and States are meant to coordinate rather than compete.

## 2. Sub-components of the topic [most imp]

* **Legislative Relations (Arts 245-255):** Territorial jurisdiction (Parliament for the whole of India, State legislatures for the state) and subject-matter distribution via the 7th Schedule (Union List, State List, Concurrent List).
* **Executive Relations (Arts 256-263):** Executive power is co-extensive with legislative power. The Centre can give directions to states (Art 256, 257) to ensure compliance with Union laws.
* **Financial Relations (Arts 268-293):** Distribution of tax revenues, grants-in-aid (statutory and discretionary), and the role of the Finance Commission (Art 280) in balancing vertical and horizontal fiscal imbalances.
* **The 7th Schedule:** The core of power distribution. Union List (97 subjects originally, e.g., Defense, Foreign Affairs), State List (66 subjects originally, e.g., Police, Public Health), and Concurrent List (47 subjects originally, e.g., Criminal Law, Education). Residuary powers vest with the Centre (Art 248).
* **Unitary Features (The Bias):** Single Constitution, single citizenship, integrated judiciary, All-India Services (IAS, IPS), emergency provisions (Arts 352, 356), and the Governor's appointment by the President.
* **Inter-State Relations:** Mechanisms for resolving disputes (Art 262 for river waters), promoting coordination via the Inter-State Council (Art 263), and Zonal Councils.
* **Asymmetric Federalism:** Special provisions for certain states (Article 371 A-J) acknowledging their unique historical, cultural, or developmental needs (e.g., Nagaland, Mizoram).
* **Local Governance (3rd Tier):** The 73rd and 74th Amendments created a constitutionally mandated third tier of government (Panchayats and Municipalities), deepening democratic decentralization.

## 3. Evolution timeline

* 1935: Government of India Act
Introduced the concept of a federal structure for India, dividing powers into Federal, Provincial, and Concurrent lists, which deeply influenced the current Constitution.


* 1950-1967: The Era of One-Party Dominance
Federalism was largely internal to the Congress party, which ruled at the Centre and in most states, leading to relatively smooth Centre-State relations but limited state autonomy.


* 1967: Electoral Shift
Congress lost power in several states, leading to friction. The period saw the frequent, and often controversial, use of Article 356 (President's Rule) to dismiss state governments.


* 1983: Sarkaria Commission Appointed
The first major commission to comprehensively review Centre-State relations, recommending a stronger Inter-State Council and restraints on the Governor's role.


* 1990s: Rise of Coalition Politics
Regional parties became crucial at the national level, marking a shift towards a more balanced "bargaining federalism." State leaders gained significant leverage over central policies.


* 1994: S.R. Bommai Judgment
The Supreme Court laid down strict guidelines for the use of Article 356, making it subject to judicial review and protecting state autonomy against arbitrary central intervention.


* 2015: NITI Aayog & 14th Finance Commission
The Planning Commission was replaced by NITI Aayog to promote "cooperative federalism." The 14th FC drastically increased the states' share in the central divisible pool of taxes from 32% to 42%.


* 2017: Implementation of GST
A landmark shift in fiscal federalism. States surrendered significant taxation powers to a common GST Council (a federal body where the Centre and states decide rates collaboratively).


## 4. Quotation

* **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar:** "The Constitution is a Federal Constitution inasmuch as it establishes what may be called a Dual Polity... The Draft Constitution can be both unitary as well as federal according to the requirements of time and circumstances."
* **K.C. Wheare:** Described India as "quasi-federal" – a "unitary state with subsidiary federal features rather than a federal state with subsidiary unitary features."
* **Granville Austin:** Described Indian federalism as "Cooperative Federalism," noting that it produces a strong central government but does not necessarily result in weak provincial governments.
* **Chanakya (Arthashastra):** Emphasized the importance of decentralized administration (through *Sthanika* and *Gopa*) while maintaining a strong, unified central command to ensure stability across the empire. (Relevance: The balance of centralized authority and decentralized execution).
* **Rig Veda:** The concept of *Samiti* (assembly) highlights decentralized decision-making processes in ancient Indian polity, reflecting early roots of distributed governance.

## 5. Reports

* **Sarkaria Commission Report (1988):** A foundational document. Recommended the establishment of a permanent Inter-State Council (Art 263), restricting Article 356 as a "rare necessity," and guidelines for the appointment of Governors (eminent persons from outside the state).
* **Punchhi Commission Report (2010):** Recommended a fixed tenure for Governors, the concept of "localized emergency" (under Arts 355/356) to avoid full state takeover, and giving the Inter-State Council more teeth in dispute resolution.
* **Second Administrative Reforms Commission (ARC) - 15th Report (State and District Administration):** Stressed the need for effective devolution of functions, funds, and functionaries to local bodies (3rd tier) to realize true democratic decentralization.
* **Finance Commission Reports (e.g., 14th and 15th):** Crucial for understanding vertical (Centre to States) and horizontal (between States) fiscal devolution. The 15th FC introduced demographic performance and ecology as criteria for fund allocation.

## 6. Indices

*(Federalism is typically evaluated qualitatively, but these indices reflect its operational success)*

* **Democracy Index (Economist Intelligence Unit):** Assesses the functioning of government. A healthy federal structure with empowered regional governments positively impacts this score.
* **Rule of Law Index (World Justice Project):** Measures constraints on government power. An independent judiciary acting as an arbiter between the Centre and States is vital for a high score.
* **Good Governance Index (GGI) - India:** Published by the central government, it ranks Indian states on various parameters. This index promotes "competitive federalism" among states.
* **Ease of Doing Business (EoDB) State Rankings:** Driven by the DPIIT, this is a prime example of competitive federalism, encouraging states to streamline regulations to attract investment.

## 7. Global comparison

* **USA:** The classic "Coming Together" federation. States have independent constitutions, a dual judicial system, and the Senate provides equal representation (2 per state) regardless of size. Residuary powers belong to the States.
* **India:** A "Holding Together" federation. A single constitution, integrated judiciary, and unequal representation in the Rajya Sabha (based on population). Residuary powers belong to the Centre.
* **Canada:** Highly influential on the Indian model. Features a strong central government, with residuary powers vesting in the Centre and the appointment of provincial governors by the federal government.
* **Australia:** Similar to the US, residuary powers lie with the states. However, it uses a Concurrent list (like India) which the US does not explicitly possess.
* **Germany:** Known for "Executive Federalism." The federal government handles policy making, while the states (*Länder*) are primarily responsible for administering and executing federal laws.

## 8. Committee reports

* **Rajamannar Committee (1971):** Appointed by the Tamil Nadu government. It took a radical pro-state stance, recommending the abolition of Article 356, shifting residuary powers to the states, and curtailing the powers of the All-India Services. (Largely ignored by the Centre).
* **Anandpur Sahib Resolution (1973):** Passed by the Akali Dal, it demanded that the Centre's jurisdiction be restricted only to Defense, Foreign Affairs, Communications, and Currency, pushing for extreme state autonomy.
* **National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (NCRWC, 2002):** Re-emphasized the recommendations of the Sarkaria Commission regarding the Governor's office and the judicious use of emergency provisions to protect the federal fabric.

## 10. Flow chart

**Resolving Legislative Conflict (Article 254 - Concurrent List)**

1. **Issue:** Both Parliament and a State Legislature pass a law on the same subject in the Concurrent List.
2. **Conflict Check:** Are the two laws inconsistent or repugnant to each other?
3. **If NO:** Both laws operate in their respective spheres.
4. **If YES:** (The Repugnancy Rule).
5. **General Rule:** The Union Law prevails. The State Law becomes void to the extent of the repugnancy.
6. **The Exception (Presidential Assent):** Did the State Law receive the assent of the President before the Union Law was enacted?
7. **If YES:** The State Law prevails *in that specific state*.
8. **The Caveat:** Parliament can still pass a new law later to override that specific State Law.

## 11. Recent context/issues/debate

* **Financial Centralization Concerns:** States frequently raise concerns over the Centre’s increasing reliance on Cesses and Surcharges, which are kept out of the divisible pool of taxes, thus shrinking the states' share of revenue.
* **The Role of the Governor:** Persistent friction in states ruled by opposition parties (e.g., Kerala, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal) regarding the Governor withholding assent to bills passed by the assembly, acting as a "stumbling block" to state legislation.
* **Use of Central Agencies:** Frequent allegations by state governments that the Centre is weaponizing agencies like the Enforcement Directorate (ED) and Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI) to destabilize elected state governments, violating the spirit of federalism.
* **Asymmetric Federalism in Transition:** The 2019 abrogation of Article 370 (removing Jammu & Kashmir's special status) and its reorganization into Union Territories sparked intense debate on the nature of Indian federalism and the Centre's power to alter statehood without state assembly concurrence (as the state was under President's Rule).
* **Delimitation Post-2026:** The impending lifting of the freeze on the delimitation of Lok Sabha constituencies. Southern states, having successfully controlled their populations, fear a massive loss of political representation and federal power to more populous Northern states.

## 12. Conclusion

* Indian federalism is not a static legal structure but a dynamic, evolving process that continually negotiates the tension between the need for a strong, unified nation and the reality of vast regional diversities.
* While the Constitution leans inherently toward the Centre, the maturity of India's democracy relies on embracing both *cooperative* federalism (working together on shared goals like GST) and *competitive* federalism (states vying for investment), ensuring that regional aspirations are met without fracturing national integrity.
