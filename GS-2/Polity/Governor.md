# Governor

## Intro

* The Governor is the constitutional head of the State executive (Part VI, Articles 153-167) serving as a vital link btw C&S Gov.


## Sub-components of the topic [most imp]

* **Appointment and Tenure (Articles 155 & 156):** Appointed by President;  Holds office *during the pleasure of the President*
* **Executive Powers:** All executive actions of the state are taken in their name. They appoint the CM, other ministers, and the Advocate General (Article 164 & 165).
* **Legislative Powers (Article 200 & 201):** The power to grant assent, withhold assent, return a bill (if not a Money Bill), or reserve it for the consideration of the President; Reserving a bill that endangers the position of the State High Court is mandatory.
* **Ordinance Making Power (Article 213):** promulgates ordinances when the SL is not in session; acting on the advice of the Council of Ministers.
* **Pardoning Power (Article 161):** Can grant pardons, reprieves, or remissions for offenses against state laws.; Unlike the President, they cannot pardon a death sentence or court-martial.
* **Discretionary Powers (Article 163):** The Constitution explicitly allows Governor's discretion - reserving bills, seeking information from the CM under Art 167, or recommending President's Rule under Art 356. The validity of their discretion cannot be questioned in court.
* **Special Responsibilities (Article 371 Series):** Specific Governors have special responsibilities for regional development (e.g., Vidarbha in Maharashtra, tribal areas in Assam), where they act independently of the state cabinet.



## 3. Evolution timeline

* 1935: Government of India Act: created the office of the Provincial Governor with massive discretionary powers to act as an agent of the imperial center


* 1949: Constituent Assembly Debates: Elected vs Appointed Gov(avoids parallel centres' of power)

* 1979: Hargovind Pant vs. Dr. Raghukul Tilak: Governor is an independent constitutional office, not an "employee" or subordinate of the Central Government, despite being appointed by them.

* 1994: S.R. Bommai Judgment: The SC curtailed the misuse of the Governor's report to dismiss state governments (Article 356) - "ruling that the majority of a government must be tested on the floor of the House, not in the Raj Bhavan"

* 2010: B.P. Singhal vs. Union of India
The Supreme Court ruled that while the President can remove a Governor at any time ("pleasure doctrine"), the removal cannot be arbitrary, capricious, or purely for political reasons (e.g., a change of government at the Centre).

* 2023: State of Punjab vs. Principal Secretary to Governor
The Supreme Court explicitly ruled that a Governor cannot "veto" a state legislature's bill by simply sitting on it indefinitely; if they withhold assent, they must return it to the assembly "as soon as possible."

## 1. Intro
* The Governor is the constitutional head of the State executive (Part VI, Articles 153-167) and serves a dual role: as the nominal head of the state government (bound by the advice of the Council of Ministers) and as a vital link/agent of the Central Government.

* Unlike the President, the Governor has explicitly defined constitutional discretions, making the office a unique and frequently contentious linchpin of India's asymmetric federalism.

## 2. Sub-components of the topic [most imp]

* **Appointment and Tenure (Articles 155 & 156):** Appointed directly by the President (effectively the Union Cabinet). Holds office *during the pleasure of the President*, meaning they have no secure tenure and can be removed at any time without cited reasons.
* **Executive Powers:** All executive actions of the state are taken in their name. They appoint the Chief Minister, other ministers, and the Advocate General (Article 164 & 165).
* **Legislative Powers (Article 200 & 201):** The power to grant assent, withhold assent, return a bill (if not a Money Bill), or reserve it for the consideration of the President. Reserving a bill that endangers the position of the State High Court is mandatory.
* **Ordinance Making Power (Article 213):** Can promulgate ordinances when the state legislature is not in session, acting on the advice of the Council of Ministers.
* **Pardoning Power (Article 161):** Can grant pardons, reprieves, or remissions for offenses against state laws. (Unlike the President, they cannot pardon a death sentence or court-martial).
* **Discretionary Powers (Article 163):** The Constitution explicitly allows the Governor to act in their discretion in certain matters (e.g., reserving bills, seeking information from the CM under Art 167, or recommending President's Rule under Art 356). The validity of their discretion cannot be questioned in court.
* **Special Responsibilities (Article 371 Series):** Specific Governors have special responsibilities for regional development (e.g., Vidarbha in Maharashtra, tribal areas in Assam), where they act independently of the state cabinet.

## 3. Evolution timeline

* 1935: Government of India Act
The British created the office of the Provincial Governor with massive discretionary powers to act as an agent of the imperial center, establishing the DNA of the modern office.

* 1949: Constituent Assembly Debates
The framers debated an elected Governor but opted for an appointed one to avoid parallel centers of power in the state (between the CM and the Governor) and to maintain national unity.

* 1979: Hargovind Pant vs. Dr. Raghukul Tilak
The Supreme Court ruled that the Governor is an independent constitutional office, not an "employee" or subordinate of the Central Government, despite being appointed by them.

* 1994: S.R. Bommai Judgment
The Supreme Court drastically curtailed the misuse of the Governor's report to dismiss state governments (Article 356), ruling that the majority of a government must be tested on the floor of the House, not in the Raj Bhavan.

* 2010: B.P. Singhal vs. Union of India
The Supreme Court ruled that while the President can remove a Governor at any time ("pleasure doctrine"), the removal cannot be arbitrary, capricious, or purely for political reasons (e.g., a change of government at the Centre).

* 2023: State of Punjab vs. Principal Secretary to Governor
The Supreme Court explicitly ruled that a Governor cannot "veto" a state legislature's bill by simply sitting on it indefinitely; if they withhold assent, they must return it to the assembly "as soon as possible."


## 4. Quotation

* **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar:** "The Governor under the Constitution has no functions which he can discharge by himself: no functions at all. While he has no functions, he has certain duties to perform." (Highlighting the nominal nature of the role in normal circumstances).
* **K.M. Munshi:** Defended the appointed Governor as the "watchdog of constitutional propriety and the link that binds the State to the Centre."
* **Rig Veda (Concept of the Sentinel):** "जागृविः" (Jaagruvi - The one who is awake/vigilant).
* *Sanskrit Root:* **'जागृ' (Jaagri - जागना/सतर्क रहना)** धातु से बना है।
* *Hindi Explanation:* संविधान निर्माताओं ने राज्यपाल की भूमिका एक 'जागृवि' (Sentinel) के रूप में सोची थी, जो राज्य में संवैधानिक मशीनरी के टूटने पर सतर्क रहे, न कि एक सक्रिय राजनेता के रूप में जो राज्य सरकार के हर काम में हस्तक्षेप करे।


* **Chanakya (Arthashastra - On Envoys):** "दूत" (Doot - Envoy/Messenger).
* *Sanskrit Root:* **'दु' (Du - जाना/संपर्क करना)**।
* *Hindi Explanation:* केंद्र और राज्य के बीच संचार के लिए राज्यपाल एक 'दूत' की तरह है, जिसका कार्य निष्पक्ष रिपोर्टिंग करना है, न कि स्वयं राजा (मुख्यमंत्री) के समानांतर सत्ता चलाना।



## 5. Committee or Reports

* **Sarkaria Commission (1988):** The gold standard report on this topic. It recommended that the Governor should be an eminent person from outside the state, a detached figure, not intimately connected with the local politics of the state, and not a recently active politician.
* **Punchhi Commission (2010):** Recommended a fixed tenure of 5 years for the Governor and suggested that the "Doctrine of Pleasure" should be deleted, making their removal possible only through an impeachment process similar to a High Court judge to ensure independence.
* **Venkatachaliah Commission (NCRWC, 2002):** Strongly recommended that the Governor should be appointed by a committee comprising the Prime Minister, Home Minister, Speaker of the Lok Sabha, and the Chief Minister of the concerned State (to prevent unilateral central appointments).
* **First Administrative Reforms Commission (ARC, 1968):** Suggested that the Governor's report under Article 356 should be objective and that they must use their discretionary powers to reserve bills only in exceptional cases, not routinely.

## 6. Indices

*(Note: As a constitutional office, the Governor is not directly ranked, but their actions impact governance indices)*

* **Rule of Law Index (World Justice Project):** When Governors act as partisan agents (e.g., arbitrarily dissolving assemblies), it negatively impacts the "Constraints on Government Powers" and "Due Process" metrics for the country.
* **Good Governance Index (GGI - India):** States where the Governor and CM are in constant friction (e.g., Kerala, West Bengal, Tamil Nadu) often see delays in the appointment of Vice-Chancellors and clearance of critical bills, stalling the state's governance metrics.
* **Democracy Index (EIU):** The misuse of the Governor's office to unseat democratically elected opposition governments (historical misuse of Art 356) directly degrades the "Electoral Process and Pluralism" score.

## 7. Global comparison

* **United States:** State Governors are directly elected by the people of the state. They are the actual Chief Executives of their states, wielding real power, and are entirely independent of the Federal President. India explicitly rejected this to avoid a clash between an elected CM and an elected Governor.
* **Canada:** The Lieutenant Governor of a province is appointed by the Governor-General (effectively the federal cabinet), very similar to the Indian model. They also act as the nominal head and a representative of the federal center.
* **Australia:** State Governors are appointed by the Monarch on the advice of the State Premier (Chief Minister). The federal government has no role in their appointment, making them strictly state-level constitutional heads, unlike India's dual-role model.

## 9. Flow chart

**The Process of Government Formation (Use of Discretion)**

1. **Election Results Declared:** The Governor receives the final tally from the ECI.
2. **Clear Majority?**
* *If YES:* Governor invites the leader of the majority party to become CM (No discretion).
* *If NO (Hung Assembly):* Governor exercises "Situational Discretion."


3. **Order of Preference (Sarkaria Guidelines):**
* A) Pre-poll alliance commanding a majority.
* B) The single largest party staking a claim to form the govt with outside support.
* C) A post-poll coalition.


4. **Invitation & Swearing In:** Governor appoints the CM.
5. **Floor Test:** The Governor explicitly mandates the new CM to prove their majority on the floor of the House within a specific timeframe (e.g., 15 days).

## 10. Recent occurrence - debate

* **Withholding Assent Indefinitely ("Pocket Veto"):** A massive constitutional crisis erupted in states like Kerala, Tamil Nadu, and Punjab, where Governors simply sat on bills passed by the assembly for years without returning or rejecting them. The Supreme Court recently clarified that Governors cannot stall bills indefinitely; if they don't assent, they must return them "as soon as possible."
* **The Chancellor Controversy:** Several states (like West Bengal and Kerala) have passed bills attempting to remove the Governor as the ex-officio Chancellor of state universities due to continuous friction over the appointment of Vice-Chancellors. Governors naturally reserved these bills for the President, stalling the reform.
* **Floor Tests and Factional Splits:** In Maharashtra (Shiv Sena split), the Supreme Court heavily criticized the Governor for calling a floor test without objective material, noting that the Governor's office cannot be used to settle intra-party rivalries or precipitate the fall of a government.

## 11. Challenges

* **Partisan Appointments:** The unwritten convention of appointing retired politicians or bureaucrats loyal to the ruling party at the Centre completely destroys the neutrality required of the office.
* **The "Pleasure Doctrine" Sword:** Because Governors can be sacked instantly by the Centre (as seen whenever the central government changes), they are structurally incentivized to act as pliant agents of the Union Ministry of Home Affairs rather than independent constitutional heads.
* **Ambiguity in Discretion:** The phrase "in his discretion" in Article 163 is broad. While courts have narrowed it, the lack of codified guidelines for choosing a CM in a hung assembly or deciding the timeline for assenting to bills leaves massive room for political maneuvering.
* **Friction with the Elected Executive:** Constant public spats between the Governor and the CM (e.g., addressing the media against the state government, refusing to read the state-prepared speech at the budget session) paralyzes state administration and degrades the dignity of the office.

## 12. Way forward / Solution / Positive Outlook

* **Collegium for Appointment (Venkatachaliah Commission):** To remove the stigma of being "Centre's agents," Governors must be appointed by a bipartisan committee (PM, Home Minister, LoP, and the respective State CM) rather than unilaterally by the PMO.
* **Security of Tenure (Punchhi Commission):** Delete the "pleasure doctrine." Governors should be given a fixed 5-year tenure and removed only through a complex impeachment process in the Rajya Sabha, insulating them from central coercion.
* **Codifying Conventions (Sarkaria Commission):** The guidelines for government formation in a hung assembly must be codified into the Constitution or the RPA, removing the Governor's subjective (and often partisan) discretion entirely.
* **Timeline for Bills:** The Constitution should be amended to replace the phrase "as soon as possible" in Article 200 with a strict timeline (e.g., 30 or 60 days) within which the Governor must assent, return, or reserve a bill.
* **Removing Chancellor Duties:** Following the Punchhi Commission's advice, Governors should be stripped of statutory roles like University Chancellors. Their focus should remain strictly on constitutional duties to avoid daily administrative friction with the state education department.

## 13. Best practices

* **The "Floor Test First" Principle (Bommai Precedent):** The Indian Supreme Court has established the global best practice that the majority of a government is decided *only* on the floor of the House, entirely removing the Governor's subjective assessment of a CM's support from the Raj Bhavan.
* **UK's Monarchial Neutrality:** While the UK Monarch is the head of state, the absolute strictness of the convention that the Monarch *never* acts against the advice of the Cabinet (never withholding royal assent since 1707) is a behavioral best practice Indian Governors must emulate.
* **Germany's Constructive Vote of No Confidence:** To prevent Governors from constantly demanding floor tests during political instability, India could adopt the German model: the assembly can only remove a CM if they simultaneously vote in a new one, reducing the Governor's role in government collapses.

## 14. Conclusion

* The office of the Governor was envisioned by the framers as a sagacious constitutional sentinel, meant to act as a shock absorber between the Centre and the States, ensuring national unity without crushing regional autonomy.
* However, historical misuse has often reduced the role to a partisan tool. Reforming the appointment process and securing their tenure is absolutely vital to restore the dignity of the Raj Bhavan and protect the fragile fabric of India's cooperative federalism.

# Governor's inaction and pending bills


## 1. Intro

* Under Article 200 of the Constitution, a Bill passed by a State Legislature must be presented to the Governor, who can assent, withhold assent, return it (if not a Money Bill), or reserve it for the President.
* The escalating constitutional crisis of Governors sitting on bills indefinitely (effectively using an unwritten "pocket veto") has created a severe choke point in Centre-State relations, paralyzing the legislative mandate of democratically elected state governments.


## 2. Sub-components of the topic [most imp]

* **Article 200 Options:** The four explicit paths: Assent, Withhold, Return for reconsideration, or Reserve for the President (Article 201).
* **The "As Soon As Possible" Clause:** The first proviso to Article 200 states that the Governor "may, as soon as possible... return the Bill" if it is not a Money Bill. This phrase is the crux of the legal battle over indefinite delays.
* **The Mandatory Assent (Repassage):** If the Governor returns a bill and the State Legislature passes it again (with or without amendments), the Governor *shall not* withhold assent.
* **The Pocket Veto Loophole:** Because the Constitution does not prescribe a specific mathematical time limit (like 30 or 60 days) for the Governor to act, Governors have weaponized the silence to simply sit on bills for years.
* **Article 201 (Presidential Consideration):** When a Governor reserves a bill, their role ends. The President can assent, withhold, or return the bill. Unlike the Governor, if the state repasses the bill, the President is *still* not bound to give assent.
* **Conflict of Democratic Mandate:** The core friction is an unelected, centrally nominated functionary stalling the legislative agenda of an elected government that enjoys the mandate of the people.
* **The Invalid "Withhold then Reserve" Route:** Recent litigation highlighted attempts by Governors to withhold assent first, and when the state re-passed the bill, attempting to refer it to the President to escape the mandatory assent rule.

## 3. Evolution timeline

* 1950: Constitution Adopted
The Constituent Assembly deliberately left out a specific time limit for the Governor in Article 200, assuming constitutional heads would act with propriety and not obstruct state legislatures.


* 1988: Sarkaria Commission
Recognized the growing friction and recommended that the Governor should not act contrary to the advice of the Council of Ministers, and should dispose of bills within a reasonable timeframe.


* 2010: Punchhi Commission
Explicitly acknowledged the "pocket veto" misuse and recommended a strict, legally binding 6-month time limit for Governors to decide on bills.


* 2022-2023: The Federal Flashpoints
State governments in Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Telangana, and Punjab approached the Supreme Court against their respective Governors for sitting on crucial bills (some pending for over 3 years) ranging from public health to university administration.


* Nov 2023: SC Judgment (Punjab Case)
The Supreme Court ruled definitively: A Governor cannot "sit" on bills indefinitely. If they decide to withhold assent, they *must* return the bill "as soon as possible." They cannot simply veto a bill by inaction.


* Dec 2023: The Tamil Nadu Clarification
The SC further clarified that once a Governor withholds assent and the Assembly re-passes the bill, the Governor cannot *then* reserve it for the President. The mandatory assent rule of Article 200's proviso applies.


## 4. Quotation

* **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar:** "The Governor under the Constitution has no functions which he can discharge by himself... he is required to follow the advice of his Ministry in all matters." (Highlighting that withholding assent on a whim violates the core constitutional design).
* **Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud (Punjab Case, 2023):** "Governors cannot be at liberty to keep Bills pending indefinitely... they are not elected representatives of the people."
* **Kala (Time) Principle in Governance:**
* *Sanskrit Root:* **'कल्' (Kal - गणना करना / गिनना)** धातु से बना है, जिसका अर्थ है समय की गणना।
* *Hindi Explanation:* चाणक्य नीति कहती है "कालमतिक्रामन्तं काल एव फलम् पिबति" (जो कार्य समय पर नहीं किया जाता, समय ही उसके फल को नष्ट कर देता है)। यदि राज्यपाल विधेयकों पर समय पर निर्णय नहीं लेते, तो विधायिका का मूल उद्देश्य ही नष्ट हो जाता है।



## 5. Committee or Reports

* **Punchhi Commission (2010):** The most critical report on this issue. It explicitly recommended amending the Constitution to impose a strict time limit of six months for the Governor to act on a Bill under Article 200.
* **Sarkaria Commission (1988):** Recommended that if a Governor reserves a bill for the President, the reasons must be communicated to the State Government. It also stated that the Governor should not withhold assent to a bill passed by a majority merely because they disagree with the policy.
* **National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (NCRWC, 2002):** Recommended that a time limit of four months should be prescribed for the Governor to decide on a bill, and similarly, a time limit of six months should be set for the President to decide on reserved state bills.

## 6. Indices

*(Note: As this is a procedural constitutional issue, it indirectly affects broader governance metrics)*

* **Rule of Law Index (World Justice Project):** When unelected officials bypass constitutional conventions and paralyze the legislative process, the "Constraints on Government Powers" and "Due Process" metrics for the state take a severe hit.
* **Good Governance Index (GGI - India):** States embroiled in these constitutional deadlocks (e.g., Kerala, Tamil Nadu) see administrative delays in critical sectors (like university appointments and public health reforms), dragging down their state GGI scores.
* **Democracy Index (EIU):** The subversion of a democratically elected state legislature's mandate by an appointed central agent degrades the "Functioning of Government" parameter.

## 7. Global comparison

* **United Kingdom:** The Royal Assent by the Monarch is a pure formality. By constitutional convention, no Monarch has withheld assent to a bill passed by Parliament since 1707 (Queen Anne). This absolute convention of timely assent is what Indian framers envisioned.
* **United States:** The US President has a strict, constitutionally mandated 10-day window (excluding Sundays) to sign or veto a bill. If they do nothing and Congress is in session, the bill automatically becomes law. This removes the possibility of an indefinite "pocket veto" while in session.
* **Canada:** The Lieutenant Governor of a province grants Royal Assent. While they *can* reserve bills for the Governor General (federal level), by strict convention, they act solely on the advice of the provincial premier and do not sit on bills arbitrarily.

## 9. Flow chart

**The Supreme Court's Clarification on Article 200 (Punjab/TN Cases)**

1. **Bill Passed:** State Legislature passes a Bill and sends it to the Raj Bhavan.
2. **Governor's Decision Node:**
* *Path A:* Assent (Bill becomes Law).
* *Path B:* Reserve for President (Sent to Union Govt).
* *Path C:* Withhold Assent.


3. **The Mandatory Consequence of Withholding (The SC Ruling):**
* Governor *cannot* just hold the withheld bill.
* Governor *must* return the bill to the Assembly "as soon as possible" with a message for reconsideration.


4. **Legislature's Action:** Assembly re-passes the bill (with or without the Governor's suggested amendments).
5. **Final Governor Action:**
* Assent is now **Mandatory**.
* (Governor cannot use Article 201 to reserve this re-passed bill for the President).



## 10. Recent occurrence - debate

* **The Punjab Case (2023):** The Governor of Punjab refused to assent to bills, claiming the assembly session itself was "illegal." The Supreme Court struck this down, stating that a Governor cannot cast doubt on the validity of the assembly session to avoid their Article 200 duties.
* **The Kerala & Tamil Nadu Standoffs (2023-24):** Governors in these states sat on crucial university reform bills for over 2-3 years. When the state governments approached the SC, the Governors suddenly referred the bills to the President. The states argued this was a colorable exercise of power—referring to the President only to escape the SC's impending deadline.
* **The "Elected vs. Nominated" Debate:** These occurrences have sparked a fierce national debate on federalism. State governments argue that a centrally nominated figure should not have the power to paralyze a government that has won an election on a specific legislative manifesto.

## 11. Challenges

* **Weaponization of Ambiguity:** The phrase "as soon as possible" in Article 200 is inherently vague. Governors exploit this constitutional silence to enforce a de facto "pocket veto," effectively killing state legislation without formally rejecting it.
* **Federal Friction & Policy Paralysis:** Crucial state-level reforms in higher education, public health, and local governance are stalled for years, leading to a complete breakdown in the working relationship between the CM and the Raj Bhavan.
* **Nullifying the Democratic Mandate:** By withholding assent, the Governor overrides the collective will of the people expressed through the state legislature, acting as a super-legislature, which destroys parliamentary democracy.
* **Lack of Justification:** When Governors reserve bills for the President, they rarely provide the state government with the reasons or the specific constitutional grounds for doing so, leaving the state in the dark.
* **Bypassing the Supreme Court:** In recent cases, Governors have used Article 201 (reserving for the President) as an "escape route" at the last minute when dragged to court, shifting the deadlock to the President's office, which also has no time limit.

## 12. Way forward / Solution / Positive Outlook

* **Constitutional Amendment for Time Limits (Punchhi/NCRWC):** The Constitution must be amended to remove "as soon as possible" and replace it with a hard numerical deadline—specifically, 6 months for the Governor (Punchhi Comm.) and 6 months for the President (NCRWC) to decide on state bills.
* **Adopting the US "Automatic Assent" Rule:** If the Governor fails to take any of the four explicit actions under Article 200 within a prescribed time (e.g., 30 days), the bill should automatically be deemed to have received assent and become law.
* **Codifying Reasons for Reservation:** Guidelines must be codified forcing the Governor to state in writing the exact constitutional violation that justifies reserving a bill for the President, preventing routine policy bills from being derailed.
* **Judicial Enforcement of Conventions:** Until a constitutional amendment is passed, the Supreme Court must continue to interpret "as soon as possible" strictly, using its Article 142 powers to mandate that unreasonable delay is a violation of the Constitution.

## 13. Best practices

* **United States (The 10-Day Rule):** The US Constitution's strict 10-day rule for the President to act on a bill, failing which it becomes law automatically (while Congress is in session), is the ultimate best practice to completely eliminate the threat of an indefinite pocket veto.
* **India (The Supreme Court as Constitutional Referee):** The recent proactive stance of the Indian Supreme Court—acting as the referee and forcing Governors to process bills rather than sitting on them—is a vital domestic best practice in utilizing judicial review to correct federal imbalances.
* **United Kingdom (Strict Convention):** The absolute adherence to the unwritten convention that the Head of State never denies or delays assent to a democratically passed bill. The cultivation of such deep-rooted constitutional morality by appointed officials is the only long-term solution.

## 14. Conclusion

* The Governor's role under Article 200 was designed as a constitutional safety valve to prevent hasty or unconstitutional state legislation, not as a tool for partisan obstruction or legislative paralysis.
* Resolving the crisis of pending bills requires moving beyond vague constitutional courtesy. Imposing strict, legally binding timelines is imperative to protect the sovereignty of state legislatures and preserve the sanctity of India's cooperative federalism.

# Misuse of Governor's Ordinance Power

## 1. Intro

* Article 213 of the Constitution empowers the Governor to promulgate Ordinances during the recess of the State Legislature, provided they are satisfied that "circumstances exist which render it necessary" to take immediate action.
* However, this exceptional legislative power of the executive has been routinely abused through the practice of "re-promulgation" (issuing the same ordinance repeatedly to bypass the legislature), which the Supreme Court has heavily condemned as a "fraud on the Constitution."

## 2. Sub-components of the topic [most imp]

* **The Power (Article 213):** A co-extensive legislative power. An ordinance has the exact same force and effect as an Act of the State Legislature, but it is strictly conditional and temporary.
* **The Conditions:** It can only be promulgated when the Legislative Assembly (or both Houses, if bicameral) is not in session, and the Governor must act on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers (Article 163).
* **The Lifespan Limit:** The ordinance must be laid before the State Legislature when it reassembles. It ceases to operate at the expiration of *six weeks* from the reassembly of the legislature, or earlier if a resolution disapproving it is passed. (Maximum possible life: 6 months + 6 weeks).
* **Prior Sanction Exception:** The Governor cannot promulgate an ordinance if a bill containing similar provisions would have required the prior sanction or assent of the President (e.g., bills affecting High Court powers or inter-state trade).
* **The Abuse - "Re-promulgation":** The practice where the state government, to avoid legislative scrutiny or lacking a majority in the upper house, allows an ordinance to lapse and immediately re-issues a fresh ordinance with identical provisions during the next recess, creating a parallel system of permanent law-making.
* **The "Enduring Rights" Doctrine:** A major legal dispute regarding whether rights or liabilities created during the life of an ordinance (like hiring teachers) survive after the ordinance is allowed to lapse or is withdrawn.

## 3. Evolution timeline

* 1950: Constitution Framed
Ordinance-making was included purely as an emergency provision, meant for unforeseen, urgent situations when the legislature could not be immediately convened.


* 1967-1981 (The Bihar Phenomenon): The 'Ordinance Raj' Era
In Bihar, the state government passed 256 ordinances, many of which were kept alive for up to 14 years through repeated re-promulgation, completely bypassing the elected legislative assembly.


* 1986: D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar
The Supreme Court famously struck down the practice of repeated re-promulgation, explicitly calling it a "subversion of the democratic process" and a "fraud on the Constitution."


* 1989-1992: The Bihar Sanskrit Schools Case
Despite the D.C. Wadhwa ruling, the Bihar government continued the practice, re-promulgating an ordinance regarding the takeover of Sanskrit schools multiple times, leading to massive litigation.


* 1998: Supreme Court Split Verdict
A two-judge bench of the SC delivered a split verdict on whether the initial promulgation of the Sanskrit Schools ordinance was valid, necessitating a larger constitution bench.


* 2017: Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar
A landmark 7-judge Constitution Bench definitively ruled that the Governor's satisfaction under Article 213 is subject to judicial review, and that failing to lay an ordinance before the legislature is a serious constitutional infraction.

* **Dr. B.R. ## 5. Committee or Reports

* **Law Commission of India (Various Observations):** Has historically cautioned against the excessive use of executive legislation, emphasizing that laws must emerge through parliamentary debate to ensure democratic legitimacy and public consensus.
* **National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (NCRWC, 2002):** Re-emphasized the *D.C. Wadhwa* principle, stating that the ordinance-making power must be strictly limited to unforeseen urgencies and must not be used as a political convenience to bypass a hostile legislative council.
* **PRS Legislative Research Reports:** Frequently track the surge in ordinance usage across various states and at the Centre, objectively highlighting how complex, permanent policy shifts (like land acquisition or farm laws) are often introduced via ordinances, circumventing committee scrutiny.

## 6. Indices

*(Note: As this is a procedural constitutional issue, it indirectly affects broader governance metrics)*

* **Rule of Law Index (World Justice Project):** The "Constraints on Government Powers" and "Open Government" metrics drop significantly when the executive (Governor/CM) bypasses the legislature, removing the primary check on executive authority.
* **Democracy Index (Economist Intelligence Unit):** The "Functioning of Government" and "Political Culture" parameters are degraded when democratic institutions (like state assemblies) are systematically sidelined by "Ordinance Raj."
* **Good Governance Index (GGI - India):** States that rely heavily on ordinances often reflect poor legislative productivity and deeper political instability, negatively impacting their overall governance perception.

## 7. Global comparison

* **United States:** The US President has *no* ordinance-making power. They can issue "Executive Orders," but these are strictly limited to directing the operations of the executive branch and cannot create new laws or alter existing statutes. Law-making is the absolute monopoly of Congress.
* **United Kingdom:** In the UK, the executive cannot issue laws without Parliament. However, under specific statutory delegation (like the Civil Contingencies Act 2004), ministers can make emergency regulations, but these require immediate parliamentary scrutiny and approval, deeply respecting parliamentary sovereignty.
* **India's Unique Position:** The Indian Constitution is unique in explicitly granting the executive (President/Governor) a co-extensive legislative power. This was borrowed from the colonial Government of India Act, 1935, making its abuse a direct relic of imperial governance structures.

## 9. Flow chart

**The Supreme Court's Framework on Article 213 (*Krishna Kumar Singh*, 2017)**

1. **Promulgation Phase:** Governor issues an Ordinance (Executive acts on 'satisfaction' of necessity).
2. **Judicial Review Check:** Is the Governor's 'satisfaction' based on oblique motives or completely devoid of reason?
* *If Yes:* Struck down by Courts immediately.
* *If No:* Valid temporary law.


3. **Mandatory Tabling:** Legislature reassembles.
* *Action A:* Ordinance is laid before the House, debated, and passed -> Becomes a permanent Act.
* *Action B:* Government deliberately fails to place the ordinance before the House -> **Declared a Serious Constitutional Infraction.**


4. **The Re-promulgation Trap:** Ordinance lapses after 6 weeks. Government immediately re-issues the identical ordinance during the next recess.
5. **Final Verdict:** The Supreme Court declares this re-promulgation as constitutionally impermissible and a **Fraud on the Constitution**, striking down the ordinance.

## 10. Recent occurrence - debate

* **The Overuse of Article 123/213 in Modern Politics:** While the classic *D.C. Wadhwa* cases were state-centric (Bihar), recent debates center on both the Centre and States using ordinances to push through highly contentious policies (e.g., the initial promulgation of the Farm Laws or Land Acquisition amendments) without parliamentary/committee debate, only to let them lapse or withdraw them when facing political backlash.
* **The "Enduring Rights" Debate (Teachers' Appointment):** The core of the *Krishna Kumar Singh* case was whether Sanskrit school teachers, given 'government employee' status by repeated ordinances, retained that status after the ordinances died. The SC ruled that there is no automatic survival of rights; it depends on whether reversing the action is completely impractical or against public interest. The teachers ultimately lost their status.

## 11. Challenges

* **Evasion of Democratic Accountability:** The primary challenge is that ordinances allow the executive (CM/Cabinet) to create laws without answering questions, facing the opposition, or submitting to the detailed scrutiny of legislative committees.
* **Bypassing the Upper House:** In bicameral states (like UP, Maharashtra, Bihar), governments often use ordinances to bypass the Legislative Council if they do not hold a majority there, subverting the checks and balances of a bicameral legislature.
* **Legal Uncertainty:** When ordinances are promulgated, allowed to lapse, and re-promulgated, it creates massive legal uncertainty for citizens and businesses who do not know if the rules governing them today will exist tomorrow (the "Enduring Rights" chaos).
* **The "Satisfaction" Ambiguity:** While the SC has said the Governor's satisfaction is subject to judicial review, courts are historically very reluctant to question the political wisdom or the subjective "urgency" claimed by the executive, leaving a wide loophole for initial promulgation.

## 12. Way forward / Solution / Positive Outlook

* **Mandatory Explanatory Statements (Parliamentary Rules):** Drawing from Lok Sabha rules, State Assemblies must strictly enforce rules requiring the government to table a detailed statement explaining the specific "unforeseen circumstances" that necessitated the ordinance instead of waiting for a session.
* **Codifying the *Krishna Kumar Singh* Ruling:** The principles of the 2017 SC judgment should be explicitly codified into the Rules of Procedure of all State Assemblies, mandating that failure to table an ordinance constitutes a breach of privilege of the House.
* **Judicial Proactiveness:** High Courts must be more proactive in immediately staying re-promulgated ordinances at the very first instance, refusing to accept "executive necessity" as an excuse for repeated promulgations.
* **Extending the Assembly Sessions (NCRWC):** The root cause of ordinance misuse is the short duration of legislative sessions. State assemblies should be constitutionally mandated to meet for a minimum number of days per year (e.g., 60 or 90 days), severely restricting the "recess" window available for Article 213.

## 13. Best practices

* **India - The Supreme Court as the Ultimate Sentinel:** The evolution of Indian jurisprudence from *D.C. Wadhwa* (1986) to *Krishna Kumar Singh* (2017) is a remarkable domestic best practice of the judiciary continuously plugging constitutional loopholes exploited by the executive, firmly establishing that "constitutional fiction" (ordinances) cannot replace democratic reality.
* **United Kingdom - Civil Contingencies Act (2004):** When the UK executive exercises emergency regulatory powers, the law requires that Parliament must be recalled within 5 days if it is prorogued, and the regulations lapse after 7 days if not approved by Parliament. This ensures immediate democratic oversight of executive law-making, a model India's Article 213 desperately lacks.
* **Kerala - Legislative Productivity:** States like Kerala consistently record higher numbers of sitting days in their Legislative Assembly compared to the national average. By keeping the legislature active and productive, the structural excuse for resorting to the "ordinance route" is heavily minimized.

## 14. Conclusion

* The ordinance-making power under Article 213 is a constitutional anomaly designed as a "necessary evil" for genuine, unforeseen emergencies.
* Its routine misuse and cynical re-promulgation by state executives represents a profound subversion of legislative sovereignty. Upholding the Supreme Court's rigorous checks is essential to ensure that the primary function of making laws remains squarely in the hands of the people's elected representatives, not the executive fiat of the Raj Bhavan.
