```text
• Constitutional Intent: Designed under Article 356 as an emergency check for constitutional breakdown, which Dr. Ambedkar envisioned would remain an unused "dead letter."
• Colonial Origin: Direct structural descendant of Section 93 of the Government of India Act 1935, which preserved autocratic executive override over provincial ministries.
• Early Partisan Use: First weaponized in 1951 in Punjab for internal factional disputes and in 1959 to dismiss Kerala's democratically elected Communist government.
• Peak Abuse (1967–1980s): Exploited over 100 times by successive central governments to unseat regional coalitions and opposition parties without proving loss of legislative majority.
• Wholesale Purges: Weaponized for mass dismissals when the Janata government dissolved 9 Congress state assemblies in 1977, followed by identical retaliation by Indira Gandhi in 1980.
• Judicial Insulation: Temporarily shielded from legal challenge through the 38th Amendment (1975), before the 44th Amendment (1978) restored judicial review and capped durations.
• Sarkaria Recommendations: Recommended that Article 356 be invoked strictly as a last resort post-warning and that the assembly floor be the sole venue for majority tests.
• Bommai Paradigm Shift: S.R. Bommai (1994) curbed political misuse by subjecting proclamations to judicial review, enforcing mandatory floor tests, and asserting the power to reinstate dissolved assemblies.
• Basic Structure Integration: Elevated democracy and federalism to protected Basic Structure doctrines, prohibiting the Centre from treating state cabinets as subordinate agents.
• Modern Legacy: Reduced frequency of invocation dramatically from an average of several times a year pre-1994 to a rare, heavily litigated exception in modern Indian polity.

```
---


**Article 356** of the Constitution empowers the President to impose President’s Rule in a state if satisfied that *"a situation has arisen in which the government of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of this Constitution"*.

During the Constituent Assembly debates, **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar** expressed the hope that Article 356 would remain a **"dead letter"** and be invoked only as a measure of absolute last resort. However, between 1950 and the early 1990s, Article 356 was converted into a political weapon to destabilize and dismiss democratically elected opposition state governments.

Historians and constitutional jurists describe this period (especially 1967–1994) as the **"Era of Abuse of Article 356."**

---

### 1. Structural Origins of Misuse

```text
GOVERNMENT OF INDIA ACT, 1935 (Section 93)
[Colonial Governor's autocratic overriding power]
                     │
                     ▼
CONSTITUENT ASSEMBLY DEBATES (1949)
• Dr. Ambedkar: Article 356 will remain a "dead letter."
• H.V. Kamath & H.N. Kunzru: Warned it would build an imperialist Centre.
                     │
                     ▼
POST-INDEPENDENCE POLITICAL REALITY
(Invoked over 125+ times; predominantly against opposition parties)

```

* **Colonial Heritage:** Article 356 directly descends from **Section 93 of the Government of India Act, 1935**, which allowed British Governors to take over provincial administration at their sole discretion.
* **Constituent Assembly Apprehensions:** Members like H.V. Kamath and Shibban Lal Saksena opposed it as an instrument of unitary tyranny. Dr. Ambedkar defended it by stating that the Union would issue warnings first, but conceded: *"I do not altogether deny that there is a possibility of these articles being abused or employed for political purposes"*.

---

### 2. Chronological Trajectory of Abuse

The misuse unfolded across distinct political phases:

#### Phase I: The Monolithic Era (1950–1967)

* **First Invocation (Punjab, 1951):** Article 356 was invoked to resolve an intra-party feud within the ruling Congress party (Gopichand Bhargava vs. Bhim Sen Sachar), rather than any breakdown of constitutional machinery.
* **The Kerala Precedent (1959):** The world’s first democratically elected Communist ministry led by **E.M.S. Namboodiripad** was dismissed by the Nehru government via Article 356, citing "law and order breakdown" during the *Vimochana Samaram* (Liberation Struggle) spearheaded by church and caste groups, despite the ministry commanding a legislative majority.

#### Phase II: Post-1967 Regional Challenge & Indira Gandhi Era (1967–1977)

* Following the 1967 General Elections, **Samyukta Vidhayak Dal (SVD)** coalition governments captured power in multiple Northern states.
* Defections (*"Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram"*) flourished. Governors—acting as political agents of the Union Cabinet—routinely recommended President’s Rule rather than letting the Legislative Assembly test the majority on the floor.
* **The Apex of Misuse:** Under Indira Gandhi's regimes, Article 356 was invoked nearly **50 times** to dissolve opposition governments or discipline uncooperative regional units.
* **38th Constitutional Amendment (1975):** Added Clause (5) to Article 356, making the "satisfaction of the President" **final, conclusive, and immune from judicial review**.

#### Phase III: The Politics of Wholesale Retaliation (1977 & 1980)

The most flagrant abuse of Article 356 came in the form of **"Wholesale / En Masse Dissolutions"**:

```text
                      1977: THE JANATA PURGE
Congress swept out at Centre ──► Home Minister Charan Singh issues ultimatum
                                 │
                                 ▼
         President's Rule invoked SIMULTANEOUSLY in 9 Congress States
        (Held valid in State of Rajasthan v. UOI, 1977 on "political grounds")
                                 │
                                 ▼
                     1980: THE CONGRESS RETALIATION
Indira Gandhi returns to power ─► Dissolves 9 opposition-ruled state assemblies
                                  in one stroke using identical justification.

```

* **1977:** The Janata Party swept the Lok Sabha elections. Home Minister Charan Singh dismissed governments in **9 Congress-ruled states** (UP, Bihar, Punjab, Rajasthan, MP, etc.) on the absurd legal premise that their Lok Sabha defeat meant state assemblies no longer reflected the "will of the people".
* **State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977):** The Supreme Court upheld this action, ruling that presidential satisfaction under Article 356 was primarily a "political question" beyond extensive judicial scrutiny.
* **1980:** When Indira Gandhi returned to power, she retaliated using the exact same precedent, dismissing **9 opposition-ruled state governments** simultaneously.

---

### 3. The Reforming Interventions

The blatant misuse led to statutory, committee, and judicial pushbacks:

#### A. 44th Constitutional Amendment Act (1978)

* Deleted Clause (5) inserted by the 38th Amendment, restoring the **power of Judicial Review** over the President's proclamation.
* Inserted safeguards: President's Rule cannot be extended beyond 1 year unless a National Emergency is in operation and the Election Commission certifies that conducting assembly elections is difficult.

#### B. Sarkaria Commission Recommendations (1988)

* Stated Article 356 must be used **"very sparingly, in extreme cases, as a measure of last resort"**.
* Mandated that before invoking Article 356, the Centre should give a **prior warning** to the errant state government.
* Recommended that the **Floor Test in the Legislative Assembly** must be the only legitimate method to verify a government's majority, not subjective reports written in the Governor's Raj Bhavan.

---

### 4. The Watershed: S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)

The "Era of Abuse" came to an effective structural halt on March 11, 1994, when a **9-judge Constitution Bench** delivered the landmark **S.R. Bommai** verdict:

```text
               S.R. BOMMAI (1994) SAFEGUARDS MATRIX
                                 │
    ┌────────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────┐
    ▼                            ▼                            ▼
FLOOR TEST MANDATORY       PARLIAMENTARY PREREQUISITE   POWER OF RESTORATION
Majority cannot be tested  Assembly CANNOT be           Courts can REVIVE a
in Governor's chamber;     dissolved until both Houses  dissolved Assembly and
only on the Assembly floor. approve the Proclamation.    restore dismissed CM.

```

1. **Federalism is a Basic Feature:** The Court declared Federalism an inviolable part of the Basic Structure; state governments are not administrative vassals of the Centre.
2. **Floor Test Doctrine:** The strength of an incumbent ministry must be tested on the **floor of the Legislative Assembly**, not through subjective assessments by the Governor.
3. **No Premature Dissolution:** The President can only **suspend** the assembly initially; dissolution can occur **only after both Houses of Parliament have ratified the proclamation**.
4. **Power of Judicial Revival:** If the proclamation is struck down as *mala fide* or based on irrelevant grounds, the Supreme Court has the power to **undo the dissolution and restore the dismissed state government and Assembly**.

#### Post-Bommai Judicial Enforcement:

* **Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006):** Struck down the dissolution of the Bihar Assembly executed to prevent horse-trading as unconstitutional, affirming that preventing a party from trying to cobble together a post-poll coalition is not a ground for President's Rule.
* **Uttarakhand & Arunachal Pradesh Cases (2016):** The Supreme Court quashed the imposition of President’s Rule and **restored the dismissed Congress governments** to power, demonstrating that the arbitrary dismissal of state cabinets had become judicially impossible.
