# Women Severely Underrepresented in Judiciary

## 1. Intro

* The judiciary is the ultimate constitutional guarantor of fundamental rights and equality, yet it paradoxically suffers from a severe gender deficit, particularly at the higher echelons (High Courts and the Supreme Court).
* Despite women comprising nearly 50% of the population and increasingly matching men in law school enrollments, female representation in the Supreme Court and High Courts hovers at an abysmal 10–13%, highlighting a deep-rooted systemic barrier.

## 2. Sub-components of the topic [most imp]

* **The "Leaky Pipeline" (Lower vs. Higher Judiciary):** Women fare significantly better in the subordinate judiciary (around 30-35% representation) due to objective direct recruitment exams and state-level reservations. However, they hit a severe "glass ceiling" when elevated to High Courts and the Supreme Court.
* **The Collegium's Opacity:** The Collegium system relies on informal networks and peer evaluations. This "old boys' club" culture structurally disadvantages women, who often lack access to these entrenched male-dominated legal networks.
* **Patriarchy in Litigation:** The legal profession is highly demanding and historically hostile to women. Lack of maternity support, poor work-life balance, and patriarchal client biases lead to high attrition rates among female litigators, shrinking the feeder pool for judicial elevation.
* **Lack of Constitutional Quotas:** Unlike local self-governments (Article 243D) or the recently passed Women's Reservation Act for Parliament, there is no constitutional mandate for gender quotas in the higher judiciary.
* **Intersectionality:** The underrepresentation is acutely worse for women from SC, ST, and OBC communities, who face the double burden of caste discrimination and gender bias, making their presence in the higher judiciary virtually non-existent.
* **Senior Advocate Designation Bias:** Elevation to the bench often favors "Senior Advocates." High Courts and the Supreme Court have historically designated very few female lawyers as Seniors, cutting off a primary promotion avenue.
* **Impact on Jurisprudence:** A heavily male-dominated bench often lacks the lived experiences necessary for empathetic adjudication, occasionally resulting in tone-deaf judgments regarding sexual violence, reproductive autonomy, and matrimonial cruelty.

## 3. Evolution timeline

* 1950: Inauguration of the Supreme Court
The Supreme Court was established without a single female judge, a trend of total male exclusivity that would last for nearly four decades.


* 1959: First Female High Court Judge
Justice Anna Chandy was appointed to the Kerala High Court, becoming the first female High Court judge in India (and one of the first in the British Commonwealth).


* 1989: First Female Supreme Court Judge
Justice Fathima Beevi was appointed as the first female judge of the Supreme Court of India, a full 39 years after the Court's inception.


* 1992: First Female Chief Justice of a High Court
Justice Leila Seth broke another glass ceiling by becoming the first woman to serve as the Chief Justice of a State High Court (Himachal Pradesh).


* 2018: Direct Elevation from the Bar
Indu Malhotra became the first female Senior Advocate to be elevated directly from the Bar to the Supreme Court.


* 2021: Historic Swearing-in
Three women judges (Justices Hima Kohli, B.V. Nagarathna, and Bela M. Trivedi) were sworn into the Supreme Court simultaneously, marking the highest number of sitting female SC judges at one time.


* 2027 (Projected): First Female CJI
Justice B.V. Nagarathna is slated to become the first female Chief Justice of India, though her tenure will be remarkably short (just 36 days).

## 5. Committee or Reports

* **Parliamentary Standing Committee on Law and Justice (2023):** Expressed deep concern over the "abysmal" representation of women and marginalized sections in higher courts. It explicitly recommended that the Collegium must ensure adequate representation and reflect the country's social diversity.
* **India Justice Report (Tata Trusts, 2022/2023):** Consistently flags the "missing women" in Indian courts. The report highlighted that several High Courts had *zero* female judges, and the national average at the HC level was struggling to cross 13%.
* **Vidhi Centre for Legal Policy (Lower Judiciary Analysis):** Published data showing that while women make up roughly 35% of Civil Judges (Junior Division) due to exams, their proportion drops drastically at the District Judge level due to promotional biases, transfer policies, and familial constraints.
* **Law Commission of India (14th Report, 1958):** Though a historical document, it first flagged the critical need to tap into *all* sections of the bar for judicial appointments, a principle that implicitly demands gender inclusion in the modern context.
* **Supreme Court Women Lawyers Association (SCWLA) Petitions:** Continually highlights systemic bias in designating "Senior Advocates," submitting reports to the SC demanding structural interventions to create a level playing field for female litigators.

## 6. Indices

* **Gender Inequality Index (UNDP):** India's persistently low ranking is a direct reflection of structural inequalities, including the glaring absence of women in apex decision-making roles like the higher judiciary.
* **Global Gender Gap Report (World Economic Forum):** Tracks political empowerment and economic participation. While the judiciary is a separate pillar, the severe lack of women at the top drags down India's overall metrics for "women in leadership roles."
* **Rule of Law Index (World Justice Project):** The "Civil Justice" and "Criminal Justice" sub-factors assess whether the system is free from discrimination. A heavily male-dominated judiciary is often perceived by the public (especially female litigants) as inherently biased.
* **SDG India Index (NITI Aayog):** Goal 5 (Gender Equality) and Goal 16 (Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions). The failure to elevate women to the higher judiciary reflects poorly on India's commitment to building inclusive institutions under SDG 16.

## 7. Global comparison

* **United States:** The US Supreme Court currently features 4 female justices out of 9 (approx. 44%), the highest in its history. This reflects a deliberate, bipartisan political push over the last decade to ensure gender parity at the highest level of justice.
* **United Kingdom:** The UK has actively worked on diversity, moving away from its traditional "old boys" network. The Judicial Appointments Commission (JAC) has a statutory duty to increase diversity, though female representation in the UK Supreme Court still hovers around 30%.
* **South Africa:** Section 174(2) of the South African Constitution explicitly mandates that "the need for the judiciary to reflect broadly the racial and gender composition of South Africa must be considered" during judicial appointments, providing a strong constitutional basis for diversity.
* **International Criminal Court (ICC):** The Rome Statute explicitly requires a "fair representation of female and male judges," mathematically mandating gender parity at the international tribunal level.
* **Rwanda:** Post-genocide Rwanda rebuilt its constitution with massive gender quotas, leading to over 50% female representation in both its parliament and its judiciary, acting as a global outlier in female empowerment.

## 9. Flow chart

**The Career Bottlenecks for Women in Law**

1. **Law School:** Near equal enrollment and graduation of men and women.
2. **Entry into Litigation:** High initial entry, but women face a hostile physical infrastructure (lack of washrooms, crèches).
3. **Mid-Career Attrition:** Marriage, maternity leave, and unequal caregiving burdens lead to massive dropout rates from active court litigation.
4. **The Designation Hurdle:** The opaque "networking" required for 'Senior Advocate' designation heavily favors male lawyers, locking women out of elite status.
5. **Subordinate Judiciary Route:** Women excel in objective direct recruitment exams (Lower Judiciary).
6. **The Promotional Glass Ceiling:** Elevation to District Judge/High Court is hampered by subjective ACRs (Annual Confidential Reports), inflexible transfer policies, and Collegium opacity.
7. **Apex Court Exclusion:** Very few women reach the High Court early enough to build the seniority required to become Chief Justices of HCs, preventing their eventual elevation to the Supreme Court.

## 10. Recent occurrence - debate

* **Lack of Basic Court Infrastructure:** Chief Justice D.Y. Chandrachud recently sparked a national debate by highlighting survey data showing that a staggering number of district courts lack separate, functional washrooms for female lawyers and judges, making the physical environment deeply degrading.
* **The "Tokenism" vs. Substantive Change Debate:** The celebration surrounding Justice B.V. Nagarathna becoming the first female CJI in 2027 is tempered by the debate that a 36-day tenure is largely symbolic, doing little to allow her to make long-term administrative reforms.
* **The Spillover from the Women's Reservation Act:** Following the passage of the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam (reserving 33% seats in Parliament and Assemblies), bar associations and civil rights groups have intensely renewed debates demanding a similar constitutional amendment for the higher judiciary.
* **The "Uncle Judges" Syndrome:** Debates surrounding the Collegium system continually highlight how elevation often favors candidates with familial legal connections (generational male lawyers), structurally locking out first-generation female lawyers who lack dynastic backing.

## 11. Challenges

* **Opaque Collegium System:** The lack of formal, objective, and published criteria for elevation allows unconscious gender biases to persist. Male judges tend to network with and elevate male lawyers they are familiar with.
* **The "Merit" Fallacy:** Opponents of diversity often use the flawed counter-argument that actively seeking female judges compromises "judicial merit," completely ignoring the systemic historical disadvantages and the fact that diversity *enhances* judicial merit.
* **Inflexible Transfer Policies:** High Court judges are subject to inter-state transfers. Deeply entrenched societal norms make it disproportionately harder for women to accept elevation if it entails uprooting their families and spouses' careers.
* **Client Bias in Private Practice:** In high-stakes corporate or criminal litigation, clients still heavily prefer male lawyers. This reduces the high-profile case visibility of female lawyers, which is often a prerequisite for being noticed by the Collegium.
* **Maternity and Seniority:** Because women often lose critical career years to maternity and child-rearing, they rarely accumulate the strict chronological seniority required by the Collegium to become Chief Justices of High Courts.

## 12. Way forward / Solution / Positive Outlook

* **Amending the Memorandum of Procedure (MoP):** As recommended by the Parliamentary Standing Committee (2023), the MoP must be officially amended to explicitly mandate the Supreme Court and High Court Collegiums to ensure adequate gender representation during elevations.
* **Infrastructure Audit & Funding (NCW/SC Directives):** The government must launch a Centrally Sponsored Scheme to fund the immediate construction of female washrooms, safe transport, and functional crèches in all district court complexes, audited by the National Commission for Women.
* **Objective Senior Designation (Indira Jaising Guidelines):** High Courts must strictly apply the Supreme Court's objective, points-based system for designating Senior Advocates, ensuring female litigators are fairly assessed on publications and pro-bono work rather than subjective networking.
* **Relaxing Age and Seniority Criteria:** The Collegium should adopt a flexible approach to age and seniority for female candidates to account for the career breaks caused by maternity, focusing on the qualitative depth of their jurisprudence rather than just years of continuous practice.
* **Publishing Diversity Data:** The Department of Justice should mandate the annual publication of diversity audits for all High Courts and the Supreme Court to "name and shame" institutions that fail to elevate women, driving accountability.

## 13. Best practices

* **India (Subordinate Judiciary Quotas):** States like Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Bihar, and Rajasthan have implemented 30-33% horizontal reservations for women in the lower judiciary. This has been highly successful, with women often comprising over 40-50% of recent Civil Judge (Junior Division) recruitments.
* **United Kingdom (Judicial Appointments Commission):** The UK's JAC operates independently of the judiciary and the executive. It has a statutory duty to "encourage diversity" in the candidate pool, making the process transparent and actively headhunting non-traditional, female candidates.
* **Canada (Open Application Process):** Supreme Court vacancies in Canada are filled through a public, open application process evaluated by an independent advisory board. This removes the secrecy of the "tap-on-the-shoulder" model and actively encourages female jurists to apply on their own merit.
* **South Africa (Constitutional Diversity Mandate):** By constitutionally embedding the requirement for the judiciary to reflect the nation's gender and racial demographics, South Africa has created a legally binding imperative that forces appointing bodies to prioritize diversity alongside merit.

## 14. Conclusion

* The legitimacy of the judiciary rests entirely on public trust, which is severely eroded when the bench does not reflect the diversity of the society whose lives it governs and whose rights it adjudicates.
* Achieving gender parity in the higher judiciary is not merely a matter of token representation; it is a profound constitutional imperative to ensure that the delivery of justice is enriched, empathetic, and informed by the lived experiences of half the nation's population.
