# Local Government


### Evolution in Democratic Decentralization
1. Community Development Programme (1952) and National Extension Service (1953) lacked popular participation; 
2. the Balwant Rai Mehta Committee (1957) recommended a three-tier Panchayati Raj system, first launched at  agaur, Rajasthan (1959); the Ashok Mehta Committee (1978) added political-party participation and district-level planning.
3. 73rd and 74th CAA 1992 made 
   1. Panchayats and Municipalities institutions of self-government (Part IX/IX-A) 
   2. State Election Commissions and 
   3. State Finance Commissions; 
   4. 11th Schedule lists 29 subjects for Panchayats, 
   5. 12th Schedule 18 functions for Municipalities.
4. Current status
   1. Over 2.5 lakh Panchayats and 
   2. ≈24 lakh elected representatives; 
   3. the Gram Sabha - all registered voters - is 
   4. India's most extensive direct-democracy mechanism, approving plans and monitoring expenditure.

### Key Developments
1. Reservations:
   1. Articles 243D and 243T mandate reservation for SC, ST and at least one-third of seats for women; 
   2. 20+ States and 2 Union Territories have raised women's reservation to 50%; 
   3. the Sashakt Panchayat Netri Abhiyan strengthens women representatives leadership.
2. Shift to Participatory Planning
   1. The Gram Panchayat Development Plan (GPDP) lets communities set local priorities
3. Self-Governance in Tribal Areas: 
   1. The Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA) recognises tribal Gram Sabhas' authority over Jal, Jungle and Jameen (water, forest, land); by December 2025, 8 of 10 PESA States had framed rules.
4. Digitalisation of Grassroots Governance: 
   1. eGramSwaraj integrates planning, monitoring, accounting and payments; Meri Panchayat won the WSIS Champion Award 2025; SabhaSaar, integrated with Bhashini, uses artificial intelligence to generate Gram Sabha minutes in 13 regional languages.
5. Technology-Based Property & Spatial Empowerment
   1. The Survey of Villages and Mapping with Improvised Technology in Village Areas (SVAMITVA) Scheme uses drones and Geographic Information System (GIS) technology to issue legally recognised property cards to rural households; Gram Manchitra supports GIS-based infrastructure planning.
6. Shift to Performance-Based Governance
   1. The Panchayat Advancement Index evaluates local performance on poverty reduction, health, water security and gender equality; the Panchayat Devolution Index 2024 assesses States on transfer of Functions, Finances and Functionaries.


### Challenges/Issues
1. Incomplete devolution of 3F: Funds, Functions and Functionaries
   1. Articles 243G and 243W do not make devolution mandatory; the Panchayat Devolution Index 2024 shows devolution rose only from 39.9% (2013-14) to 43.9% (2021–22).
      1. Legally enforceable Activity Mapping Frameworks with a Function-Finance-Functionary Matrix; 
2. Fiscal Dependency & Weak State Finance Commissions
   1. Local bodies' Own-Source Revenue is only ≈0.4% of GDP; State Finance Commissions (Articles 2431/243У) are often constituted late, with delayed and inconsistent reports.
      1. Constitute State Finance Commissions on a fixed calendar; mandate tabling of reports and Action Taken Reports within six months via a State Fiscal Devolution Dashboard.
3. Bureaucratic Supremaсу Over Elected Representatives
   1. State-controlled cadres remain accountable to departmental superiors, not elected councils, creating an authority-accountability mismatch.
      1. Establish dedicated local government cadres accountable to elected councils; universalise social audits and independent local Ombudsmen.
4. Weak Gram Sabhas & Tokenistic Participation
   1. Low attendance, inadequate notice, poor documentation and domination by local elites.
      1. Restore the Gram Sabha as a deliberative institution - publicly announced calendar, adequate notice, plainlanguage budget and audit documents,
5. Delayed Elections & Constrained State Election Commissions
   1. Elections are sometimes postponed; State Election Commissions remain dependent on State governments for staff and funding despite the Supreme Court's emphasis on their independence (Stote of Goa v. Fouziya Imtiaz Shaikh, 2021).
      1. Ensure genuine functional and financial independence for State Election Commissions, in line with the Supreme Court's directive.
6. Weak Implementation of PESA
   1. State forest, mining, excise and land-acquisition laws frequently conflict with PESA; Gram Sabha consultation is sometimes reduced to procedural approval.
      1. Harmonise state laws on mining, excise, markets and land acquisition with PESA; digitally record and independently verify Gram Sabha consent in tribal lanauaaes
7. Digital Divide & PortalBased Compliance
   1. Connectivity and digital skills vary across Panchayats; uploading plans online does not guarantee informed Gram Sabha deliberation, risking technocratic recentralisation.
      1. Ensure digital platforms complement - rather than replace - genuine Gram Sabha deliberation; verify ground-level outcomes through independent social audits.
8. Representation Empowerment Gap
   1. The practice of proxy representation ("Sarpanch Pati") persists in some areas; frequent rotation of reserved constituencies weakens leadership continuity.
      1. Penalise unauthorised interference by "Sarpanch Patis"; institutionalise the Sashakt Panchayat Netri Abhiyan through State training institutes and district mentorship centres.

> Conclusion
1. While the 73rd and 74th Amendments successfully institutionalised grassroots democracy, true self-governance remains constrained by bureaucratic control and fiscal deficits.

2. The 16th Finance Commission's grants offer a crucial financial window, but money alone cannot fix structural bottlenecks.
3. India's next-generation reforms must transcend token electoral existence - true democratic decentralisation requires functional autonomy, predictable devolution, administrative capacity and absolute accountability at the grassroots.
