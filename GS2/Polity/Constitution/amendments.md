1. Intro
Operating under Part-XX, Article-368, constitutional amendments provide institutional elasticity, balancing adaptability with structural fidelity to core democratic principles.

2. Procedure
   1. A-368(1): Parliament - addition, variation, repeal
   2. A-368(2): Steps
      1. Bill Introduction
      2. total membership majority + 2/3rd majority - present and voting
      3. President's assent
      4. Amendment - done
      5. Additional Requiremnt: Ratification by at least half of the states

         2. Ch-4(The Union Judiciary)[124-147] of P-5(The Union)[52-151], Ch-5(The High Courts in the States)[214-232] of P-6[152-237], Ch-1(LEGISLATIVE RELATIONS) of P-11(RELATIONS BETWEEN THE UNION AND THE STATES)

3. Limitations to amendments
   1. Procedural
      1. Discussions, Debate and Deliberations requiring on the floor of the house
      2. 2/3-special majority
      3. absolute majority
      4. ratification by half of the states only on matters of federal character(7th schedule, Elec of President)
      5. Separate passage(A-368(2)) by each house without Joint sitting under A-108.
      6. State inability to initiate Constitutional Amendments
   2. Substantive Hurdles
      1. Open to Judicial Review
      2. SC can strike down CAA - 99th CAA, 2014
      3. Limited by Basic Structure Doctrine - Keshavananda Bharati Case, 1973
      4. Limited amending powers under minerva mills case 1980
      5. Fund Rights protection - Waman Rao Case: 1981
      6. IR Coelho Case: 2007
   3. Miscellaneous other hurdles
      1. Press Conference with QnA sessions - 4th pillar of Democracy
      2. Coalition Government
      3. General Elections every 5 years
   - Amendments are possible, but not unlimited.
   - The Basic Structure doctrine
   - Constitution is flexible in form but rigid in core values.
   - This balance makes constitutional change gradual and legitimate.

4. Major issues
   - amendments without serious deliberation.
   - disruptions hampering discussion, debate and deliberations
   - Constitutional amendment can become a vehicle for majoritarian or identity politics.
   - The challenge is balancing reform with constitutional morality.
   - Judicial review is essential to keep amendments within constitutional limits.

5. Important amendments
   - 42nd Amendment, 1976: added Directive Principles and strengthened state power; controversial in its scope.
   - 44th Amendment, 1978: restored democratic safeguards after Emergency.
   - 73rd and 74th Amendments, 1991: decentralised local governance.
   - 101st Amendment, 2016: introduced GST and cooperative federalism.
   - 103rd Amendment, 2018: introduced EWS reservation.

6. Case laws
   - Shankari Prasad(1951): upheld Parliament's power to amend the Constitution. 
   - Sajjan Singh(1965): affirmed that fundamental rights can be amended.
   - Golaknath(1967): held that Parliament cannot amend fundamental rights. 
   - Kesavananda Bharati(1973): established the Basic Structure doctrine, limiting Parliament's amending power.
   - L. Chandra Kumar(1997): reinforced judicial review of constitutional amendments.
   - Source: Supreme Court judgments.

7. 101st CAA, 2016 - GST - replaced fragmented web of central and state indirect taxes with a unified destination based value-added tax regime
   1. features (asked in 2017)
      1. Concurrent Taxing Powers (Article 246A): Conferred simultaneous power upon both Parliament and State Legislatures to make laws with respect to goods and services tax. This broke the traditional watertight separation where the Centre taxed manufacturing/services and States taxed sales.
      2. Inter-State Trade & IGST (Article 269A): Vested exclusive power in the Union to levy and collect Integrated GST (IGST) on inter-state supply of goods and services, as well as on imports. The collected IGST is apportioned between the Union and the States as per the recommendations of the GST Council.
      3. Institutionalized GST Council (Article 279A): Created a constitutional joint forum—the GST Council—chaired by the Union Finance Minister and comprising Union Minister of State for Revenue/Finance and State Finance Ministers, tasked with recommending tax rates, exemptions, model laws, and thresholds.
      4. Subsumption of Multiple Indirect Taxes:
         1. Central Taxes Subsumed: Central Excise Duty, Additional Duties of Excise, Service Tax, Countervailing Duty (CVD), Special Additional Duty of Customs (SAD), and Central Surcharges/Cesses. 
         2. State Taxes Subsumed: State VAT/Sales Tax, Central Sales Tax (CST), Entertainment Tax (other than levied by local bodies), Luxury Tax, Taxes on lottery/betting/gambling, Entry Tax, and Octroi.
      5. Exclusions & Petroleum Carve-Out: Alcohol for human consumption was kept entirely outside the ambit of GST (States retain exclusive taxing power). Five petroleum products (petroleum crude, high-speed diesel, petrol, natural gas, aviation turbine fuel) were temporarily excluded, with their induction subject to future dates decided by the GST Council.
      6. Compensation Mechanism (Section 18): Mandated Parliament to provide compensation to States by law for loss of revenue arising from GST implementation for a period of 5 years (funded via the GST Compensation Cess).
   2. significance
      1. Creation of a Common National Market: Abolished internal trade barriers, physical border check-posts, and entry taxes, enabling frictionless inter-state movement of freight across India ("One Nation, One Tax, One Market").
      2. Elimination of Cascading Effect ("Tax on Tax"): Seamless flow of Input Tax Credit (ITC) across the entire value chain—from manufacture down to the end consumer—removed tax cascading and lowered the effective tax burden on several finished goods.
      3. Shift to Destination-Based Taxation: Shifted the tax incidence from the origin/manufacturing State to the consuming State. This structurally enhanced the long-term revenue base of populous, consumption-heavy States.
      4. Formalization and Digital Compliance Trail: Built around a unified digital infrastructure (GSTN). Continuous invoice matching reduced avenues for unrecorded transactions, widening the formal tax net.
   3. spirit of federalism - how ?
      1. Shared Legislative Competence: Unlike the traditional Seventh Schedule model where List I and List II subjects are mutually exclusive, Article 246A grants concurrent jurisdiction to the Union and States over the same tax base without a direct application of Article 254 repugnancy.
      2. Consensus-Driven Institutional Architecture (Article 279A):
         1. The GST Council operates on a shared decision-making formula: the Centre holds one-third (33.3%) of the total voting weight, while all States together hold two-thirds (66.7%).
         2. Any decision requires a three-fourths (75%) majority. This means neither the Centre nor the States alone can push through a decision unilaterally; the Centre cannot pass a resolution without the support of a substantial number of States, nor can all States combined pass a resolution without the Centre's agreement.
      3. Horizontal & Vertical Equity: Every State, irrespective of geographic size or economic output (from Uttar Pradesh to Sikkim), has one vote within the States' collective share, upholding horizontal equality among federating units.
      4. Judicial Affirmation (Mohit Minerals Case, 2022): The Supreme Court affirmed that the GST regime embodies "uncooperative and cooperative federalism", clarifying that GST Council recommendations are persuasive and not legally binding on Parliament and State Legislatures, thereby preserving the fundamental legislative autonomy of States.
   4. Federal Challenges
      1. State lost taxing autonomy
      2. benefited consuming states: Manufacturing-heavy states (e.g., Tamil Nadu, Gujarat, Maharashtra) faced initial revenue shocks and reliance on compensation cess.
      3. Dispute Redressal: Delays in establishing an independent statutory GST Appellate Tribunal created administrative friction.
