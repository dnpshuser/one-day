1. Intro
Operating under Article 368, constitutional amendments provide institutional elasticity, balancing  adaptability with structural fidelity to core democratic principles.

2. Procedure
- Article 368 lays down the amendment machinery.
- Most amendments require a special majority of Parliament.
- Certain amendments(modifying federal structure) require ratification by half the states.
- This ensures both national consensus and federal balance.

3. Scope and limitations
- Amendments are possible, but not unlimited.
- The Basic Structure doctrine 
- Constitution is flexible in form but rigid in core values.
- This balance makes constitutional change gradual and legitimate.

4. Major issues
- amendments without serious deliberation.
- Constitutional amendment can become a vehicle for majoritarian or identity politics.
- The challenge is balancing reform with constitutional morality.
- Judicial review is essential to keep amendments within constitutional limits.

5. Important amendments
- 42nd Amendment: added Directive Principles and strengthened state power; controversial in its scope.
- 44th Amendment: restored democratic safeguards after Emergency.
- 73rd and 74th Amendments: decentralised local governance.
- 101st Amendment: introduced GST and cooperative federalism.
- 103rd Amendment: introduced EWS reservation.

6. Case laws
- Shankari Prasad(1951): upheld Parliament's power to amend the Constitution. 
- Sajjan Singh(1965): affirmed that fundamental rights can be amended.
- Golaknath(1967): held that Parliament cannot amend fundamental rights. 
- Kesavananda Bharati(1973): established the Basic Structure doctrine, limiting Parliament's amending power.
- L. Chandra Kumar(1997): reinforced judicial review of constitutional amendments.
- Source: Supreme Court judgments.

1. Conclusion
Harmonizing constitutional dynamism with the Basic Structure doctrine, future amendments must institutionalize broad-based pre-legislative consultation, as recommended by the NCRWC.
