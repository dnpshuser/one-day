## Evolution

**Article 21** of the Constitution of India is concise in its wording:

> *"No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law."*

Despite its textual brevity, judicial interpretation over seven decades has transformed Article 21 from a narrow safeguard against arbitrary executive detention into the substantive reservoir of human rights in India.

---

### 1. The Literal & Restrictive Phase: *A.K. Gopalan (1950)*

In **A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras (1950)**, a communist leader challenged his preventive detention. The 6-judge Constitution Bench took an originalist, mechanical view:

* **Silo Approach to Rights:** The Court held that Fundamental Rights are mutually exclusive watertight compartments. An infringement of personal liberty had to be tested under Article 21 alone, not in conjunction with Article 19(1)(d) (freedom of movement) or Article 14 (equality).
* **"Procedure Established by Law" vs. "Due Process":** The Court held that "procedure established by law" meant merely a formal, enacted law passed by a competent legislature. If valid legislation authorized deprivation of liberty, courts could not test whether the law itself was fair, just, or reasonable.
* **Narrow Definition of "Life":** "Life" was understood essentially as physical existence—freedom from physical restraint, incarceration, or direct bodily harm.

---

### 2. The Doctrinal Shift: The Golden Triangle in *Maneka Gandhi (1978)*

The watershed transformation came after the Emergency in **Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978)**, where the petitioner’s passport was impounded without giving any reasons:

* **The "Golden Triangle" (Articles 14, 19, and 21):** The 7-judge bench overruled the *Gopalan* compartmentalization. It ruled that Fundamental Rights form an interconnected web:
```
                        Article 14
                        (Equality & Non-arbitrariness)
                               ▲
                              / \
                             /   \
                            /     \
                           ▼       ▼
                     Article 19 ──► Article 21
                    (Freedoms)      (Life & Liberty)

```


Any procedure depriving a person of life or personal liberty must satisfy the requirements of Article 19 and must not be arbitrary under Article 14.
* **Importing Substantive "Due Process":** The Court read the American doctrine of substantive due process into the Indian Constitution: the procedure cannot be merely legal; it must be **"just, fair, and reasonable"**, not fanciful, oppressive, or arbitrary.
* **Life as Human Dignity:** The Court departed from the physical-survival definition: "Life" does not mean mere animal existence; it encompasses the quality of life and human dignity.

---

### 3. Expansion Through Substantive Rights

Following *Maneka Gandhi*, the Supreme Court weaponized Article 21 through Public Interest Litigation (PIL), converting non-justiciable **Directive Principles of State Policy (Part IV)** into enforceable Fundamental Rights:

| Epochal Landmark Judgment | Derivative Right Inscribed under Article 21 | Constitutional Significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Francis Coralie Mullin v. UT of Delhi (1981)** | **Right to Live with Human Dignity** | Clarified that "life" includes bare necessities of life: adequate nutrition, clothing, shelter, and facilities for reading, writing, and expressing oneself. |
| **Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984)** | **Right Against Bonded Labour & Exploitation** | Read Article 23 into Article 21 to enforce rehabilitation and dignity for bonded and child laborers. |
| **Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985)** | **Right to Livelihood** | Pavement and slum dwellers cannot be evicted without procedural fairness, as deprivation of livelihood effectively deprives life. |
| **Parmanand Katara v. Union of India (1989)** | **Right to Emergency Medical Care** | Declared that every doctor (government or private) has an overriding professional and constitutional obligation to extend immediate medical aid to injured persons without waiting for police clearance. |
| **Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar (1991)** | **Right to Pollution-Free Water and Air** | Placed environmental protection and enjoyment of unpolluted natural resources directly within the core of Article 21. |
| **Mohini Jain (1992) & Unni Krishnan (1993)** | **Right to Primary Education** | Held that the right to life cannot be exercised without education. Directly catalyzed Parliament to pass the **86th Amendment Act, 2002**, inserting **Article 21A**. |
| **Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997)** | **Right to Gender Equality & Safe Workplaces** | Formulated the *Vishaka Guidelines* against sexual harassment at work by interpreting Article 21 alongside CEDAW. |
| **Hussainara Khatoon (1979) & Sunil Batra (1980)** | **Prisoner Rights & Right to Speedy Trial** | Extended rights against solitary confinement, bar fetters, handcuffs, and prolonged pre-trial incarceration. |

---

### 4. Modern Frontiers: Autonomy, Privacy, and Climate

In the modern era, the Supreme Court has expanded Article 21 from socio-economic survival to personal autonomy, bodily integrity, and intergenerational equity:

* **Right to Privacy as an Intrinsic Facet (*K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, 2017*):**
A 9-judge bench unanimously held that privacy is a fundamental right emanating directly from the sanctuary of Article 21 (informational privacy, bodily autonomy, and spatial privacy). Any state intrusion must pass the three-fold test of **legality, legitimate state aim, and proportionality**.
* **Right to Die with Dignity (*Common Cause v. Union of India, 2018*):**
Recognized that the right to live with dignity extends to a dignified death; legalized **passive euthanasia** and formalized the execution of **Living Wills / Advance Medical Directives**.
* **Decriminalization of Autonomy (*Navtej Singh Johar, 2018* & *Joseph Shine, 2018*):**
Affirmed that Article 21 guarantees sexual orientation, self-determination, and the dignity of individual choice against state-enforced majoritarian morality.
* **Right Against Adverse Impacts of Climate Change (*M.K. Ranjitsinh v. Union of India, 2024*):**
Recognized that the escalating climate crisis threatens the right to life and health, elevating the right to be free from climate harm into an actionable constitutional entitlement under Articles 14 and 21.

### DNA testing

The law relating to the DNA testing of a fetus (child in the womb) to establish paternity sits at a sensitive intersection of **Article 21 (Right to Privacy, Bodily Autonomy, and Reproductive Choice)** and statutory evidentiary rules governing legitimacy.

The Supreme Court and various High Courts have consistently held that **ordering an invasive prenatal medical test (such as amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling) solely to determine paternity in civil or matrimonial disputes violates Article 21 and is impermissible in ordinary circumstances**.

---

### Constitutional Foundations under Article 21

```text
                           ARTICLE 21
                               │
       ┌───────────────────────┼───────────────────────┐
       ▼                       ▼                       ▼
Right to Bodily         Right to Privacy        Right to Life & Health
   Integrity             & Autonomy                of the Fetus
(No forced medical      (K.S. Puttaswamy:        (Invasive tests carry
  procedure)             Reproductive choices)     risk of miscarriage)

```

1. **Bodily Autonomy of the Mother:**
* In *K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017)* and *Suchita Srivastava v. Chandigarh Administration (2009)*, the Supreme Court established that a woman's reproductive choices and bodily integrity are fundamental dimensions of personal liberty under Article 21.
* Compelling a pregnant woman to undergo an invasive surgical procedure to extract fetal DNA against her consent amounts to an unlawful violation of her bodily sovereignty.


2. **Inherent Medical Risk to the Unborn Child:**
* Establishing fetal paternity requires obtaining fetal cells through procedures like **Amniocentesis** (amniotic fluid extraction) or **Chorionic Villus Sampling (CVS)**. Both procedures carry medically recognized risks of intrauterine infection, fetal trauma, and miscarriage.
* Courts have held that subjecting a viable fetus to physical danger simply to resolve an adult's matrimonial dispute or suspicion of adultery infringes the right to life and safety.


3. **Protection Against the Stigma of Bastardy:**
* In *Goutam Kundu v. State of West Bengal (1993)* and reaffirmed in *Aparna Ajinkya Firodia v. Ajinkya Arun Firodia (2023)*, the Supreme Court emphasized that courts should not order DNA tests routinely, as a negative finding brands a child with the social stigma of illegitimacy.



---

### Statutory Interplay: Evidence Act vs. DNA Testing

In ordinary paternity disputes, the legal presumption of legitimacy takes precedence over scientific inquiries:

```text
HUSBAND & WIFE (Valid Marriage)
              │
              ▼
   Birth / Conception during Marriage
              │
              ▼
    PRESUMPTION OF LEGITIMACY
    [Section 112, IEA / Section 116, BSA]
              │
              ▼
   Can ONLY be rebutted by proving:
        "NON-ACCESS" between spouses
(DNA test cannot be claimed as a matter of right)

```

* **Section 112 of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872** (now **Section 116 of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023**):
* Any child born during the continuance of a valid marriage is **conclusive proof of legitimacy**, unless the husband can establish that the parties had **no access** to each other at any time when the child could have been begotten.


* **No Roving Inquiries:**
* In *Kamti Devi v. Poshi Ram (2001)* and *Bhabani Prasad Jena v. Convenor Secretary, Orissa State Commission for Women (2010)*, the Supreme Court held that a DNA test cannot be directed as a "fishing or roving inquiry" to generate evidence of adultery.
* There must be a strong *prima facie* case establishing non-access before any DNA examination can even be considered.



---

### Key Judicial Precedents on Prenatal Paternity Testing

| Case Law & Forum | Legal Context | Judicial Ruling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bhabani Prasad Jena v. State Commission for Women (SC, 2010)** | Direction issued to conduct a DNA test on a fetus to verify paternity allegations. | The Supreme Court set aside the order, holding that courts must exercise extreme caution. A DNA test cannot be directed mechanically, balancing the competing interests of truth versus privacy and bodily dignity under Article 21. |
| **K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (SC, 2017)** | Expansion of Right to Privacy. | Affirmed that privacy includes **informational privacy** and **spatial/bodily privacy**. Compulsory medical testing directly infringes the proportionality standard. |
| **Aparna Ajinkya Firodia v. Ajinkya Arun Firodia (SC, 2023)** | Husband demanded DNA testing to disprove paternity. | The Supreme Court held that a child should not be subjected to a DNA test simply to settle a husband's allegations of infidelity. The test can only be ordered when there is exceptional, incontrovertible proof of non-access. |
| **High Court Jurisprudence (e.g., Delhi & Kerala HCs)** | Petitions seeking prenatal paternity tests to prove infidelity or defend maintenance claims. | High Courts have consistently held that **a woman cannot be compelled to undergo an amniocentesis or CVS procedure while pregnant to determine paternity**, citing high risk to fetal life and violation of Article 21. |

### The Exception: Criminal Investigations (Rape and Sexual Assault)

The only situation where prenatal DNA sampling is statutorily governed and judicially permitted is **criminal investigations of sexual offenses**:

```text
INVESTIGATION OF RAPE / POCSO
              │
              ▼
Pregnancy results from alleged offense
              │
              ├────────────────────────────────────────┐
              ▼                                        ▼
Medical Termination of Pregnancy (MTP)     Victim decides to carry to term
              │                                        │
              ▼                                        ▼
Tissue / Fetal remains preserved           Blood/DNA sample collected
post-abortion for forensic DNA              from the child POST-BIRTH
(Section 53A CrPC / 52 BNSS)                (Avoids invasive prenatal risk)

```

1. **Section 53A of the CrPC** (now **Section 52 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023**):
* Authorizes the collection of DNA and biological samples in rape cases for forensic comparison.


2. **Standard Protocol:**
* If a victim of rape undergoes a **Medical Termination of Pregnancy (MTP)**, doctors are legally mandated to preserve the aborted fetal remains/tissue for forensic DNA testing to establish the accused's culpability.
* If the pregnancy is carried to term, courts and investigative agencies almost universally wait until **after delivery** to collect blood or buccal swabs from the newborn, rather than performing an invasive, high-risk prenatal extraction.



---

### Summary of Legal Principles

* **Civil / Matrimonial Matters:** A husband cannot compel his pregnant wife to undergo a prenatal DNA test to prove infidelity or disprove paternity. It violates her Article 21 right to bodily autonomy and endangers the fetus.
* **Presumption Trumps Biology:** Statutory law presumes legitimacy unless "non-access" is proven first; scientific tests cannot bypass this presumption.
* **Forensic Necessity:** In rape cases, fetal DNA is extracted from aborted tissue post-MTP, or postponed until after live birth to avoid intrauterine medical risks.
