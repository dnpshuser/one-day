1. Intro: Constitutional morality is the moral architecture of the Constitution, understanding its underlying spirit, ethos, values (liberty, equality, fraternity, dignity), and institutional self-restraint beyond mere adherence to the strict letter of the Constitution.
2. Given by George Grote (1846)
3. Principles: 
   1. Paramount Reverence/respect for the Constitution: A deep, habituated respect by citizens and magistrates for institutional rules and forms.
   2. Diffusion of Restraint: The willingness of a political majority to restrain itself and not crush the minority.
   3. Freedom of Criticism with Absolute Obedience:
4. Grote viewed constitutional morality not as an innate instinct, but as a rare, fragile political culture that societies must actively cultivate over generations.
5. > "Constitutional morality is not a natural sentiment. It has to be cultivated. We must realise that our people have yet to learn it. Democracy in India is only a top-dressing on an Indian soil, which is essentially undemocratic."    - BR Ambedkar
   1. This is why The Constitution of India is so detailed one to avoid it becoming the caprice of the majority

6. Judicial Renaissance: The Delhi High Court in Naz Foundation (2009)
The judicial revival of the doctrine began in Naz Foundation v. Government of NCT of Delhi (2009), delivered by Chief Justice A.P. Shah:

The Conflict: In challenging Section 377 IPC (criminalizing homosexuality), the State argued that Indian society viewed homosexuality as "immoral."

The Formulation: The High Court decoupled "morality" from majoritarian cultural mores:

The Court held that the "morality" referred to in the Constitution is Constitutional Morality, not Popular or Public Morality.

Even if a vast majority of the population disapproves of an individual's private lifestyle, the constitutional promise of dignity, equality, and personal liberty (Articles 14, 15, 21) cannot be overridden by popular prejudice.

#### Judgements
***Manoj Narula v. Union of India (2014)***	CJI R.M. Lodha applied the doctrine to executive governance, observing that constitutional morality demands that <u>persons facing severe criminal trials should not be inducted into the Union Council of Ministers.</u>

***Govt. of NCT of Delhi v. UOI (2018)***
   1. Q.:  case originated from a severe, long-standing institutional conflict over who holds the ultimate executive control over the administration of India's capital city: the Central Government (represented by the Lieutenant Governor) or the elected Delhi Government (headed by the Chief Minister).
   2. The 5-judge bench held that <u>constitutional morality requires institutional self-restraint and cooperative federalism between the Lieutenant Governor and the elected Chief Minister, preventing governance gridlock.</u>

***Navtej Singh Johar v. UOI (2018)***	Decriminalized Section 377 IPC. CJI Dipak Misra affirmed that courts must be guided strictly by constitutional morality, not majoritarian or public morality. The court's duty is to protect vulnerable minority groups against majoritarian bias.

***Indian Young Lawyers Association v. State of Kerala (Sabarimala, 2018)***	Struck down the temple entry ban on women aged 10–50. The majority held that the phrase "morality" in Article 25(1) must mean Constitutional Morality (equality, liberty, and non-discrimination), which overrides traditional, patriarchal religious morality.

***Joseph Shine v. UOI (2018)***
1. What Section 497 Used to Say: 
   1. Definition: It defined adultery as sexual intercourse by a man with another man's wife without the husband's consent or connivance.
   2. Punishment: It carried a prison term of up to five years, a fine, or both.
   3. Exemptions: Women were not prosecuted even as abettors, and only the husband could file a complaint.
2. Why It Was Struck Down: 
   1. In the landmark case Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2018), the Supreme Court declared Section 497 unconstitutional under Supreme Court Observer analysis:
      1. Gender Bias: It treated women as the property of their husbands and only punished men.
         1. Violation of Equality: It violated Articles 14 and 15 of the Constitution by discriminating based on s
         2. Infringement on Autonomy: It violated the right to privacy, dignity, and sexual autonomy under Article 21. 
      2. Current Legal Status: Adultery is not a crime in India anymore.
         1. However, it remains a valid ground for divorce or judicial separation under civil family laws.	
         2. Struck down Section 497 IPC (adultery). The Court ruled that Victorian and patriarchal social conventions subordinating a woman's sexual autonomy violate constitutional morality.

#### Challenges/Concerns 
Subjective and Amorphous: Former Attorney General K.K. Venugopal called it a "dangerous weapon," cautioning that without objective codification, constitutional morality could allow judges to substitute their personal ideological preferences for the text of the Constitution.

Counter-Majoritarian Tension: In her lone dissent in the Sabarimala case (2018), Justice Indu Malhotra cautioned against using constitutional morality to overhaul deep-seated internal religious practices of faith communities, warning it could dilute the freedom of religious denominations under Article 26.

Core Tenets of Justice Indu Malhotra's Dissent
1. Faith Transcends Rationality: Issues of deep religious faith and practice cannot be evaluated through the lens of judicial rationality or secular logic. What may seem irrational, archaic, or discriminatory to an outsider may be central to a believer’s faith.  
2. High Threshold for Judicial Intervention: The judiciary should not adjudicate or strike down internal religious practices unless they constitute pernicious, oppressive social evils (such as Sati or human sacrifice). The custom at Sabarimala, rooted in the deity Lord Ayyappa's manifestation as a Naishtika Brahmachari (eternal celibate), did not qualify as an oppressive social evil.  
3. Protection of Group Autonomy (Article 26): Religious communities and denominations possess the fundamental right under Article 26(b) to manage their internal affairs in matters of religion. Subjecting traditional rituals to individual equality claims under Article 14 renders collective religious autonomy meaningless.  
4. Locus Standi and Dissent from Within: Entertaining a Public Interest Litigation (PIL) filed by third parties (who were not devotees of Lord Ayyappa) sets a perilous precedent. Any challenge to a religious custom must come from actual aggrieved worshippers of that faith, not external entities seeking abstract social reform.  
5. Article 17 Inapplicability: The prohibition of untouchability under Article 17 was historically designed exclusively to eradicate caste-based discrimination and exclusion, and cannot be stretched to cover gender-based ritual prohibitions. 
6. Constitutional Morality Requires Harmonisation, Not Hegemony  
   1. The majority viewed Constitutional Morality as the absolute supremacy of individual equality (Article 14) over religious practice (Articles 25 and 26). Justice Malhotra held that in a deeply diverse, secular nation, Constitutional Morality requires the harmonisation of competing fundamental rights—it obligates the State to protect religious minorities and denominations so they can practice their rituals without judicial interference
7. Risk of Majoritarian or Secular Homogenisation
   1. She cautioned that if courts begin testing every religious belief against uniform secular ideals of equality:

Minority faiths and diverse sectarian traditions will lose protection.

Courts will effectively assume the role of religious arbiters, determining which rituals are "acceptable" and which are not.

The secular character of the Indian Constitution—which protects the freedom to believe and practice even idiosyncratic or non-conformist traditions—will be subverted.

Subjectivity of the Standard

Because "Constitutional Morality" is not explicitly defined in the constitutional text, invoking it to override established religious customs allows subjective judicial sensibilities to replace constitutional boundaries.

1. Meaning and importance
   - It requires public institutions to act in conformity with constitutional values, not narrow political interests.
   - It includes dignity, justice, liberty, equality, secularism, and inclusivity.
   - It is central to preserving democracy in a plural society.
   - This principle has become an interpretive tool in judicial reasoning.

1. Judicial independence and accountability
   - Judges must be independent from executive pressure, but not beyond scrutiny for misconduct.
   - Constitutional morality requires ethics, fairness, and transparency in judicial functioning.
   - Independence is essential for rights protection; accountability must be institutional and legal, not political.
   - This balance ensures judicial legitimacy.

1. Case law and practical application
   - K.S. Puttaswamy recognised privacy as intrinsic to dignity and autonomy.
   - Navtej Singh Johar upheld dignity over majoritarian morality.
   - Sabrimala and triple talaq debates show how constitutional morality clashes with social conservatism.
   - Courts often use the idea to strike a balance between rights and public order.

1. Constitutional anchors
   - Articles 14, 19, 21, 25, and 32 embody constitutional morality in rights-based governance.
   - It also informs legislative and executive action in social policy and public administration.

1. Conclusion
Constitutional morality reinforces justice, dignity, and accountability, making Indian democracy more resilient, inclusive, and rights-based.
