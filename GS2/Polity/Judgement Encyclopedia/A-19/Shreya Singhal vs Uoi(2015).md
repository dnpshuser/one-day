**Section 66A of the Information Technology (IT) Act, 2000** was introduced via the Information Technology (Amendment) Act, 2008. It criminalized the sending of "offensive" messages through computer resources or communication devices, but became infamous for rampant misuse by law enforcement to suppress political dissent, criticism, and online satire.

In a historic judgment in **Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015)**, the Supreme Court of India struck down Section 66A in its entirety as unconstitutional.

---

### 1. What Section 66A Provided

Under Section 66A, any person who sent, by means of a computer resource or a communication device:

* Any information that was **grossly offensive** or had a **menacing character**;
* Any information known to be false, sent for the purpose of causing **annoyance, inconvenience, danger, obstruction, insult, injury, criminal intimidation, enmity, hatred, or ill will**;
* Any electronic mail or electronic mail message intended to deceive or mislead the recipient about the origin of such messages,

could be punished with **imprisonment for up to three years and a fine**.

---

### 2. The Trigger & Context for Judicial Challenge

The provision gained widespread notoriety due to arbitrary arrests of ordinary citizens and activists for innocuous social media posts:

* **The Palghar Arrests (2012):** Two college girls in Maharashtra were arrested under Section 66A—one for merely questioning the shutdown of Mumbai following the death of a political leader on Facebook, and the other for "liking" the post.
* **Political Cartoons & Critique:** Similar arrests occurred across states for circulating political cartoons or posting comments criticizing government officials and ministers.

Law student **Shreya Singhal** and civil liberty groups (like PUCL) filed a Public Interest Litigation (PIL) under Article 32, challenging Section 66A as a violation of **Article 19(1)(a)** (Freedom of Speech and Expression).

---

#### A. The Tripartite Spectrum of Free Speech

The Court drew a clear legal boundary between three stages of speech:

1. **Discussion:** Expressing an opinion or sharing an idea (fully protected under Article 19(1)(a)).
2. **Advocacy:** Persuading others to an unpopular point of view (fully protected under Article 19(1)(a)).
3. **Incitement:** Directly urging immediate unlawful action or violence (can be restricted under Article 19(2)).

The Court ruled that Section 66A criminalized speech even at the stage of mere discussion and advocacy, without requiring any element of **incitement** to clear public disorder.

#### B. The Doctrine of Vagueness and Overbreadth

* **Vagueness:** Terms such as *"grossly offensive"*, *"menacing"*, *"annoyance"*, and *"inconvenience"* were neither defined in the IT Act nor in the Indian Penal Code. What is offensive to one person might be innocuous to another.
* **Overbreadth:** Because the statute lacked clear and objective definitions, it cast a net so wide that innocent, constitutionally protected speech was brought within the zone of criminality.

#### C. The "Chilling Effect" on Free Speech

The Court noted that when penal laws are vague and arbitrarily enforceable, citizens engage in self-censorship out of fear of arrest. This creates an unconstitutional "chilling effect" on public debate and digital expression.

#### D. Beyond the Scope of Article 19(2)

Under Article 19(2), speech can only be curtailed on eight specific, exhaustive grounds: *sovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, or incitement to an offence*.

* Section 66A imported subjective concepts like "annoyance" and "inconvenience," which **do not figure anywhere in Article 19(2)**.
* There was no proximate relationship or clear nexus between an "annoying" post and a breach of "public order."

#### E. Rejection of the "Good Administration" Defense

The Government argued that Section 66A would not be misused and that administrative guidelines would prevent frivolous arrests. The Supreme Court firmly rejected this argument, holding that **the constitutionality of an enactment must be judged on the provisions of the statute itself, not on assurances of benevolent executive administration**.

---

### 4. Significance and Aftermath (The "Zombie Law" Issue)

* **Victory for Digital Liberty:** The ruling established that constitutional rights under Article 19(1)(a) apply with equal force in digital and physical spaces.
* **The "Zombie Section 66A":** Despite being struck down in 2015, police stations across several states continued to register FIRs under Section 66A due to a lack of institutional communication. This forced the Supreme Court (in *PUCL v. Union of India, 2021–2022*) to issue strict administrative directives ordering states, High Courts, and police chiefs to immediately drop all pending Section 66A proceedings and scrub the defunct provision from police registers.
