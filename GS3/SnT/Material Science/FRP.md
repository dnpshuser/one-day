1. What is this ?
   - Fibre-Reinforced Polymer (FRP) is a composite material made of high-strength reinforcing fibres (like carbon, glass, or aramid) embedded in a polymer matrix (like epoxy or polyester), combining the strength of the fibres with the protective and load-transferring properties of the matrix.

2. How manufactured ?
    - FRP is manufactured by combining **reinforcing fibres** with a **polymer resin (matrix)**. The fibres provide strength, while the resin holds them in position, transfers loads between them, and protects them from moisture and chemicals.
    - The broad manufacturing sequence is:
       1. **Design and fibre orientation:** The required shape and loads are studied. Fibres are placed along the directions in which maximum strength is needed. Several layers may be oriented differently because FRP is stronger along the fibre direction.
       2. **Preparation of mould:** A mould of the required shape is cleaned and coated with a release agent so that the finished component can be removed easily.
       3. **Fibre placement:** Glass, carbon, or aramid fibres are placed as continuous strands, woven fabric, or pre-formed sheets. The quantity and arrangement of fibres determine the final strength and stiffness.
       4. **Resin impregnation:** Liquid resin is applied or injected so that it thoroughly wets and surrounds the fibres. Proper wetting is important because dry gaps can create weak points.
       5. **Compaction and air removal:** Pressure, rollers, vacuum bags, or an injection process remove trapped air and excess resin. This reduces voids and prevents delamination (separation between layers).
       6. **Curing:** The resin is allowed to harden through a chemical reaction. Heat or pressure may be used to speed up curing and improve the quality of the component. Thermoset resins cannot be remelted after this stage.
       7. **Finishing and quality control:** The component is removed from the mould, trimmed, drilled, and surface-finished. It is then checked for voids, cracks, uneven fibre distribution, or delamination.
    - Common manufacturing methods include:
       - **Hand lay-up:** Fibre sheets are placed in a mould, resin is brushed or rolled over them, and the layers are compacted. It is simple and economical, but labour-intensive and less uniform.
       - **Vacuum-assisted resin infusion:** Dry fibres are sealed under a vacuum bag and resin is drawn through them. Vacuum removes air and gives better fibre-to-resin distribution, making it suitable for large parts such as boat hulls and wind-turbine blades.
       - **Compression moulding:** A prepared fibre-resin mixture or pre-form is placed in a heated mould and pressed. It gives faster, more uniform production of automobile panels and other medium-sized components.
       - **Pultrusion:** Continuous fibres pass through a resin bath and then a heated die, which gives them a fixed shape and cures the resin. It is used for constant-profile products such as rods, beams, and corrosion-resistant rebar.
       - **Filament winding:** Resin-coated continuous fibres are wound at controlled angles around a rotating mandrel. After curing, the mandrel is removed to produce hollow, strong structures such as pipes, tanks, and pressure vessels.
       - **Prepreg and autoclave process:** Fibres pre-impregnated with a measured amount of resin are laid in the mould and cured under controlled heat and pressure in an autoclave. This gives high-quality, low-void components, especially for aircraft, but is expensive.
    - Thus, the choice of method depends on the **shape, size, required strength, production volume, cost, and surface finish** of the product.

3. Application
   1. **Aviation:** Used extensively for fuselages, wings, and tail sections (e.g., Boeing 787, Airbus A350) to reduce weight, improve fuel efficiency, and resist fatigue.
   2. **Automobile industry:** For lightweighting vehicles to improve fuel economy and performance, especially in electric vehicles (to offset battery weight) and high-performance cars (chassis, body panels).
   3. **other application:**
      - **Civil Engineering:** Strengthening bridges and buildings, corrosion-free rebar.
      - **Wind Energy:** Manufacturing long, strong, and lightweight turbine blades.
      - **Sporting Goods:** High-performance equipment like bicycle frames, tennis rackets, and skis.
      - **Marine:** Building corrosion-resistant boat hulls and superstructures.

4. Advantages
   - **High Strength-to-Weight Ratio:** Offers strength comparable to steel at a fraction of the weight.
   - **Corrosion Resistance:** Excellent resistance to rust and chemical attack, leading to a long service life.
   - **Design Flexibility:** Can be molded into complex aerodynamic or ergonomic shapes.
   - **Durability:** High fatigue resistance and low maintenance requirements.

5. Disadvantages
   - **High Cost:** Raw materials (especially carbon fibre) and manufacturing are more expensive than traditional metals.
   - **Difficult to Repair:** Damage (like delamination) can be hard to detect and requires specialized techniques for repair.
   - **Recycling Challenges:** Most FRPs use thermoset polymers, which are difficult to recycle, posing environmental concerns.
   - **Anisotropic Nature:** Properties are directional, meaning strength is highest along the fibre orientation, which complicates design and analysis.

6. Conclusion
   - FRP composites are advanced materials driving innovation across high-performance sectors by enabling the creation of lighter, stronger, and more durable products. Their superior properties are critical for advancing fuel efficiency, renewable energy, and resilient infrastructure.
