LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) is an active remote sensing technology that uses pulsed laser light to measure variable distances and build high-resolution, three-dimensional maps.


How It Works

• Laser Emission: The system sends out rapid pulses of laser light (typically in ultraviolet, visible, or near-infrared wavelengths) toward a target surface. 
• Time-of-Flight: It measures the exact time it takes for each pulse to bounce off an object and return to the sensor. 
• Distance Calculation: Using the known speed of light and travel time, the system computes the distance to the surface (\(Distance = \frac{\text{Speed of Light} \times \text{Time}}{2}\)). 
• Positioning: Combined with a GPS/GNSS receiver and an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU), these millions of measurements create a dense 3D representation called a point cloud. 

Key Components

• Laser Scanner & Emitter: Generates and directs the light pulses.

• GPS / GNSS: Tracks the exact geographic location and altitude of the platform. 
• IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit): Measures the orientation, pitch, roll, and yaw of the moving sensor. 

Main Types of LiDAR

• Topographic LiDAR: Uses near-infrared lasers to map land surfaces, buildings, and vegetation.
• Bathymetric LiDAR: Uses water-penetrating green light to measure the depths of seafloors and riverbeds.
• Airborne vs. Terrestrial: Systems can be mounted on aircraft, drones, mobile vehicles, or fixed ground tripods, or even operate via space-based satellites. 

Common Applications

• Autonomous Vehicles & Robotics: Helps self-driving cars perceive obstacles, pedestrians, and road geometry in real time.
• Forestry & Ecology: Penetrates tree canopies to measure vegetation height, density, and ground topography.
• Archaeology: Uncovers hidden ruins and ancient land structures beneath dense foliage.
• Urban Planning & Infrastructure: Models cities, manages power lines, and assesses flood risks or coastal erosion.
