# Basic Structure Doctrine & Article 368

# Content 1
## 1. Intro

* Article 368 of the Indian Constitution grants Parliament the constituent power to amend the Constitution by way of addition, variation, or repeal.
* However, the **Basic Structure Doctrine** is a judicial innovation establishing that Parliament's amending power is not absolute; it is subject to an "implied limitation." Parliament cannot use Article 368 to destroy, alter, or abrogate the fundamental, foundational core (the "Basic Structure") of the Constitution.
* This delicate equilibrium ensures that while the Constitution remains flexible enough to adapt to changing societal needs, its democratic and secular soul cannot be hijacked by a transient political majority.


## 2. Features/Qualities

* **Unwritten & Judicial Origin:** The term "Basic Structure" is not explicitly written anywhere in the 395 Articles of the original constitutional text; it is purely a jurisprudential creation by the Supreme Court.
* **Implied Limitation:** It acts as a substantive, invisible boundary on legislative power, establishing the legal philosophy that the "power to amend" does not equate to the "power to destroy."
* **Dynamic and Case-by-Case Determination:** The Supreme Court has deliberately refused to provide a rigid, exhaustive list of basic features. What constitutes the basic structure is determined dynamically on a case-by-case basis based on the constitutional text and history.
* **The 9th Schedule Cut-off (Waman Rao & I.R. Coelho):** The doctrine applies prospectively regarding the 9th Schedule. Any law placed in the 9th Schedule *before* April 24, 1973 (the date of the Kesavananda verdict) is completely immune from basic structure scrutiny. However, any law placed in it *after* this exact date is fully subject to judicial review if it violates the basic structure.
* **Supremacy of the Constitution:** It legally solidifies that Parliament is merely a creature of the Constitution, not its master.
* **Protects Constitutional Identity:** It ensures that the essential identity of the Republic—its democratic, secular, federal, and republican nature—remains immortal regardless of the political climate or absolute parliamentary majorities.

## 3. Sub-components of the topic [most imp]

* **Article 368 (Power vs. Procedure):** Before 1971, Article 368 only mentioned the "procedure" for amendment. The 24th Amendment explicitly added that Parliament has the substantive "power" to amend any provision, including Fundamental Rights.
* **The Article 13(2) Tussle:** Article 13 states the State shall make no "law" that abridges Fundamental Rights. The core legal battle for decades was whether a constitutional amendment under Article 368 qualifies as a "law" under Article 13.
* **The "Essence of the Constitution" Test:** Established in the *Minerva Mills (1980)* case, ensuring that the delicate balance and harmony between Fundamental Rights (Part III) and Directive Principles (Part IV) is maintained as a core basic feature.
* **Judicial Review (Article 32 & 226):** Declared as an inviolable part of the basic structure in the *L. Chandra Kumar (1997)* case, preventing the legislature from creating administrative tribunals that completely bypass the jurisdiction of High Courts and the Supreme Court.
* **The "Golden Triangle":** Articles 14 (Equality), 19 (Freedoms), and 21 (Life and Liberty) are fiercely protected by the judiciary as the structural, non-negotiable pillars of fundamental human dignity.
* **Secularism & Federalism:** Reaffirmed as basic features in the *S.R. Bommai (1994)* case, severely limiting the Union's power to arbitrarily dismiss state governments under Article 356.
* **Free and Fair Elections:** Recognized as a basic feature in cases like *Kihoto Hollohan (1992)* and *Indira Nehru Gandhi (1975)*, protecting the sanctity of the democratic mandate against executive manipulation.

## 4. Evolution timeline

* 1951: Shankari Prasad Case
The Supreme Court ruled that Parliament's amending power under Article 368 is absolute and it can amend Fundamental Rights to facilitate agrarian reforms.


* 1967: Golaknath Case
An 11-judge bench reversed the 1951 stance, ruling that Fundamental Rights are "transcendental and immutable" and Parliament cannot amend them at all.


* 1971: 24th Constitutional Amendment
Parliament retaliated to Golaknath by amending Articles 13 and 368, explicitly asserting its absolute power to amend any part of the Constitution.


* April 24, 1973: Kesavananda Bharati Verdict
A 13-judge bench upheld the 24th Amendment but birthed the "Basic Structure Doctrine," ruling that Parliament cannot alter the foundational core of the Constitution.


* 1976: 42nd Constitutional Amendment
The Executive attempted to kill the doctrine by adding clauses (4) and (5) to Article 368, declaring there shall be no limitation whatsoever on Parliament's amending power.


* 1980: Minerva Mills Case
The Supreme Court struck down clauses (4) and (5) of Article 368, reaffirming that Parliament's "limited amending power" is itself a part of the basic structure.


* 1981 & 2007: Waman Rao & I.R. Coelho Cases
The SC definitively ruled that laws placed in the 9th Schedule on or after April 24, 1973, are not immune from judicial review if they violate the basic structure.

## 7. Committee or reports (year)

* **National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (NCRWC, 2002):** Headed by Justice M.N. Venkatachaliah, it explicitly supported the Basic Structure Doctrine, stating it has successfully stabilized the constitutional framework and saved India from authoritarianism.
* **Swaran Singh Committee (1976):** A historically massive impact committee formed during the Emergency. It recommended explicitly overriding the Basic Structure Doctrine, directly leading to the draconian 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act (which was later struck down in *Minerva Mills*).
* **Sarkaria Commission on Centre-State Relations (1988):** Heavily relied on the Basic Structure Doctrine's interpretation of "Federalism" to recommend stringent limits on the Union's power to dismiss state governments under Article 356.
* **Punchhi Commission (2010):** Reasserted that cooperative federalism and the autonomy of states are shielded by the basic structure, urging the Centre to respect this constitutional boundary during law-making and concurrent list legislation.
* **Law Commission 255th Report (2015):** While analyzing electoral and judicial reforms, it deeply emphasized that the separation of powers and judicial independence are non-negotiable core components of India's constitutional identity.
* **Second Administrative Reforms Commission (2nd ARC, 2008):** In its reports on governance and ethics, it highlighted that the basic structure doctrine ensures that the foundational ethos of civil rights cannot be legislated away by a transient executive mandate.

## 9. Global comparison

* **Germany (The "Eternity Clause"):** Article 79(3) of the German Basic Law explicitly prohibits any constitutional amendments that affect human dignity, the republican democratic system, or federalism. Germany uses a *textual, written* basic structure, whereas India's is an *unwritten, judicial* innovation.
* **United Kingdom (Parliamentary Sovereignty):** The UK has no written constitution and strictly follows absolute Parliamentary Sovereignty. Parliament can pass or repeal any law with a simple majority. The courts cannot strike down primary legislation, meaning there is absolutely no equivalent to the basic structure doctrine.
* **United States (Article V Rigidity):** The US Constitution is extremely difficult to amend (requires 2/3rds of Congress and 3/4ths of States). However, it has no judicial "basic structure" limit. Any amendment is valid, except for one explicitly written rule: no state can be deprived of its equal suffrage in the Senate without its consent.
* **Bangladesh (Doctrine Adoption):** In the landmark *Anwar Hossain Chowdhury case (1989)*, the Supreme Court of Bangladesh actively imported and applied the Indian Basic Structure Doctrine to strike down a constitutional amendment altering the High Court's structure.
* **South Africa (Tiered Supermajorities):** The South African Constitutional Court reviews amendments but relies on explicit founding values rather than an unwritten basic structure. Core constitutional values (like non-racialism and human dignity) require an exceptionally high 75% parliamentary supermajority to amend, compared to 66% for standard provisions.
* **Kenya (Referendum Threshold):** The 2010 Kenyan Constitution explicitly delineates which parts can be amended by parliament and which core features (like the Bill of Rights or the independence of the Judiciary) require a mandatory national referendum, effectively codifying a democratic basic structure.

## 11. Flow chart

**The Article 368 Amendment & Judicial Review Lifecycle**

1. **Initiation:** A Constitution Amendment Bill is introduced in either House of Parliament.
2. **Passage (Article 368):** Requires a Special Majority in both Houses (plus ratification by 50% of State Legislatures if it alters federal features).
3. **Presidential Assent:** The President gives mandatory assent (as bound by the 24th CAA); the Bill becomes an Act.
4. **Judicial Challenge:** Citizens/Entities file a writ petition in the Supreme Court under Article 32.
5. **The Basic Structure Test:** The Supreme Court evaluates the amendment's impact on the constitutional identity.
6. **Verdict Outcome A:** Does NOT alter core identity -> Amendment is **Upheld** (e.g., 103rd CAA - EWS Quota).
7. **Verdict Outcome B:** Destroys core identity -> Amendment is **Struck Down** as ultra vires (e.g., 99th CAA - NJAC).

* **Places of Worship Act Challenges:** Ongoing legal battles where petitioners want to challenge the 1991 Act, while defenders argue the Act is insulated because it directly operationalizes "Secularism," a core pillar of the basic structure.

## 13. Advantage/Merits/Role

* **Prevents Totalitarianism:** It acts as the ultimate safeguard preventing a ruling party with a brute supermajority from legally converting India into a one-party state, a dictatorship, or a theocracy.
* **Protects Constitutional Supremacy:** It firmly establishes that the Constitution is the supreme law of the land, and Parliament is merely a creature of the Constitution, not its master.
* **Saves Democratic Institutions:** It has successfully preserved the independence of the judiciary, the Election Commission, and the federal structure against aggressive centralization attempts during turbulent political eras.
* **Dynamic and Evolving:** Because it is unwritten, the Supreme Court can dynamically expand the basic structure to protect emerging rights and principles as society modernizes, keeping the Constitution a living document.
* **Minority Protection:** It ensures that the fundamental cultural and religious rights of minorities cannot be voted out of existence by a majoritarian parliament.
* **Global Jurisprudential Export:** It is widely considered India's greatest contribution to global constitutional law, having been studied, adapted, or adopted by courts in several other developing democracies.

## 14. Disadvantage/De-Merits

* **Tyranny of the Unelected:** The most severe critique is that it allows unelected, unaccountable judges to veto the collective, democratic will of 1.4 billion people represented through Parliament (Judicial Overreach).
* **Extreme Subjectivity (The Vague Core):** Because there is no written, exhaustive list of basic features, the validity of a constitutional amendment depends entirely on the subjective philosophical leanings of the specific judges on the bench at that time.
* **Extra-Constitutional Origin:** Critics argue it is legally illegitimate because the phrase "Basic Structure" does not appear anywhere in the 395 Articles of the original constitutional text; it is purely a judicial invention.
* **Democratic Friction & Policy Paralysis:** It leads to continuous, exhausting tussles between the legislature and the judiciary, severely slowing down critical institutional reforms (as seen with the decades-long failure to reform judicial appointments).
* **Blurs Interpretation vs. Drafting:** It blurs the separation of powers. By dictating what Parliament *cannot* amend, the judiciary effectively acts as a permanent, super-constituent assembly.
* **Inconsistent Application:** The Court's reluctance to apply the doctrine consistently (e.g., heavily invoking it for judicial appointments but occasionally avoiding it in certain federalism disputes) leads to accusations of judicial hypocrisy.

## 15. Challenges

* **Defining the Boundaries:** The Supreme Court faces the continuous challenge of resisting the temptation to classify every important right or popular policy as a "basic feature," which would dilute the doctrine's extreme-emergency nature.
* **Navigating the 9th Schedule (Post-I.R. Coelho):** Interpreting exactly *which* laws placed in the 9th Schedule after 1973 violate the basic structure remains a highly complex, litigious quagmire, burdening the dockets.
* **Majoritarian Pushback:** When the Executive commands a massive electoral mandate, it naturally views the basic structure doctrine as an anti-democratic obstacle, leading to institutional brinkmanship and the deliberate stalling of judicial appointments to pressure the Court.
* **Erosion by Statutory Means:** Parliaments can bypass amending the Constitution entirely by passing standard statutes (like draconian anti-terror laws or opaque electoral finance schemes) that quietly chip away at basic features without triggering an Article 368 review.
* **Politicization of the Doctrine:** Political parties opportunistically attack the doctrine as "judicial tyranny" when in power, but desperately invoke it as the "savior of democracy" the moment they sit in the opposition.

## 16. Way forward / solution / positive outlook

* **Codification of Core Principles (NCRWC Recommendation):** While a rigid list is ill-advised, the Supreme Court should outline a tighter, more precise jurisprudential framework to ensure the doctrine is applied uniformly, curbing arbitrary judicial vetoes.
* **Institutional Dialogue Over Confrontation:** The Judiciary and the Executive must engage in a mature "Constitutional Dialogue," recognizing that both derive their legitimacy from the same document and must operate with mutual institutional respect rather than hostility.
* **Strict Application to 'Identity' Only:** The Supreme Court must exercise extreme self-restraint, invoking the doctrine *strictly* when the existential identity of the Constitution is threatened, not for mere disagreements over legislative policy or administrative structures.
* **Pre-Legislative Scrutiny:** Parliament should empower its Departmentally Related Standing Committees (DRSCs) to independently vet proposed constitutional amendments against the Basic Structure doctrine before passage, saving immense judicial time and institutional embarrassment.
* **Public Constitutional Literacy:** The true guardian of the basic structure is not the Supreme Court, but an informed citizenry. Civic education must focus on constitutional morality so that voters naturally reject political mandates aimed at destroying democratic institutions.

## 17. Best practices

* **Germany (Textual Clarity):** Germany's "Eternity Clause" solves the subjectivity problem by explicitly writing the unamendable features (human dignity, federalism) into the text of the Constitution, preventing judicial guesswork.
* **Colombia (Doctrine of Constitutional Substitution):** The Colombian Constitutional Court uses a similar doctrine to check executive overreach. They successfully used it to strike down a constitutional amendment that would have allowed a popular President to seek a third term, perfectly preserving democratic rotation.
* **South Africa (Public Participation Thresholds):** Before amending core constitutional values, South African law mandates extensive, formalized public participation and massive supermajorities, ensuring the "basic structure" is protected by the people, not just the courts.
* **India (The Minerva Mills Balance):** The Indian Supreme Court's own precedent in *Minerva Mills* is a best practice in judicial restraint—striking down absolute parliamentary power while refusing to elevate Directive Principles over Fundamental Rights, perfectly maintaining the constitutional scales.
* **Kenya (Referendum Threshold):** The Kenyan Constitution explicitly delineates which parts can be amended by parliament and which core features require a mandatory national referendum, placing the ultimate power of basic structure alteration in the hands of the voting public.

## 18. Conclusion

* The Basic Structure Doctrine elegantly synthesizes the tension between democratic flexibility and constitutional rigidity, ensuring that Parliament's power to *adapt* the Constitution is never weaponized to *destroy* it.
* While critiques of judicial overreach hold merit, the doctrine remains India's ultimate institutional bulwark. It guarantees that regardless of shifting political tides or massive electoral majorities, the soul of the Indian Republic—its democracy, secularism, and rule of law—remains immortal.

The debate surrounding the Basic Structure Doctrine and Article 368 has re-entered the constitutional center-stage due to several institutional friction points:

* **Executive & Legislative Pushback on Judicial Supremacy:** High constitutional functionaries, including the Vice-President of India, have publicly questioned the doctrine, arguing that striking down constitutional amendments passed by elected representatives dilutes parliamentary sovereignty and distorts the separation of powers.
* **The Post-50-Year Assessment of *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973–2023):** Half a century after its inception, legal scholars and courts are evaluating whether the doctrine has served as an indispensable bulwark against executive aggrandizement or evolved into a vehicle for "judicial overreach" and the creation of an unelected "third chamber."
* **Substantive Review of Contemporary Amendments:** The invalidation of the 99th Constitutional Amendment Act (NJAC) in 2015, the reading down of the 97th Amendment (cooperatives) on federal grounds in *Union of India v. Rajendra N. #### A. The Constitutional Provisions

* **Article 368(1):** Grants Parliament the *constituent power* to amend by way of addition, variation, or repeal any provision of the Constitution in accordance with the procedure laid down therein.
* **Article 13(2):** Prohibits the State from making any "law" that takes away or abridges Fundamental Rights.
* **Article 13(4) & Article 368(3):** Introduced via the 24th Amendment Act (1971) to insulate Article 368 from Article 13(2).

#### B. Theoretical Anchor: The Distinction Between Constituent & Amending Power

* **Original Constituent Power:** Belonged solely to the Constituent Assembly that drafted the Constitution.
* **Derivative / Amending Constituent Power:** Conferred upon Parliament via Article 368. Because Parliament is a *creature* of the Constitution, its amending power is inherently derivative, secondary, and bounded. It cannot replace or abrogate the founding identity of the parent document.

### 3. Core Dimensions & Applied Frictions

#### A. Constitutional Supremacy vs. Parliamentary Sovereignty

* Unlike the United Kingdom, which functions on A.V. Dicey’s model of absolute Parliamentary Sovereignty, India adopted **Constitutional Supremacy**.
* Parliament is not sovereign; the Constitution is supreme. As held in *Minerva Mills v. Union of India* (1980), Parliament’s power to amend is a *limited power*, and **a limited power cannot be enlarged into an absolute power by the donee of that power**.

#### C. Substantive vs. Procedural Review

Article 368 provides the *procedure* for amendment (special majority, federal ratification where applicable). The Basic Structure doctrine adds a layer of *substantive review*: an amendment can be procedurally flawless yet unconstitutional if its substantive effect damages a fundamental facet of the constitutional scheme.

### 4. Structural Bottlenecks & Counter-Perspectives

* **Democratic Deficit ("Counter-Majoritarian Difficulty"):** Unelected judges have the power to veto the unanimous will of a bicameral Parliament representing the collective franchise of over 1.4 billion citizens.
* **Doctrinal Vagueness & Subjectivity:** The Supreme Court has never provided an exhaustive, definitive list of what constitutes the "basic structure." This indeterminacy leads to charges of **judicial solipsism**—where the constitutionality of a law depends on the judicial philosophy of individual benches.
* **Risk of Constitutional Fossilization:** Thomas Jefferson cautioned that laws and institutions must go hand in hand with the progress of the human mind. An overly rigid application of the doctrine risks paralyzing constitutional adaptations required to handle emergent socio-economic challenges, structural administrative reorganizations, and technological disruption.
* **Inconsistent Thresholds:** While the 99th Amendment (NJAC) was struck down as violative of judicial independence, the 103rd Amendment (10% EWS quota)—which breached the 50% reservation ceiling laid down in *Indra Sawhney*—was upheld (3:2), revealing internal judicial divergence in applying the doctrine.

---

### 5. Reforms & Forward-Looking Roadmap

* **Adoption of the "Identity Test" over Mere Subjective Assessment:** As proposed by legal scholar M.K. Nambyar and articulated by Justice Khanna in *Kesavananda*, the test should be whether the amendment **destroys or alters the fundamental identity** of the Constitution, rather than merely altering individual provisions.
* **Institutional Comity & "Constitutional Dialogue":** The relationship between the Supreme Court and Parliament must transition from judicial unilateralism to a *constitutional dialogue model* (practiced in Canada and South Africa), where judicial invalidation is followed by legislative recalibration, rather than confrontation.
* **Higher Bench Thresholds for Basic Structure Review:** Currently, a Constitution Bench of 5 judges can strike down a constitutional amendment by a 3:2 majority. There should be a statutory or internal judicial rule mandating a **minimum bench strength of 9 or 11 judges** and requiring a **two-thirds super-majority** within the bench to strike down an amendment to the Constitution.
* **Comparative Learning from "Eternity Clauses":** India could formalize the doctrine’s boundaries by studying models like **Article 79(3) of Germany’s Basic Law**, which explicitly enumerates unamendable provisions (human dignity, federal principles, democracy), reducing interpretive uncertainty.
