## 1. Intro

* Part XVIII of the Indian Constitution (Articles 352–360) contains the Emergency provisions, designed to safeguard the sovereignty, security, unity, and integrity of the nation, as well as its democratic political system and the Constitution itself.
* These provisions empower the Central Government to handle extraordinary situations by transforming the federal structure into a unitary one without any formal constitutional amendment.

## 2. Sub-components of the topic [most imp]

* **National Emergency (Article 352):** Can be declared by the President when the security of India or a part of it is threatened by war, external aggression, or armed rebellion. The 44th Amendment Act (1978) replaced the vague term 'internal disturbance' with 'armed rebellion'.
* **President's Rule / State Emergency (Article 356):** Imposed when the constitutional machinery in a state fails, meaning the state government cannot carry on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution. Article 365 acts as an enforcer if a state fails to comply with Union directions.
* **Financial Emergency (Article 360):** Proclaimed if the financial stability or credit of India, or any part of its territory, is threatened. (This has never been invoked).
* **Parliamentary Approval & Duration:** A National Emergency needs approval by a special majority within one month. President's Rule needs a simple majority within two months.
* **Effect on Fundamental Rights:** Article 358 automatically suspends the six fundamental rights under Article 19 during a National Emergency (only if based on war or external aggression). Article 359 allows the President to suspend the right to move courts for the enforcement of other specified fundamental rights (except Articles 20 and 21).
* **Effect on Centre-State Relations:** The Union gains sweeping powers; Parliament can legislate on State List subjects, and the Centre can direct state executive actions and alter financial distribution.

## 3. Evolution timeline

* 1962 (Indo-China War): Declared from October 1962 to January 1968 due to external aggression during the war with China.

* 1971 (Indo-Pakistani War): Declared in December 1971 during the war with Pakistan.

* 1975 (Internal Disturbances): Declared in June 1975 and lifted in March 1977 under controversial circumstances during Prime Minister Indira Gandhi's tenure

* June 25, 1975: Declaration of the Emergency
Following the Allahabad High Court verdict invalidating PM Indira Gandhi's election, a National Emergency was declared under Article 352 on the grounds of "internal disturbance." This led to mass arrests and press censorship.


* 1975: 38th Constitutional Amendment Act
Made the declaration of a National Emergency immune to judicial review, severely restricting the courts' ability to question the grounds of the proclamation.


* April 1976: ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla (Habeas Corpus Case)
The Supreme Court ruled (4:1) that individuals could not seek judicial review (Habeas Corpus) when Article 21 (Right to Life and Personal Liberty) was suspended during the Emergency. Justice H.R. Khanna's solitary dissent argued that liberty could not be suspended without legal recourse.


* March 1977: End of Emergency
The Emergency was revoked, leading to general elections and the formation of the first non-Congress government at the Centre.


* 1978: 44th Constitutional Amendment Act
Introduced crucial safeguards: "internal disturbance" was replaced by "armed rebellion," mandatory written advice from the Cabinet was required, and Articles 20 and 21 were shielded from suspension during an emergency. It also reversed the 38th Amendment, restoring judicial review.


* 1980: Minerva Mills Case
The Supreme Court explicitly held that a proclamation of National Emergency can be challenged in court on the grounds of being malafide or based on extraneous facts.


* 1994: S.R. Bommai v. Union of India
A landmark judgment that established stringent guidelines for the imposition of President's Rule under Article 356, making it subject to judicial review and protecting the federal structure from arbitrary central intervention.


## 4. Quotation

* **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar:** "I do not altogether deny that there is a possibility of the Articles (relating to State Emergency) being abused or employed for political purposes... I hope the proper thing we ought to expect is that such articles will never be called into operation and that they would remain a dead letter."
* **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel:** "If we are to have a strong centre, it must have powers to deal with emergencies." (Highlighting the pragmatic necessity of these provisions for national integration).
* **Chanakya (Arthashastra):** "In the happiness of his subjects lies the king's happiness; in their welfare his welfare. He shall not consider as good only that which pleases him but treat as beneficial to him whatever pleases his subjects." (Relevance: ***Emergency powers should be a shield for the people, not a weapon against them***).
* **Mahabharata (Shanti Parva):** "A king should protect his subjects as a pregnant woman protects the child in her womb." (Emphasizing that exceptional powers must be used solely for the protection of the state and its people during crises).


## 5. Reports

* **Shah Commission Report (1978):** Appointed to investigate the excesses committed during the 1975-77 Emergency. It highlighted arbitrary detentions, misuse of the Maintenance of Internal Security Act (MISA), and the subversion of institutional norms.
* **Sarkaria Commission Report (1988):** Recommended that Article 356 (President's Rule) should be used very sparingly, as a matter of "last resort," and only after exhausting all other options under Article 355 (giving warnings to the state).
* **Punchhi Commission Report (2010):** Recommended the concept of a "Local Emergency" to isolate specific disturbed districts rather than taking over the entire state machinery under Article 356, aiming to preserve federal equilibrium.
* **National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (NCRWC, 2002):** Re-emphasized the Bommai judgment's guidelines regarding Article 356 and stressed the need for institutional safeguards against the misuse of emergency powers.

## 6. Indices

## 7. Global comparison

* **United States:** The US Constitution does not have an equivalent to India's Article 356 (State Emergency). The federal government cannot take over state administration. National emergencies are declared under the National Emergencies Act, but Congress has significant oversight and the power to terminate them.
* **Germany (Weimar Republic vs. Basic Law):** The Weimar Constitution's Article 48 allowed the President emergency powers that were frequently misused, aiding the rise of the Third Reich. Modern Germany's Basic Law has highly restricted emergency provisions, ensuring parliamentary supremacy and judicial oversight.
* **United Kingdom:** Emergency powers are statutory (e.g., Civil Contingencies Act 2004) rather than explicitly constitutional. They require immediate parliamentary scrutiny and have strict time limits.
* **South Africa:** The Constitution allows for a State of Emergency only when the life of the nation is threatened by war, invasion, or general insurrection. The declaration is subject to parliamentary approval and strict judicial review, with certain fundamental rights being entirely non-derogable.

## 8. Committee reports

* **Administrative Reforms Commission (ARC):** While not exclusively focused on emergencies, the ARC has consistently recommended strengthening Centre-State relations to prevent situations that necessitate the use of Article 356.
* **Committee on State Autonomy (Rajamannar Committee, 1971):** Appointed by the Tamil Nadu government, it drastically recommended the deletion of Articles 356, 357, and 360, arguing they were antithetical to federalism.
* **Law Commission of India:** Various reports have touched upon the necessity of balancing national security needs with fundamental rights, particularly concerning preventive detention laws often used during emergencies.

## 10. Flow chart

**The Process of Imposing President's Rule (Article 356)**

1. **Trigger:** Governor's Report indicating a breakdown of constitutional machinery OR President's independent satisfaction (Art 365 - failure to comply with Union directions).
2. **Proclamation:** President issues the proclamation imposing Article 356. State Council of Ministers is dismissed.
3. **Administration:** Governor assumes executive power on behalf of the President.
4. **Parliamentary Approval:** The proclamation must be laid before both Houses of Parliament.
5. **Vote:** Must be approved by a simple majority in both Houses within 2 months.
6. **Extension:** If approved, it lasts for 6 months. Can be extended up to a maximum of 3 years (with ECI certification required beyond 1 year).
7. **Legislative Action:** Parliament passes the state budget and assumes the power to legislate on State List subjects.

## 11. Recent context/issues/debate

* **The 50th Anniversary of the 1975 Emergency:** Recent debates marking the 50th year since the 1975 Emergency have refocused attention on institutional vulnerability. Critics highlight how the Supreme Court's failure in the ADM Jabalpur case remains a cautionary tale about judicial abdication during executive overreach.
* **Misuse of Article 356:** While the Bommai judgment significantly curbed the arbitrary dismissal of state governments, political controversies still arise (e.g., recent political crises in Maharashtra and Uttarakhand) regarding the Governor's role in determining floor tests and recommending President's Rule.
* **"Undeclared Emergency" Allegations:** Opposition parties frequently use the term "undeclared emergency" to describe the alleged misuse of central investigative agencies (CBI, ED) and preventive detention laws (UAPA) against political opponents and dissenters, arguing that the *effect* of an emergency can be achieved without a formal constitutional proclamation.
* **The Punchhi Commission's "Local Emergency":** There is ongoing academic debate on whether Article 355 and 356 should be amended to allow for localized interventions in specific disturbed areas, preserving the elected state government while addressing regional crises.

## 12. Conclusion

* The Emergency provisions, as envisioned by Dr. Ambedkar, were meant to be a constitutional safety valve, a "dead letter" to be used only as an absolute last resort to protect the republic.
* The dark period of 1975 demonstrated the severe consequences of their misuse. While the 44th Amendment and the Bommai judgment have provided robust safeguards, continuous vigilance by an independent judiciary and an active civil society remains essential to prevent the subversion of India's democratic federalism.

# Emergency Provisions & Federal Integrity: Art. 356 vs. DMA

## 1. Intro

* **Constitutional Emergency Architecture** was originally designed as a drastic, extreme safeguard to protect national integrity, with Article 356 acting as the ultimate weapon against state-level constitutional machinery failure.
* **Statutory Centralism** has recently emerged as a sophisticated backdoor to unitary control, where laws like the Disaster Management Act (DMA) allow the Union to bypass the strict judicial guardrails placed on Article 356 by the Supreme Court.

## 2. Features/Qualities

* **Article 356 (President's Rule)** allows the Union executive to completely dismiss an elected state government and suspend the state legislature upon the subjective satisfaction of the President.
* **Judicial Reviewability** ensures that the imposition of President's Rule is no longer an absolute executive privilege; courts can examine the material evidence justifying the emergency.
* **Disaster Management Act 2005 (DMA)** establishes a highly centralized, top-down bureaucratic hierarchy (NDMA -> SDMA -> DDMA) to manage national crises.
* **Overriding Statutory Authority** is granted to the Union via Section 72 of the DMA, explicitly stating that its provisions shall have an overriding effect over any other law in force.
* **Administrative Emergency Equivalency** occurs when the DMA empowers the Union Home Ministry to issue binding directives to State Chief Secretaries, functionally overriding state autonomy without declaring a formal constitutional emergency.

## 3. Sub components of the topic [most imp]

* **The S.R. Bommai Safeguards (1994)** legally cemented that 'Federalism' and 'Secularism' are basic features, making the arbitrary dismissal of state governments constitutionally invalid.
* **Mandatory Floor Test** was established as the singular, objective forum to determine a state government's majority, stripping the Governor of discretionary subjective dismissal powers.
* **Status Quo Ante** empowers the Supreme Court to resurrect a dismissed state government and dissolved assembly if the presidential proclamation is found unconstitutional.
* **Section 6 of the DMA** grants the Prime Minister-led National Authority (NDMA) the sweeping power to lay down binding policies and guidelines for state authorities.
* **Epidemic Diseases Act 1897** serves as the archaic, colonial-era companion to the DMA, heavily utilized by the Union to command state health infrastructures during pandemics.

## 4. Evolution timeline

* 1950: The Dead Letter Hope
Dr. B.R. Ambedkar defends Article 356 in the Constituent Assembly, expressing hope it would remain a "dead letter" used only as an absolute last resort.


* 1970s-1980s: The Era of Misuse
Successive Union governments weaponize Article 356, imposing President's Rule over 100 times to routinely topple politically hostile state governments.


* 1994: S.R. Bommai Verdict
A 9-judge Supreme Court bench structurally revolutionizes federalism, laying down draconian judicial constraints that practically end the rampant political misuse of Article 356.


* 2005: Enactment of DMA
Following the 2004 Tsunami, Parliament passes the Disaster Management Act under the Concurrent List, quietly creating a highly unitary crisis-management architecture.


* 2020: COVID-19 National Lockdown
The Union uses the DMA to enforce a nationwide lockdown, legally overriding state powers over 'Public Health' (State List) without invoking a National Emergency (Article 352).


## 5. Economic perspective

* **Fiscal Federalism Strangulation** occurs when the Union uses the DMA to dictate draconian economic closures, completely destroying state revenue streams (GST/Excise) without providing corresponding financial compensation.
* **National Disaster Response Fund (NDRF)** creates a financial dependency paradigm, where states must repeatedly petition the Union for emergency relief packages, subjecting disaster relief to political bargaining.
* **PM CARES Fund Centralization** sparked massive federal economic friction, as corporate CSR funds were overwhelmingly routed to the central fund rather than directly to the severely cash-strapped Chief Minister Relief Funds.
* **Asymmetric Borrowing Constraints** legally prevent states from independently raising debt to fight disasters without explicit Union permission (Article 293), exacerbating local economic paralysis.
* **Micro-Economic Dictation** was evident when Union Ministries used DMA guidelines to dictate minute state-level commercial details, such as e-commerce delivery zones and liquor shop timings, bypassing state cabinets entirely.

* **Dr. B.R. Ambedkar (Constituent Assembly):** "I hope the first thing the President will do is to issue a mere warning to a province that has erred... I share the hope that these articles will never be called into operation and that they would remain a dead letter."

## 7. Committee or reports(year)

* **Sarkaria Commission on Centre-State Relations (1988)** heavily restricted the scope of Article 356, stating it must be an absolute measure of last resort after exhausting all other constitutional warnings (Article 355).

## 14. Advantage/Merits/Role

*(The necessity of Centralized Crisis Architecture)*

* **Unified Command Structure** eliminates chaotic, contradictory interstate protocols, ensuring a standardized national defense against borderless crises like pandemics or super-cyclones.
* **Resource Mobilization Scale** allows the Union to instantly redirect massive logistical assets (Railways, Air Force, Central Armed Police Forces) that individual states simply do not possess.
* **Macro-Economic Stabilization** ensures that the Reserve Bank of India and the Finance Ministry can unleash coordinated fiscal stimulus and moratoriums rapidly across all territories simultaneously.
* **Preventing Local Collapse** serves as a vital safety net when a catastrophic disaster completely physically destroys a state's administrative machinery (e.g., the 2013 Kedarnath floods).
* **Constitutional Restraint Survival:** The existence of the DMA actually protects Article 356 from being invoked for mere administrative failures, preserving the extreme sanctity of the constitutional emergency provision.

## 15. Disadvantage/De-Merits

* **One-Size-Fits-All Myopia** legally forces hyper-diverse states into uniform central protocols, resulting in absurd policies (like enforcing strict lockdowns in zero-case rural tribal districts).
* **Erosion of State Capacity** occurs when state bureaucracies are reduced to mere postal offices for central directives, stunting their ability to develop indigenous, localized crisis response mechanisms.
* **Accountability Asymmetry** allows the Union to claim political credit for successful disaster management (vaccine drives) while comfortably shifting the blame for ground-level failures onto the state Chief Ministers.
* **Circumvention of Federal Spirit** effectively breaks the constitutional promise, as the Union assumes absolute control over State List subjects (Health, Police, Commerce) without parliamentary debate or formal emergency declarations.
* **Bureaucratic Paralysis** strikes district magistrates who are torn between obeying their immediate political masters (the State Cabinet) and the legally binding, punitive directives of the Union Home Ministry.

## 16. Challenges

* **Absence of Judicial Precedents:** Unlike Article 356, the Supreme Court has not yet laid down comprehensive *Bommai*-like federal guardrails restricting the Union's arbitrary use of the DMA.
* **Politicization of Central Agencies:** The deployment of central disaster forces (NDRF) and financial teams is frequently weaponized to micromanage and politically embarrass opposition-ruled states during crises.
* **Information Monopolization** by the Union government during emergencies severely throttles transparent state-level data collection, blinding local epidemiologists and disaster planners.
* **The Concurrent List Abuse:** The systematic use of the Concurrent List (under which the DMA was passed) to hollow out the exclusive legislative competence of the State List remains the most unresolvable threat to Indian federalism.

## 17. Way forward/solution/positive outlook

* **Statutory Institutionalization of Consensus:** Parliament must amend the DMA to legally mandate the inclusion of the Inter-State Council (Article 263) in formulating national disaster guidelines, explicitly requiring state consultation.
* **Operationalizing the Punchhi Formula:** The Constitution should be amended to introduce a 'Localized Emergency' framework under Article 355, providing a middle ground between statutory coercion and total state dissolution.
* **Financial Federal Safeguards:** The Finance Commission must establish a rigid, non-discretionary statutory formula for disaster relief fund distribution, entirely stripping the Union Home Ministry of political favoritism.
* **Empowering State Disaster Forces:** The Union must drastically redirect funding to build independent, highly specialized State Disaster Response Forces (SDRF), shifting the response epicenter from Delhi to the districts.

## 18. Best practices

* **Australian National Cabinet:** Replacing statutory dictation with political consensus, Australia's pandemic management proved that equal partnerships between PMs and Premiers vastly outperform top-down coercion.
* **Swiss Subsidiarity Principle:** Switzerland successfully manages disasters by delegating maximum absolute authority to the Cantons (states) and Communes, with the federal government only stepping in for international logistics.
* **German Federal Financial Equalization:** Germany ensures that poorer states dealing with sudden economic disasters receive constitutionally guaranteed horizontal financial transfers, preventing the center from using relief funds as political leverage.

## 19. Other dimensions relevant to the topic

* **The Governor's Dual Loyalty** heavily complicates disaster management, as the constitutional head often clashes publicly with the elected Chief Minister over the interpretation of Union Home Ministry directives.
* **Media and Communication Centralization** during emergencies frequently sees the Union invoking the DMA to censor regional digital media and shape a singular, unquestionable national narrative.
* **Municipal Disempowerment** highlights the tragic irony that while the Union and States fight over federal power, the 74th Amendment (Urban Local Bodies) is completely ignored in disaster planning despite cities being the primary disaster epicenters.

## 20. Conclusion

* **Preserving** the constitutional equilibrium requires recognizing that national crises demand cooperative synergy, not statutory subjugation.
* **Restraining** creeping centralism through robust judicial oversight of laws like the DMA is as critical today as the *Bommai* restraints were on Article 356.
* **Institutionalizing** mutual political respect and financial equity is the only definitive path to secure a **firm, functional, federation.**

### 2. Foundational & Statutory/Theoretical Anchor

#### A. The Constitutional Fortress: Article 356 & The *S.R. Bommai* (1994) Doctrine

Under Article 356, the President can assume the functions of the State Government if satisfied that a situation has arisen where the government of the State *"cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of this Constitution."*

In ***S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)***, a 9-judge Constitution Bench established strict judicial and procedural barriers against the abuse of Article 356:

* **Conditional, Not Absolute Power:** The power under Article 356 is conditional; the presidential satisfaction is subject to **judicial review** on grounds of *mala fides*, extraneous considerations, or reliance on irrelevant material.
* **The Floor Test as the Sole Litmus Test:** The subjective assessment of the Governor regarding a Chief Minister's majority is irrelevant; the majority **must be tested objectively on the floor of the Legislative Assembly**.
* **Suspension First, Dissolution Later:** The State Legislative Assembly **cannot be dissolved** immediately upon issuing the proclamation. It can only be suspended until both Houses of Parliament ratify the proclamation within two months (Article 356(3)).
* **Power of *Status Quo Ante*:** If the Court finds the proclamation unconstitutional, it has the plenary power to **reactivate and restore the dissolved Assembly and Ministry** (as exercised in Arunachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand in 2016).
* **Federalism and Secularism as Basic Features:** Any state policy subverting secularism or the basic structure justifies Article 356 intervention, but routine political friction does not.

#### B. The Statutory Architecture: Disaster Management Act, 2005

Enacted under the Union’s legislative competence over entry 23 of the Concurrent List (*Social security and social insurance*) or residual entries, the DMA 2005 establishes a top-down hierarchy:

* **Section 6(2)(i):** The National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA), chaired by the Prime Minister, has the power to lay down policies, plans, and guidelines for disaster management.
* **Section 10(2)(l):** Empowers the National Executive Committee (headed by the **Union Home Secretary**) to issue binding directions to State Governments and State Executive Committees.
* **Section 38:** Mandates that State Governments **must take all measures** directed by the Central Government.
* **Section 72 (The *Non-Obstante* Clause):** Gives the provisions of the DMA **overriding effect over all other laws** inconsistent with it.

### 3. Core Dimensions & Applied Frictions

#### A. Overt Constitutional Emergency vs. Covert Statutory Centralism

| Dimension | Constitutional Emergency (Article 356) | Statutory Centralism (DMA, 2005) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Constitutional Threshold** | Severe: Complete breakdown of constitutional machinery in the state. | Broad: Any "catastrophe, mishap, calamity or grave occurrence" beyond local coping capacity. |
| **Parliamentary Scrutiny** | **High:** Requires approval by both Houses of Parliament within two months (Art. 356(3)). | **Nil:** Operates entirely through executive notifications and standard operating procedures (SOPs). |
| **Judicial Scrutiny** | **Rigorous:** Full review of cabinet advice and material under *Bommai* standards. | **Deferential:** Courts routinely apply the "Doctrine of Deference to Executive Expertise" during crises. |
| **Operational Impact** | State executive dismissed; Parliament legislates for the State. | State executive remains in office, but its administrative discretion is legally subordinated to the Union Home Secretary. |

#### B. The Circumvention of Federal Autonomy

* **Subversion of Seventh Schedule Separation:** During health and climatic crises, statutory centralism allows the Union bureaucracy to issue micro-directives on localized lockdowns, transport curbs, shop timings, and pharmaceutical pricing, sidestepping the State List.
* **The Absence of a "Sunset Clause":** Unlike Article 356, which expires automatically after six months unless extended by Parliament (up to a hard ceiling of three years under Article 356(4)), emergency powers under the DMA 2005 can be renewed indefinitely through administrative orders without legislative votes.

#### C. The Misuse of Article 355 as an Intermediate Coercive Tool

* Article 355 was designed as an **umbrella duty** justifying Central intervention under Articles 352, 356, or 365.
* In recent federal standoffs, the Union has increasingly decoupled Article 355 from Article 356, using it as an independent source of power to deploy central forces or establish supervisory committees in states without declaring an emergency.
* Legal scholars argue that using Article 355 to run parallel administrative command structures undermines democratic accountability: the Chief Minister remains politically accountable to the voters, while real operational control shifts to the Centre.

### 4. Structural Bottlenecks & Counter-Perspectives

* **Why Article 356 Was Abandoned for Alternative Central Levers:**
1. *Judicial Invalidation Risk:* Rulings in *Rameshwar Prasad (2006)*, *Harish Rawat (2016)*, and *Nabam Rebia (2016)* made invoking Article 356 legally precarious for the Centre.
2. *Shifting Tactics:* The Union has transitioned to softer, lower-risk mechanisms to check recalcitrant states: **Governor's pocket vetoes under Article 200**, investigations by Central agencies (ED/CBI), and conditional fiscal transfers.




* **The Defense of Statutory Centralism (The Necessity Argument):**
* Modern crises (global pandemics, extreme weather events, cross-border biological threats, national cyber outages) do not stop at state boundaries.
* Without a centralized statutory command (Section 10 of the DMA), inter-state competition, export bans between states (e.g., medical oxygen disputes), and conflicting quarantine rules would paralyze the national response.

### 5. Reforms & Forward-Looking Roadmap

#### A. Sarkaria Commission (1988) & Punchhi Commission (2010) on Article 356

* **Measure of Last Resort:** Article 356 should be invoked only after all alternative remedies under Articles 256, 257, and 355 have been exhausted, and after issuing an explicit **formal warning** to the errant state.
* **"Localized Emergency" (Punchhi Commission):** Amend Articles 355 and 356 to enable the Union to bring a specific trouble-torn district or municipal area under temporary central security administration for up to three months, without dissolving the state legislature or dismissing the ministry.

#### B. Federalizing the Disaster Management Act (2005)

* **Institutionalized Inter-State Consultation:** Mandatory consultation with the **Inter-State Council (Article 263)** before notifying national disaster frameworks that impact subjects under State List entries.
* **Statutory Time Limits:** Amend the DMA to mandate that any national directive issued under Section 10 that limits fundamental freedoms or overrides state powers must be tabled before Parliament within 30 days if prolonged beyond a baseline period.
* **Empowering State Disaster Management Authorities (SDMAs):** Shift from top-down Union Home Ministry directives to **collaborative federalism**, where the National Executive Committee sets broad technical standards while leaving operational execution to the states.

#### Landmark Precedents & Reports

1. ***S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994):*** Limited Article 356, brought presidential satisfaction under judicial review, and established floor tests as mandatory.


2. ***Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006):*** Ruled that subjective assessment of horse-trading by the Governor cannot justify dissolving an assembly under Article 356 without a floor test.
3. ***Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker (2016):*** Struck down the Governor’s unilateral discretionary summoning of the assembly that precipitated President's Rule in Arunachal Pradesh.
4. ***Sarkaria Commission (1988) & Punchhi Commission (2010):*** Recommended treating Article 356 as an exceptional tool and advocated for localized emergency mechanisms.
