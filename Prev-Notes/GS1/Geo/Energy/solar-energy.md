1. Intro
	- Harnessing inexhaustible photochemistry, solar energy stands as the prime catalyst for equitable decarbonization, energy sovereignty, and climate-resilient growth.
2. Evolution short story point wise
	1. India launched the Jawaharlal Nehru National Solar Mission in 2010 and later placed solar at the centre of its renewable-energy expansion.
	2. The transition has widened from large parks to rooftops, agriculture feeders, solar pumps, mini-grids and green-hydrogen production.
3. India's progress till date: data
	- solar is now the largest component of India's renewable-energy capacity with 164.59 GW capacity - npp
	- Floating solar Capacity: 700MW
4. India's target (mention commitment if any)
	1. Achieve 500 GW of non-fossil electricity capacity by 2030.
	2. Meet 50 percent of cumulative installed electricity capacity from non-fossil sources by 2030.
	3. Reduce the emissions intensity of GDP by 45 percent from 2005 levels by 2030 and achieve net-zero emissions by 2070.
	4. The National Solar Mission and PM Surya Ghar: Muft Bijli Yojana support utility-scale and household rooftop expansion.
5. Regional Variations - solar plant distribution
	1. Rajasthan and Gujarat: Highest potential due to high insolation, arid land and large solar parks.
	2. Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh: Large central and decentralised projects.
	3. Tamil Nadu and Telangana: Strong solar growth supported by industrial demand and grid connectivity.
	4. Himalayan and North-Eastern states: Lower large-scale potential in some areas, but scope for rooftops and decentralised systems.
6. Achievements
   1. Decadal solar expansion: 3 GW(2016) -> 160 GW(2026)
7. Potential
   1. Floating solar capacity: 700MW -> 102.18GWp. The National Institute of Solar Energy (NISE) estimates floating solar potential at 102.18 GWp, utilizing only 20% of available reservoir surface areas.
8. Benefits
	1. Clean, modular and scalable, from household rooftops to multi-gigawatt parks.
	2. Reduces 
   	1. coal use, 
   	2. import dependence and 
   	3. local air pollution.
	3. Enables 
   	1. electric mobility, 
   	2. green hydrogen and 
   	3. decentralised power.
   	4. rural enterprises and green jobs.
	4. Floating solar power: 
   	1. Avoids land acquisition conflicts
   	2. Reduces evaporation loss
   	3. Inhibition of Algal Blooms
9.  Challenges
	1. Intermittency, 
	2. evening peak mismatch and the 
	3. need for storage and flexible generation.
	4. Land acquisition, 
	5. habitat fragmentation and 
	6. transmission bottlenecks.
	7. Dependence on 
   	1. imported cells 
   	2. critical minerals and 
   	3. global supply chains.
	8.  Rooftop barriers such as 
    	1.  finance, 
    	2.  consumer awareness, 
    	3.  net-metering rules and 
    	4.  distribution-company concerns.
	9.  End-of-life panel waste and recycling need a circular-economy framework.
10. Key govt initiatives
	- Pradhan Mantri Surya Sarovar Yojana (PM-SSY):  ₹5,070 crore to deploy 5,000 MW of Floating Solar Photovoltaic (FSPV) projects integrated with 10,000 MWh of battery energy storage systems across India’s reservoirs and inland water bodies.
	- National Solar Mission; 
	- Solar Parks Scheme; 
	- PM-KUSUM for solar pumps and decentralised plants; 
	- PM Surya Ghar for rooftop solar; 
	- Production Linked Incentive scheme for high-efficiency solar PV modules; and 
	- International Solar Alliance.
11. Conclusion
	- Solar energy remains the bedrock of India's Net-Zero roadmap, provided capacity additions are matched with storage integration, domestic manufacturing, and grid modernization.
