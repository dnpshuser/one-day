### UPSC Quick Revision Note
1. Intro
Parliamentary disruption is a democratic dysfunction that weakens deliberation, accountability, and the constitutional legitimacy of legislative institutions.

2. Meaning and scope
- Disruption means obstructing the normal functioning of Parliament through stalling, slogan-shouting, walkouts, and procedural filibustering.
- It is different from legitimate dissent, which is constitutional and democratic in a parliamentary system.
- The aim of parliamentary democracy is not just majority rule but reasoned consensus through debate and scrutiny.
- Structured disagreement is essential; procedural paralysis is not.

3. Causes of disruption
- Opposition protests on matters of public importance, especially when urgent issues are not addressed promptly.
- Government’s reluctance to allow debate or reject adjournment motions and notices under Rule 267.
- Lack of effective deterrence and weak enforcement of parliamentary discipline.
- Perceived partisanship of the presiding officer and executive dominance in the House.
- Social media visibility and political strategy, where disruption becomes a tool for publicity rather than deliberation.
- Rigid party discipline under the Anti-Defection Law and whip system reduces independent parliamentary judgment.
- The opposition sometimes uses disruption to compensate for weak legislative numbers and limited agenda-setting power.

4. When disruption is not justified
- Routine obstruction over insignificant issues is unacceptable in a functional democracy.
- Disruption on issues that are already under discussion or can be debated in a structured manner lacks legitimacy.
- Physical intimidation, violence, tearing papers, or damaging property violates parliamentary norms and democratic conduct.
- Parliament must balance the right to protest with the duty to legislate and scrutinise.

5. Impacts on democracy
- Question Hour and Zero Hour lose meaning, weakening accountability of the executive.
- Bills and budgets may be passed without meaningful debate, undermining representative democracy.
- Legislative scrutiny is bypassed, reducing the quality of law-making and policy review.
- Minor parties and independent MPs lose space, leading to centralisation and executive dominance.
- Parliamentary productivity falls, eroding public trust in democratic institutions.

6. Institutional responsibility
- Opposition: must challenge the government but in a manner consistent with parliamentary norms and deliberation.
- Government: should be responsive to opposition concerns, facilitate debate, and avoid procedural arrogance.
- Presiding officer: must maintain order, enforce rules impartially, and prevent disruption from overshadowing debate.
- Citizens: must judge the quality of representation beyond theatrics and slogans.

7. Reforms needed
- Guarantee a minimum session calendar, as recommended by the National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (NCRWC).
- Protect Question Hour and allocate fair time for opposition debates and government accountability.
- Strengthen presiding officers’ neutrality and enforce procedural discipline without partisan bias.
- Use a Parliament productivity index to monitor time utilisation and debate quality.
- Adopt a graded code of conduct for MPs and stricter penalties for repeated disruption.
- Encourage pre-legislative consultation and greater use of standing committees for detailed scrutiny.

8. Conclusion
Parliament must move from a culture of mutual veto to a culture of structured disagreement. Democratic legitimacy rests not on disruption but on reasoned debate, accountability, and constitutional restraint, which are essential for SDG 16 and a functioning republic.

Source: Lok Sabha/Rajya Sabha Secretariat reports, PRS Legislative Research, parliamentary productivity data, and NCRWC recommendations.
