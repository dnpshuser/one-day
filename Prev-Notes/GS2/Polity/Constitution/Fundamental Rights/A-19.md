2. Seven freedoms and scope

4. Public order, morality and security
5. Digital expression and modern challenges
6. Conclusion

---

1. Intro
Article 19 guarantees the freedoms that make democratic citizenship possible, allowing expression, movement, association, and participation in public life. 

2. Freedom of speech and expression
   - It includes speech, criticism, artistic expression, and dissemination of ideas.
   - Democracy cannot function without robust public discussion and criticism.
   - The right is not absolute, as reasonable restrictions protect sovereignty, public order, morality, and decency.
   - Courts have repeatedly upheld open debate while limiting speech that threatens public order or incitement.

3. Right to assemble and form associations
   - Citizens have the right to peaceful assembly and association for political, social, and professional purposes.
   - This helps democracy by enabling collective action and mobilisation.
   - Restrictions may be imposed for public order, national security, and law enforcement.
   - However, restrictions must be proportional and not arbitrary.

4. Right to movement and residence
   - Article 19(1)(d) and (e) allow movement and residence across Indian territory.
   - It supports individual mobility, labour, and access to opportunity.
   - Restrictions are allowed on grounds of public order, security, and welfare legislation.
   - In a large federal state, this right is crucial for migration, employment, and development.

5. Freedom of profession, occupation, and trade
   - It allows citizens to pursue livelihoods and economic opportunity.
   - The State may regulate professions for public welfare, safety, and standards.
   - The balance between liberty and regulation is central to constitutional governance.
   - This right strengthens personal dignity and economic agency.

6. Section 66A and modern challenges
   - Shreya Singhal (2015) struck down Section 66A as unconstitutional for vagueness and chilling effect on speech.
   - Online expression raises new questions about free speech, misinformation, and hate speech.
   - The test remains whether restrictions are precise, reasonable, and proportionate.

7. Conclusion
Article 19 remains central to democratic freedom, requiring a careful balance between liberty and regulation in a modern constitutional state.

## Evolution

**Article 19** of the Constitution of India is the cornerstone of civil liberties, guaranteeing six fundamental freedoms to Indian citizens. Unlike absolute civil rights, Article 19 embodies an institutional compromise between individual liberty and social control through explicit "reasonable restrictions" codified in clauses (2) through (6).

Over seven decades, the Supreme Court of India transformed Article 19 from a literal, compartmentalized provision into a dynamic, rights-expanding charter.

```text
                           ARTICLE 19(1) FREEDOMS
                                      │
     ┌──────────────┬─────────────────┼─────────────────┬──────────────┐
     ▼              ▼                 ▼                 ▼              ▼
19(1)(a)         19(1)(b)          19(1)(c)          19(1)(d)/(e)   19(1)(g)
Speech &         Peaceful          Unions &          Movement &     Trade &
Expression       Assembly        Associations        Residence     Profession
     │              │                 │                 │              │
Restricted by   Restricted by     Restricted by     Restricted by  Restricted by
 19(2)           19(3)             19(4)             19(5)          19(6)

```

*(Note: Article 19(1)(f)—the Right to Property—was repealed by the 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978, and transitioned to a constitutional/legal right under Article 300A).*

### 1. Structural Architecture and the Mechanics of Restrictions

Article 19 guarantees rights exclusively to **citizens** (not companies or foreign nationals, though corporate shareholders can invoke them if their individual rights are impaired, as settled in *RC Cooper* and *Bennett Coleman*).

Each sub-clause of Article 19(1) is paired with a corresponding restriction clause that sets an **exhaustive** list of permissible grounds:

| Sub-Clause (Right) | Corresponding Limitation | Permissible Grounds of Restriction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **19(1)(a)** Speech & Expression | **19(2)** | SSFfPoDmCcDi - Sovereignty & integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign States, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, or incitement to an offence. |
| **19(1)(b)** Peaceful Assembly | **19(3)** | Sovereignty & integrity of India, public order. |
| **19(1)(c)** Form Associations/Unions | **19(4)** | Sovereignty & integrity of India, public order, or morality. *(Includes Co-operative Societies via the 97th Amendment, 2011).* |
| **19(1)(d) & (e)** Movement & Residence | **19(5)** | Interests of the general public, or protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe. |
| **19(1)(g)** Trade, Business, Profession | **19(6)** | Interests of the general public; professional/technical qualifications; State monopolies. |

#### The "Reasonableness" Doctrine

The word **"reasonable"** was introduced into Article 19(2) by the **1st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1951** following the *Romesh Thappar* decision. In **State of Madras v. V.G. Row (1952)**, Chief Justice Patanjali Sastri established the definitive standard for reasonableness:

* The restriction must strike a balance between individual freedom and social security.
* The test is **objective**, evaluated from the perspective of an ordinary, reasonable person, not by subjective judicial impressions.
* **Proportionality Rule:** The means adopted by the legislature must not be disproportionate or excessive compared to the object sought to be achieved.

### 2. The Doctrinal Evolution: From Isolation to the Golden Triangle

```text
               PHASE 1: SILO APPROACH (1950)
                [A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras]
   ┌───────────────┐     ┌───────────────┐     ┌───────────────┐
   │  Article 14   │     │  Article 19   │     │  Article 21   │
   └───────────────┘     └───────────────┘     └───────────────┘
            (Watertight compartments: If detained under
             Art. 21, Art. 19 cannot be invoked)
                           │
                           ▼
              PHASE 2: DIRECT EFFECT TEST (1970)
                [R.C. Cooper v. Union of India]
   (Rejected the "object of state action" test; focused
    on the DIRECT IMPACT/EFFECT of the law on rights)
                           │
                           ▼
             PHASE 3: THE GOLDEN TRIANGLE (1978)
               [Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India]
                       ┌───────────────┐
                       │  Article 14   │
                       └───────┬───────┘
                              / \
                             /   \
                            ▼     ▼
             ┌───────────────┐   ┌───────────────┐
             │  Article 19   │◄──┤  Article 21   │
             └───────────────┘   └───────────────┘
     (A law restricting liberty under Art. 21 must pass the
      test of reasonableness under Art. 19 and non-arbitrariness
      under Art. 14)

```

* **A.K. Gopalan (1950):** The Court adopted the "watertight compartments" theory. It held that if a person's physical liberty was restricted under Article 21 (preventive detention), they could not claim freedom of movement or speech under Article 19.
* **R.C. Cooper (Bank Nationalisation Case, 1970):** Overruled the *Gopalan* doctrine. The Court shifted the test from the "form and object of state action" to the **"direct effect and operation"** of the state action on the fundamental rights of the citizen.
* **Maneka Gandhi (1978):** Integrated Articles 14, 19, and 21 into the **Golden Triangle**. Any procedure established by law under Article 21 that curtails personal liberty must not be arbitrary (Article 14) and must satisfy the reasonable restrictions under Article 19.

### 3. Evolutionary Expansion of Article 19(1)(a): Speech and Expression

Article 19(1)(a) has seen the broadest expansion. The judiciary read multiple implicit sub-rights into "Freedom of Speech and Expression":

```text
                           ARTICLE 19(1)(a)
                                  │
       ┌──────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────┐
       ▼                          ▼                          ▼
 Press & Media            Informational Rights        Digital & Behavioral
 • Romesh Thappar (1950)   • Right to Know (Raj       • Internet Access (Anuradha
 • Bennett Coleman (1972)    Narain, 1975)              Bhasin, 2020)
 • Sakal Papers (1962)     • Candidate Backgrounds    • Strike down of 66A IT Act
 • Indian Express (1985)     (ADR Case, 2002)           (Shreya Singhal, 2015)
                           • Right to Remain Silent   • Horizontal enforcement
                             (Bijoe Emmanuel, 1986)     (Kaushal Kishor, 2023)

```

#### A. Freedom of the Press

While the Indian Constitution does not explicitly mention the press, the Supreme Court held that freedom of the press is an implicit, foundational element of Article 19(1)(a):

* **Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras (1950):** Struck down a ban on the circulation of the weekly journal *Cross Roads*, establishing that freedom of speech includes the **freedom of circulation**.
* **Sakal Papers v. Union of India (1962):** Struck down the Daily Newspapers (Price and Page) Order, holding that the State cannot regulate the price or volume of pages, as this directly curtails newspaper circulation and expression.
* **Bennett Coleman & Co. v. Union of India (1972):** Invalidated the Newsprint Control Order restricting newsprint quotas, establishing the **"direct effect" test**—even if an economic regulation targets newsprint, if its direct effect limits publication, it violates Article 19(1)(a).

#### B. Right to Know and Voter Information

* **State of U.P. v. Raj Narain (1975):** Justice K.K. Mathew held that citizens have a right to know every public act done by public functionaries. This laid the constitutional foundation for the subsequent enactment of the **Right to Information (RTI) Act, 2005**.
* **Union of India v. Association for Democratic Reforms (ADR, 2002):** Ruled that voters have a fundamental right under Article 19(1)(a) to know the criminal antecedents, financial assets, liabilities, and educational qualifications of candidates contesting elections.

#### C. The Right to Remain Silent

* **Bijoe Emmanuel v. State of Kerala (1986) (National Anthem Case):** Three Jehovah's Witness school children refused to sing the National Anthem due to religious beliefs, though they stood up respectfully. The Supreme Court held that freedom of speech and expression includes the **right to remain silent**. Compelling someone to speak or sing against their conscience violates Article 19(1)(a).

#### D. The Digital Frontier and Online Speech

* **Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015):** The Court struck down **Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000** for being unconstitutionally vague and overbroad. The Court drew a clear constitutional line:
```text
Discussion ──► Advocacy ──► Incitement
(Permissible)  (Permissible) (Can be Restricted under Art. 19(2))

```


Mere discussion or advocacy of an unpopular cause cannot be suppressed unless it reaches the threshold of clear incitement to violence or public disorder.
* **Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020):** Arising from internet shutdowns in Jammu & Kashmir, the Court ruled that **the freedom of speech and expression, as well as the freedom to practice any trade or business over the medium of the internet, is constitutionally protected under Article 19(1)(a) and 19(1)(g)**. Indefinite internet suspensions were held to be illegal under the proportionality standard.

#### E. Horizontal Application against Private Individuals

* **Kaushal Kishor v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2023):** A 5-judge Constitution Bench held that Fundamental Rights under **Articles 19 and 21 are enforceable horizontally against persons other than the State or its instrumentalities**, expanding the operational reach of speech protections against non-state actors.

### 4. Evolution of Other Freedoms: 19(1)(b) to 19(1)(g)

#### Article 19(1)(b): Freedom of Assembly

* Guarantees the right to assemble peaceably and without arms.
* **Himat Lal K. Shah v. Commissioner of Police (1973):** Upheld the right of citizens to hold public meetings on public streets, subject to reasonable police regulation for traffic control.
* **Amit Sahni v. Commissioner of Police (Shaheen Bagh Case, 2020):** Balanced the right to protest against the right of the general public to unhindered movement. The Court ruled that protests cannot occupy public ways indefinitely; demonstrations must take place in designated protest areas.

#### Article 19(1)(c): Freedom of Association

* **Damayanti Naranga v. Union of India (1971):** The right to form an association includes the **negative right not to join**, as well as the right to continue the association with composition determined by its members without arbitrary state intrusion.
* **97th Constitutional Amendment (2011):** Explicitly added **"Co-operative Societies"** to Article 19(1)(c). In *Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah (2021)*, the Supreme Court struck down parts of this amendment for violating federal ratifications, but preserved the insertion of co-operatives into Article 19(1)(c) for multi-state cooperatives.

#### Article 19(1)(d) & (e): Movement and Residence

* Protects internal movement throughout the territory of India (external travel abroad is protected under Article 21, as decided in *Satwant Singh* and *Maneka Gandhi*).
* **Restrictions Under Article 19(5):** Movement can be restricted to protect public order or the unique cultural and land rights of **Scheduled Tribes** (e.g., Inner Line Permits and restrictions on non-tribals acquiring land in Fifth/Sixth Schedule regions).
* **Ajay Canu v. Union of India (1988):** Compulsory helmet laws were challenged as an infringement of free movement; the Court held that regulatory measures ensuring personal safety and public health fall well within reasonable restrictions.

#### Article 19(1)(g): Freedom of Trade, Profession, and Business

* **Inherently Injurious Trades (*Res Extra Commercium*):** As established from *Cooverjee B. Bharucha (1954)* to *Khoday Distilleries (1995)*, no citizen can claim a fundamental right to engage in trades that are noxious, hazardous, or destructive to public health and morals (e.g., trafficking, trade in intoxicants, gambling). These activities fall under *res extra commercium* (outside ordinary commerce).
* **Street Vendors' Rights (*Sodan Singh v. New Delhi Municipal Committee, 1989*):** Recognized that the right to hawk or carry on street vending on public pavements is a legitimate form of trade protected by Article 19(1)(g), subject to reasonable municipal hawking zones.
* **Digital Commerce (*Anuradha Bhasin, 2020*):** Carrying out trade and e-commerce through the internet was affirmed as a fundamental right protected under Article 19(1)(g).
