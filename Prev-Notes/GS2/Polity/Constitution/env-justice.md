### Explained: 
#### Case Laws
1. Cooverjee B. Bharucha vs. Excise Commissioner (1954): The Supreme Court, while deciding the matter relating to carrying on the trade of liquor in Cooverjee B. Bharucha Vs Excise commissioner, Ajmer (1954, SC 220) observed that, if there is a clash between environmental protection and right to freedom of trade and occupation, the courts have to balance environmental interests with the fundamental rights to carry on any occupations.

**Cooverjee B. Bharucha v. Excise Commissioner and the Chief Commissioner, Ajmer (1954)** is a foundational five-judge Constitution Bench decision of the Supreme Court of India that settled the constitutional status of trading in intoxicating liquors under **Article 19(1)(g)**.

The judgment established that the State can impose severe restrictions, including an outright monopoly or total prohibition, on trades that are inherently harmful to public health and safety without violating the fundamental right to trade.

---

### Factual Background

* **The Auction:** Under the *Excise Regulation, 1915* (applicable to the centrally administered territory of Ajmer), liquor shop licenses were auctioned to the highest bidder for a fixed tenure.
* **The Dispute:** Cooverjee B. Bharucha was a liquor licensee whose bid was initially the highest. However, the Excise Commissioner cancelled the auction before final confirmation, conducted a fresh auction, and awarded the license to another party at a significantly higher fee.
* **The Challenge:** Bharucha approached the Supreme Court under **Article 32**, contending that:
1. The auction process and statutory conditions infringed his fundamental right to practice any profession, or to carry on any occupation, trade, or business under **Article 19(1)(g)**.
2. Creating a system where licenses were auctioned to single bidders amounted to creating an illegal State-backed monopoly, which exceeded the scope of "reasonable restrictions" permitted under **Article 19(6)**.



---

### Key Legal Issues

1. Does a citizen possess an inherent or fundamental right under Article 19(1)(g) to trade in intoxicating liquor?
2. Can the State completely prohibit or create a state monopoly over a commercial trade under the guise of "reasonable restrictions" under Article 19(6)?

---

### The Supreme Court's Verdict

Delivering the unanimous verdict of the Constitution Bench, **Chief Justice Mehr Chand Mahajan** dismissed the petition:

* **Nature of the Business Matters:**
The Court held that the reasonableness of a restriction under Article 19(6) depends on the nature of the trade. What is unreasonable for an innocuous trade (like selling grains or cloth) may be entirely reasonable for dangerous or hazardous commodities.
* **No Unconditional Right in Liquor:**
Relying partly on American jurisprudence (*Crowley v. Christensen*), the Court observed that there is no inherent right in a citizen to sell intoxicating liquors by retail. Because of its potential to destroy public morals, peace, and health, liquor trade has historically been treated on a separate footing.
* **Prohibition as a Form of Restriction:**
The Court clarified that the word "restriction" in Article 19(6) can, in appropriate circumstances regarding inherently hazardous goods, extend to **total prohibition** or the creation of an exclusive **State monopoly** via auctioning rights.
* **Revenue via Regulation:**
The State's auctioning of licenses is a legitimate regulatory mechanism to control consumption and simultaneously raise public revenue.

---

### Subsequent Evolution & Doctrinal Legacy

*Cooverjee Bharucha* formed the first stepping stone in a half-century-long judicial doctrine regarding "vices" and State control:

```
Cooverjee Bharucha (1954)
• State can heavily regulate/monopolize liquor.
• Restriction under Art 19(6) can include complete prohibition.
                  │
                  ▼
State of Bombay v. R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala (1957)
• Doctrine of "Res Extra Commercium" formulated.
• Gambling/betting are not "trade or business" at all.
                  │
                  ▼
Khoday Distilleries Ltd. v. State of Karnataka (1995)
• Consolidated all prior case law into definitive rules.
• Confirmed: No fundamental right under Art 19(1)(g) to trade in liquor.
• Liquor is "Res Extra Commercium" (outside the realm of ordinary commerce).

```

*Cooverjee Bharucha* laid down the initial principle that enabled Indian States to enact prohibition laws under **Article 47** (Directive Principles of State Policy) without being invalidated by Article 19(1)(g).

5. Subash Kumar vs. State of Bihar (1991): The
Supreme Court ruled that Article 32 of the Indian
Constitution is intended to protect a citizen’s

**Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar (1991)** is a landmark Supreme Court decision that officially entrenched the **right to pollution-free water and air as an integral component of the Right to Life under Article 21**.

At the same time, it serves as a leading precedent on the **limits of Public Interest Litigation (PIL)**, warning against litigants misusing constitutional writ jurisdiction for private vendettas or commercial extortion.

---

### Factual Background

* **The Parties:** Subhash Kumar (the petitioner) filed a writ petition under **Article 32** against the State of Bihar and the **West Bokaro Collieries** (owned by Tata Iron & Steel Co. - TISCO), located in Hazaribagh (now in Jharkhand).
* **The Allegation:** The petitioner alleged that TISCO's washeries were discharging toxic coal slurry and untreated industrial sludge directly into the **Bokaro River**. He argued that:
* The sludge settled in the riverbed, polluting agricultural lands and groundwater.
* The discharge violated the *Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974*.
* It threatened the health and livelihoods of downstream residents, violating **Article 21**.


* **The Relief Sought:** Kumar requested an immediate ban on the discharge of slurry into the river, forfeiture of the slurry, and penal action against the company and the Bihar State Pollution Control Board.

---

### The Reality Behind the Petition

During the proceedings, counter-affidavits revealed the true motive behind the PIL:

* **Commercial Dispute:** Subhash Kumar was not a neutral social activist. He was an influential coal dealer who had previously obtained contracts from TISCO to collect and purchase slurry from the plant.
* **Retaliatory Action:** When TISCO refused to allocate more slurry quota to him and terminated his contracts, he launched multiple complaints and ultimately filed a writ petition under Article 32 masquerading as public interest advocacy to coerce the company into resuming business with him.
* **Pollution Control Compliance:** The Bihar State Pollution Control Board proved that TISCO had constructed settling tanks, slurry recovery plants, and water treatment systems that complied with statutory discharge parameters under the Water Act.

---

### Key Rulings & Legal Principles

Delivered by a two-judge bench comprising **Justice K.N. Singh** and **Justice N.D. Ojha**, the judgment established two enduring doctrines in Indian constitutional law:

#### 1. Article 21 Encompasses Pollution-Free Water and Air

The Court explicitly expanded the substantive core of the Right to Life:

> *"Right to live is a fundamental right under Art. 21 of the Constitution and it includes the right of enjoyment of pollution-free water and air for full enjoyment of life."*

* The Court held that if anything imperils or degrades that quality of life in violation of statutory environmental laws, **any citizen has the right to invoke Article 32** to remove the environmental hazard by filing a writ petition before the Supreme Court.

#### 2. The Doctrine Against Abuse of PIL (Locus Standi Safeguards)

While affirming the expansive scope of Article 21, the Court **dismissed the petition with costs (₹5,000)** because the petitioner had abused the judicial process:

* **Bona Fide Test:** A Public Interest Litigation must be initiated for the protection of public good, the enforcement of human rights of underprivileged sections, or ecological safety. It **cannot be invoked to satisfy personal malice, political rivalry, or private commercial motives**.
* **Gatekeeping by Courts:** The Court emphasized that constitutional courts must rigorously scrutinize the *bona fides* of PIL petitioners at the threshold. If a litigant files a PIL with an oblique motive or to settle personal scores, the petition should be dismissed with punitive costs to deter vexatious litigation.

The **Taj Trapezium Case**, formally cited as **M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1996)**, is a landmark environmental ruling delivered by the Supreme Court of India (led by Justice Kuldip Singh, known as the "Green Judge").

The case established that economic activity and industrial operations must yield to the preservation of cultural heritage and ecology under the **Precautionary Principle** and the **Doctrine of Sustainable Development**.


### 1. Factual Background and the Threat

In the early 1980s, environmentalist and advocate **M.C. Mehta** filed a writ petition under **Article 32** of the Constitution, drawing attention to the rapid deterioration of the white Makrana marble of the **Taj Mahal** in Agra.

---

* **The Polluting Sources:** Over 292 heavy industrial units—including iron foundries, chemical factories, brick kilns, glass works in Firozabad, and the nearby **Mathura Oil Refinery**—were emitting massive quantities of Sulfur Dioxide ($\text{SO}_2$) and particulate matter.
* **The Chemical Mechanism (Acid Rain & "Marble Cancer"):**
1. Industrial emissions of $\text{SO}_2$ and Nitrogen Oxides ($\text{NO}_x$) mixed with atmospheric moisture to form sulfuric and nitric acids:

$$\text{SO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \frac{1}{2}\text{O}_2 \longrightarrow \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$$


2. The resulting acid precipitation reacted with the calcium carbonate ($\text{CaCO}_3$) of the marble, corroding it into calcium sulfate (gypsum):

$$\text{CaCO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{CaSO}_4 \cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$$


3. This chemical corrosion caused peeling, pitting, and yellow-brown discolouration, a phenomenon termed **"Marble Cancer"**.


### 2. The Expert Committees

Before issuing binding directives, the Supreme Court commissioned and reviewed technical assessments:

* **Varadarajan Committee (1977):** Examined environmental impacts around Mathura and recommended safety zones and air monitoring stations.
* **National Environmental Engineering Research Institute (NEERI) Reports (1990 & 1993):** Found high ambient $\text{SO}_2$ levels around Agra and recommended shutting down or shifting foundries and brick kilns, while mandating clean fuel conversions.


### 3. Key Directives of the Supreme Court (December 1996)

* **Definition of the Taj Trapezium Zone (TTZ):** The Court formalized a demarcated geographical trapezoidal zone covering **10,400 sq. km** surrounding the Taj Mahal (encompassing parts of Agra, Firozabad, Mathura, Hathras, and Etah in Uttar Pradesh, and Bharatpur in Rajasthan) to protect monuments including the Taj Mahal, Agra Fort, and Fatehpur Sikri.
* **The Clean Fuel Ultimatum (292 Industries):** The Court issued a strict binary order to 292 identified coal/coke-consuming industrial units:
* **Option 1:** Switch fuel supply to **Compressed Natural Gas (CNG)** or Liquified Petroleum Gas (LPG) supplied via GAIL pipelines by designated cut-off dates.
* **Option 2:** If unable or unwilling to switch to clean fuel, **stop operating and relocate** outside the Taj Trapezium Zone.


* **Protection of Workmen:** To prevent industrial workers from bearing the brunt of closures/relocations, the Court mandated:
* Workmen had to receive continuity of employment and relocation compensation (a shifting bonus equal to one year's wages).
* If an industry chose to shut down completely rather than relocate, workers were entitled to gratuity, retrenchment compensation, plus an extra year of wages as additional terminal compensation.


* **Ban on New Polluting Units:** Prohibited the establishment of new coal-based or hazardous manufacturing units within the TTZ.
* **Vehicular and Power Directives:** Mandated the supply of unleaded petrol, low-sulfur diesel, construction of bypass roads to divert heavy vehicular transit away from Agra, and uninterrupted power supply to reduce reliance on diesel generator sets.


### 4. Constitutional and Legal Principles Enforced

| Legal Doctrine | Source / Context | Application in the Taj Case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Precautionary Principle** | Principle 15, Rio Declaration (1992); read into **Articles 21, 48A, and 51A(g)** | Lack of absolute scientific certainty could not be an excuse to delay remedial action; the burden was placed on industries to prove their emissions were non-injurious. |
| **Polluter Pays Principle** | Customary International Law; imported in *Vellore Citizens (1996)* | Industrialists had to bear the financial cost of fuel conversions, worker compensation, and environmental rehabilitation. |
| **Public Trust Doctrine** | Derived from Roman law / *Kamal Nath (1997)* | Historical monuments of world heritage value are held in trust by the State for present and future generations; the State cannot allow private commercial interests to degrade them. |
| **Continuing Mandamus** | Procedural innovation under **Article 32** | The Supreme Court did not dispose of the case with a single judgment; it kept the matter open through regular monitoring, monitoring committees, and compliance reviews for decades. |
