**R.C. Cooper v. Union of India (1970)**, widely known as the **Bank Nationalisation Case**, is an 11-judge Constitution Bench decision of the Supreme Court of India that fundamentally altered Indian constitutional jurisprudence. It dismantled the old doctrine of mutual exclusivity among fundamental rights, established the "effect test" for state action, and redefined the standards of compensation for compulsory property acquisition.

---

### Factual Background

* **The Policy Measure:** In July 1969, the Central Government promulgated the *Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Ordinance*, followed by the *Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, 1969*, nationalizing 14 major commercial banks accounting for over 70% of the country’s banking deposits.
* **The Petitioner:** Rustam Cavasjee Cooper, a major shareholder, director of the Central Bank of India Ltd., and an account holder, challenged the constitutional validity of the Ordinance and Act under **Article 32**.
* **The Constitutional Challenge:** Cooper contended that the acquisition:
1. Infringed the fundamental rights to property under **Article 19(1)(f)** and **Article 31(2)**.
2. Violated **Article 14** (Equality) due to hostile discrimination against the 14 named banks, while foreign banks and other Indian scheduled banks were excluded.
3. Violated **Article 19(1)(g)** because the acquired banks were barred from carrying on ordinary banking business while non-acquired commercial banks could do so freely.



---

### Key Legal Issues Before the 11-Judge Bench

1. **Locus Standi of a Shareholder:** Can a shareholder and director invoke fundamental rights under Article 32 when the property acquired belongs to a corporate entity?
2. **Mutual Exclusivity of Fundamental Rights:** Does the deprivation of property under Article 31(2) preclude testing the acquisition law against Articles 14, 19(1)(f), and 19(1)(g)?
3. **Object Test vs. Effect Test:** Is the constitutionality of a law judged by the subjective *object/form* of the legislature, or by its *direct effect and operation* on citizen rights?
4. **Adequacy of Compensation:** Does the constitutional term "compensation" in Article 31(2) mandate an equivalent in money (fair market value), and were the statutory valuation principles illusory?

### Key Rulings & Principles

#### 1. Shareholder's Right to Invoke Article 32

The Union argued that a company is not a citizen and therefore cannot invoke Article 19. The Supreme Court rejected this formalistic bar:

* A shareholder can maintain a petition if the state action impairs their individual rights (such as voting rights, receipt of dividends, or capital value) alongside the company's rights.
* Technical corporate identity cannot be used by the State as a shield to deny constitutional remedies to citizens holding equity in the enterprise.

#### 2. Overruling the "Silo Approach" (*A.K. Gopalan* Overruled)

Under *A.K. Gopalan (1950)*, fundamental rights were treated as isolated silos; if an enactment was valid under Article 31, it could not be scrutinized under Article 19(1)(f).

* The 10:1 majority (delivered by Justice J.C. Shah; Justice A.N. Ray dissenting) expressly discarded this theory.
* The Court held that fundamental rights are not mutually exclusive. A law depriving a citizen of property must not only comply with Article 31, but must also satisfy the requirement of non-arbitrariness under **Article 14** and the test of "reasonable restrictions" under **Article 19(1)(f) and 19(1)(g)**.

#### 3. The "Direct Effect and Operation" Test

The Court discarded the old "pith and substance / object" test for determining fundamental rights violations:

* The test is not what the legislature *intended* to achieve (the object or form of the statute).
* The true constitutional test is the **direct and inevitable effect and operation of the law** upon the rights of the citizen.

#### 4. Hostile Discrimination under Article 14

The Act prohibited the 14 nationalized banks from carrying on any other banking business, while simultaneously permitting other domestic and foreign banks to continue banking and allied commercial activities. The Court held this differential treatment to be unreasonable and discriminatory under Article 14.

#### 5. Illusory Compensation under Article 31(2)

Although the 4th Amendment (1955) had stated that the adequacy of compensation could not be questioned in court, the Supreme Court ruled:

* "Compensation" inherently implies a **just equivalent** in money for the property acquired.
* If the statutory method of valuation yields an illusory amount, or is based on irrelevant principles (such as valuing assets at book value without accounting for goodwill or the true value of long-term leases), the law violates Article 31(2) and is open to judicial review.

---

### Constitutional Aftermath

The verdict struck down the 1969 Banking Act, triggering a major confrontation between Parliament and the Judiciary:

* **Government Response:** Parliament immediately re-enacted the law with restructured compensation terms to cure the valuation defects.
* **The 25th Constitutional Amendment Act (1971):**
* Substituted the word **"compensation"** with the word **"amount"** in Article 31(2) to strip the judiciary of the power to demand a market equivalent.
* Inserted **Article 31C** to give primacy to Directive Principles under Article 39(b) and (c) over Articles 14, 19, and 31.


* **Bridge to *Maneka Gandhi (1978)*:** By establishing that fundamental rights must be read together, *R.C. Cooper* served as the direct doctrinal precursor to the **Golden Triangle (Articles 14, 19, and 21)**.

• Judgment Outcome: Invalidated the Banking Companies Act, 1969 by a 10:1 majority, forcing the Union Government to enact a revised law with rectified compensation provisions.
• Catalyzing the 25th Amendment: Triggered the 25th Constitutional Amendment (1971), which replaced "compensation" with the word "amount" in Article 31(2) and introduced Article 31C.
