**Kaushal Kishor v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2023)** is a landmark 5-judge Constitution Bench judgment of the Supreme Court of India. It primarily settled two major constitutional questions: whether additional restrictions can be judicially imposed on free speech under **Article 19(1)(a)**, and whether Fundamental Rights under **Articles 19 and 21** can be enforced **horizontally against private individuals and non-state actors**.

---

### 1. Factual Background

The case arose from two separate incidents involving controversial remarks by public ministers:

* **The Bulandshahr Highway Gang-Rape (2016):** A minor girl and her mother were brutally gang-raped on the National Highway near Bulandshahr, Uttar Pradesh. Azam Khan, then a senior Cabinet Minister in Uttar Pradesh, made a public press statement dismissing the incident as an engineered "political conspiracy" to malign the state government.
* **The Kerala Incident:** A minister in Kerala made derogatory remarks targeting women, leading to a demand for a code of conduct for public officials.

The victims and petitioners moved the Supreme Court under Article 32, arguing that statements by public functionaries prejudiced a fair criminal trial, insulted human dignity, and violated their fundamental right to life with dignity under **Article 21**.

---

### 2. Five Constitutional Questions & Rulings (4:1 Majority)

The bench—comprising Justices S. Abdul Nazeer, B.R. Gavai, A.S. Bopanna, V. Ramasubramanian, and B.V. Nagarathna—delivered a **4:1 majority verdict** (authored by Justice V. Ramasubramanian), with **Justice B.V. Nagarathna delivering a partially dissenting opinion**.

#### Question 1: Can grounds outside Article 19(2) be added to restrict free speech?

* **Majority Ruling:** **No.** The eight grounds listed in **Article 19(2)** are **exhaustive**.
* The judiciary cannot invent new grounds of restriction (such as "speech by public ministers" or "conflict with another citizen's dignity") under the guise of invoking other fundamental rights.
* An individual's right under Article 19(1)(a) cannot be curtailed on any ground outside Article 19(2).

#### Question 2: Horizontal Application of Articles 19 and 21

* **Majority Ruling:** **Yes.** Historically, Part III was viewed as a vertical shield against the State. While Articles 15(2), 17, 23, and 24 explicitly apply horizontally, the Court held that:
* A fundamental right under **Article 19 and Article 21 can be enforced against persons other than the State or its instrumentalities**.
* Modern threats to privacy, dignity, and personal liberty frequently originate from corporations, digital platforms, and private individuals; hence, courts can issue writs against non-state entities when fundamental rights are infringed.



#### Question 3: Is the State under an affirmative duty to protect Article 21 against non-state actors?

* **Majority Ruling:** **Yes.** The State has an affirmative/positive obligation to ensure that the life and personal liberty of a citizen are protected, even when the threat arises from acts or omissions of another private citizen or entity.

#### Question 4: Is the government vicariously liable for a Minister's statement?

* **Majority Ruling:** **No.** The constitutional doctrine of **collective responsibility** is a political accountability mechanism running from the Council of Ministers to the Legislature (House of the People / State Legislative Assembly) for formal government decisions.
* A statement made orally by a Minister outside the legislative chamber, even if connected to affairs of the State or intended to defend the government, **cannot be attributed vicariously to the government itself**.

#### Question 5: Does a Minister's statement constitute a "Constitutional Tort"?

* **Majority Ruling:** A mere oral statement made by a minister, even if contrary to constitutional values, does not automatically constitute an actionable constitutional tort.
* **Actionable Threshold:** An action in tort will lie **only if the statement gets translated into actual executive action or omission**, resulting in actionable harm, injury, or loss to a citizen.

---

### 3. Justice B.V. Nagarathna's Dissenting View

Justice Nagarathna agreed that Article 19(2) cannot be amended by courts, but parted ways on critical points:

1. **Public Functionaries Bear Higher Responsibility:** Public officials have an ethical and constitutional duty to maintain sobriety, dignity, and decorum; their speech carries significant social authority.
2. **Dignity vs. Speech:** Free speech cannot be an absolute shield to crush individual dignity and privacy under Article 21, especially of crime victims.
3. **Attribution to Government:** If a minister speaks in an official capacity to articulate government policy or defend state actions, such statements *should* be attributed to the government under vicarious liability.
4. **Horizontal Writs:** She cautioned against allowing ordinary Article 32/226 writ petitions directly against private citizens for speech violations, observing that established civil and criminal remedies (e.g., tort of defamation, criminal law) should be pursued rather than turning every private dispute into a constitutional writ.

The 2016 Bulandshahr Highway Gang-Rape case moved along two distinct tracks: **the constitutional track** before the Supreme Court (which gave rise to *Kaushal Kishor* in 2023) and **the criminal trial track** prosecuted by the Central Bureau of Investigation (CBI).

Both tracks reached their legal conclusions.

---

### 1. The Criminal Investigation & Trial

* **Transfer to the CBI:** Following severe public outrage, allegations that police helpline calls went unanswered, and the initial arrest of three innocent individuals by the local police, the **Allahabad High Court transferred the entire investigation to the CBI** in August 2016.
* **The Chargesheets:** The CBI filed its initial chargesheet in late 2016 and a supplementary chargesheet in 2018 against members of a nomadic criminal gang involved in highway robberies and sexual assault.
* **Key Evidence:** The prosecution examined 25 witnesses and relied heavily on **forensic DNA analysis**. Notably, DNA matching one of the accused recovered from the clothes of the victim's mother served as definitive scientific proof.
* **Accused Status during Trial:** Prime accused Salim Bawariya died in judicial custody in 2019 while the trial was ongoing, and two other gang members were killed in separate police encounters, leaving five accused to face the final judgment.

---

### 2. The Final Criminal Verdict

In **December 2025**, after a nine-year trial, the **Special POCSO Court / Additional Sessions Court in Bulandshahr** delivered the final judgment:

* **Conviction & Sentence:** All five surviving accused—Juber, Sajid, Dharamvir, Naresh, and Sunil Kumar—were **convicted and sentenced to life imprisonment** for gang rape, dacoity, and wrongful confinement.
* **Monetary Fine:** The court imposed a fine of ₹1.81 lakh on each convict, directing that the recovered fine amount be distributed equally between the two survivors (the mother and her minor daughter).

---

### 3. The Constitutional & Political Fallout

* **Azam Khan's Unconditional Apology:** Before the larger questions on ministerial free speech were referred to the 5-judge Constitution Bench, the Supreme Court took strong exception to then-UP Minister Azam Khan's public comments. The Court compelled him to tender an **unconditional written apology** to the survivors and the Court for calling the incident a "political conspiracy."
* **Creation of Constitutional Precedent:** The victim's petition under Article 32 directly resulted in the Supreme Court's 5-judge Constitution Bench ruling in **Kaushal Kishor v. State of U.P. (2023)**, which established that:
1. Article 19(2) speech restrictions are exhaustive and cannot be expanded by judges to gag ministers.
2. Articles 19 and 21 are enforceable horizontally against non-state actors.
3. A minister's personal statements cannot be vicariously attributed to the State under collective responsibility.
