### Water resource distribution in India
1. Intro
2. current water need of India
3. current water availability
4. Factors for water stress in India
5. Water resource distribution in India
6. Measures to improve water efficiency
7. Conclusion
 
1. Intro
	- India's water challenge is less a simple shortage than a mismatch between seasonal supply, spatial distribution, rising demand and declining quality.
2. current water need of India
	1. Agriculture remains the largest user of freshwater, followed by domestic and industrial demand.
	2. Demand rises with population, urbanisation, irrigation, energy, industry and changing consumption.
	3. **Source:** NITI Aayog, Composite Water Management Index, 2018; sector shares vary by methodology.
3. current water availability
	1. India receives about 4,000 BCM of annual precipitation, but only a limited portion is utilizable because of seasonality, floods, evaporation and storage constraints.
	2. Per-capita availability has declined with population growth. **Source: Central Water Commission, basin-wise water-resource assessments.**
4. Factors for water stress in India
	1. Monsoon concentration and regional variability.
	2. Groundwater over-extraction, inefficient flood irrigation and water-intensive crops.
	3. Pollution from sewage, industry, agriculture and solid waste.
	4. Wetland loss, urban concretisation, climate extremes and fragmented governance.
5. Water resource distribution in India
	1. Himalayan rivers are relatively perennial; peninsular rivers are more seasonal and monsoon-dependent.
	2. Brahmaputra, Ganga and Godavari basins have substantial resources, while western Rajasthan, Deccan interiors and parts of Tamil Nadu face higher stress.
	3. Groundwater is important in alluvial plains but discontinuous in hard-rock peninsular terrain.
	4. **Source:** Central Water Commission basin assessments; estimates vary by hydrological series.
6. Measures to improve water efficiency
	1. Drip and sprinkler irrigation, crop diversification and millets in stressed regions.
	2. Aquifer mapping, groundwater budgeting, community monitoring and energy-pricing reform.
	3. Reuse treated wastewater for industry, landscaping and non-potable urban uses.
	4. Restore wetlands, lakes, floodplains and catchments; expand rainwater harvesting and watershed development.
	5. Strengthen river-basin institutions, pollution enforcement and climate-resilient infrastructure.
	6. Jal Jeevan Mission, Atal Bhujal Yojana, National Water Mission, AMRUT and PMKSY provide policy support.
7. Conclusion
	- Water-secure India will emerge through basin-level conservation, reuse, efficient crops and accountable community stewardship.
