1. Intro
	- Solar energy converts India's abundant sunlight into clean electricity and has emerged as the fastest-scaling pillar of the country's energy transition.
2. Evolution short story point wise
	1. Photovoltaic cells convert sunlight directly into electricity, while concentrated solar power uses mirrors to produce heat and steam.
	2. Falling module prices, better efficiency and grid-connected policy support made utility-scale solar commercially viable.
	3. India launched the Jawaharlal Nehru National Solar Mission in 2010 and later placed solar at the centre of its renewable-energy expansion.
	4. The transition has widened from large parks to rooftops, agriculture feeders, solar pumps, mini-grids and green-hydrogen production.
3. India's progress till date: data
	- India crossed 100 GW of installed solar capacity in 2025 and has become one of the world's largest solar markets; solar is now the largest component of India's renewable-energy capacity.
	- Capacity includes utility-scale parks, rooftop systems, off-grid plants and solar pumps, with strong regional variation.
4. India's target (mention commitment if any)
	1. Achieve 500 GW of non-fossil electricity capacity by 2030.
	2. Meet 50 percent of cumulative installed electricity capacity from non-fossil sources by 2030.
	3. Reduce the emissions intensity of GDP by 45 percent from 2005 levels by 2030 and achieve net-zero emissions by 2070.
	4. The National Solar Mission and PM Surya Ghar: Muft Bijli Yojana support utility-scale and household rooftop expansion.
5. Regional Variations - solar plant distribution
	1. Rajasthan and Gujarat: Highest potential due to high insolation, arid land and large solar parks.
	2. Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh: Large central and decentralised projects.
	3. Tamil Nadu and Telangana: Strong solar growth supported by industrial demand and grid connectivity.
	4. Himalayan and North-Eastern states: Lower large-scale potential in some areas, but scope for rooftops and decentralised systems.
6. Benefits
	1. Clean, modular and scalable, from household rooftops to multi-gigawatt parks.
	2. Reduces coal use, import dependence and local air pollution.
	3. Supports energy access, irrigation, rural enterprises and green jobs.
	4. Enables electric mobility, green hydrogen and decentralised power.
7. Challenges
	1. Intermittency, evening peak mismatch and the need for storage and flexible generation.
	2. Land acquisition, habitat fragmentation and transmission bottlenecks.
	3. Dependence on imported cells and modules, critical minerals and global supply chains.
	4. Rooftop barriers such as finance, consumer awareness, net-metering rules and distribution-company concerns.
	5. End-of-life panel waste and recycling need a circular-economy framework.
8. Key govt initiatives
	- National Solar Mission; Solar Parks Scheme; PM-KUSUM for solar pumps and decentralised plants; PM Surya Ghar for rooftop solar; Production Linked Incentive scheme for high-efficiency solar PV modules; and International Solar Alliance.
9. Conclusion
	- A solar-led transition backed by storage, domestic manufacturing, recycling and fair land and grid planning can deliver affordable power while advancing India's climate and development goals.
