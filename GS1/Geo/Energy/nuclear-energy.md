1. Intro
	- Nuclear energy offers dense, low-carbon and round-the-clock electricity, making it important for India's energy security alongside rapid renewable expansion.
2. Evolution (step by step point wise)
	1. Homi J. Bhabha established India's atomic-energy vision and institutional base after independence.
	2. The Department of Atomic Energy and Atomic Energy Commission were created in 1954 and 1958 respectively.
	3. The **three-stage programme** was designed around India's limited uranium and abundant thorium: PHWRs using natural uranium, fast breeder reactors using plutonium, and thorium-based reactors producing U-233.
	4. Indigenous PHWR technology expanded nuclear generation, while international cooperation after the 2008 waiver improved access to fuel and technology.
	5. The next phase includes indigenous 700 MW PHWRs, fast breeder technology and small modular reactor research.
3. India's progress till date: data - current capacity
	- India has about 8.2 GW of installed nuclear capacity from 24 commercial reactors, supplying around 3 percent of electricity generation; capacity is expected to rise with projects under construction and approval.
4. India's target
	- India aims to raise nuclear capacity to about 22.5 GW by 2031-32 and has set a long-term goal of 100 GW by 2047, while pursuing net-zero emissions by 2070.
5. Major plant distribution
	1. **Tarapur, Maharashtra:** India's first commercial nuclear power station.
	2. **Rawatbhata, Rajasthan; Kakrapar, Gujarat; Narora, Uttar Pradesh; Kaiga, Karnataka:** Major PHWR clusters.
	3. **Kudankulam, Tamil Nadu:** VVER-based reactors developed with Russian cooperation.
	4. **Kalpakkam, Tamil Nadu:** Fast breeder and advanced nuclear research centre.
	5. **Jaitapur, Maharashtra and Kovvada, Andhra Pradesh:** Proposed large-project sites.
6. Benefits
	1. High energy density and reliable baseload power with low life-cycle carbon emissions.
	2. Small land footprint compared with equivalent fossil or renewable generation.
	3. Supports industrialisation, medical isotopes, food irradiation, agriculture and strategic autonomy.
	4. Reduces coal use, air pollution and exposure to imported hydrocarbons.
7. Availability of raw materials
	- Uranium resources occur in Jharkhand, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Rajasthan and Meghalaya; mining and processing are undertaken through UCIL.
	- India has large thorium resources in monazite-bearing beach sands of Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and Odisha, but commercial thorium utilisation requires the successful completion of the three-stage programme.
8. Challenges
	1. High capital cost, long construction periods and project delays.
	2. Safety, radioactive-waste management and emergency preparedness concerns.
	3. Land acquisition, public acceptance and coastal vulnerability at some sites.
	4. Fuel constraints, technology access and liability concerns.
	5. Nuclear plants require substantial cooling water and careful thermal-discharge management.
9. Key govt initiatives
	- Department of Atomic Energy and NPCIL reactor programme; Bharatiya Nabhikiya Vidyut Nigam for fast breeder technology; the Atomic Energy Act, 1962; liability framework under the Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act, 2010; and recent policy efforts to enable greater private participation and small modular reactor research.
10. Conclusion
	- A transparent, safety-first nuclear programme integrated with renewables, storage and responsible waste management can strengthen India's pathway to reliable net-zero growth.
