1. Intro
	- Geothermal energy is the heat stored within the Earth, a reliable renewable resource that can provide electricity and direct heating with a small land footprint.
2. How geothermal energy converted into electricity ?
	1. **Exploration:** Geological, geochemical and seismic surveys identify hot rocks, reservoirs and fractures.
	2. **Drilling:** Production wells bring hot water or steam to the surface, while injection wells return cooled water underground.
	3. **Power generation:** Steam directly drives a turbine in a dry-steam plant; hot water is flashed into steam in a flash plant; moderate-temperature water heats a secondary fluid in a binary-cycle plant.
	4. **Reinjection and monitoring:** Water is reinjected to maintain reservoir pressure and reduce surface pollution and subsidence.
3. India's progress till now: data
	- India has negligible commercial geothermal electricity generation, although it has substantial low-to-moderate temperature resources for direct heating, bathing, greenhouse cultivation and industrial uses.
	- Estimated potential is often placed at about 10 GW, but the figure is indicative because exploration and resource assessment remain incomplete.
4. Major plant location
	1. **Puga and Chumathang, Ladakh:** High-temperature springs with potential for power and heating in remote settlements.
	2. **Tattapani, Chhattisgarh:** Important hot-spring belt with power-generation potential.
	3. **Cambay Basin, Gujarat:** Sedimentary basin with geothermal prospects.
	4. **West Coast, Son-Narmada-Tapi belt and Godavari basin:** Warm springs and tectonic features suitable mainly for direct-use applications.
5. Ongoing plans-targets
	- Priorities are systematic mapping, pilot binary-cycle plants, district heating, geothermal heat pumps, and hybridisation with solar power in remote areas.
	- Public-private exploration, risk-sharing for unsuccessful drilling and integration with the National Geothermal Energy Policy framework can improve commercial viability.
6. Benefits
	1. Provides firm, round-the-clock renewable power unlike intermittent solar and wind.
	2. Has low operational emissions and a small land requirement.
	3. Supports local heating, greenhouse farming, tourism and livelihood diversification.
	4. Reduces diesel dependence in remote and cold regions such as Ladakh.
7. Challenges
	1. High upfront cost and drilling risk because the resource is site-specific.
	2. Difficult terrain and weak grid connectivity at promising locations.
	3. Scaling, corrosion and emissions of dissolved gases can affect plant life.
	4. Reinjection must be carefully managed to prevent induced seismicity and reservoir depletion.
8. Key govt initiatives
	- Geological Survey of India resource assessment; Ministry of New and Renewable Energy support for renewable-energy research and pilots; and state-level exploration by agencies such as ONGC and state power corporations.
9. Conclusion
	- With scientific exploration, risk-sharing finance and direct-use applications, geothermal energy can become a dependable complement to India's solar-wind transition.
