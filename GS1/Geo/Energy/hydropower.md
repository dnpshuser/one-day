1. Intro
	- Hydropower converts the potential and kinetic energy of flowing or stored water into electricity and remains India's largest source of dispatchable renewable power.
2. India's progress till date: data
	- India has about 47 GW of large hydropower capacity and about 5 GW of small hydropower capacity, besides several projects under construction; large hydropower is treated as renewable energy.
	- Hydropower contributes roughly one-tenth of installed electricity capacity, though its generation share varies with monsoon, reservoir levels and competing water demands.
3. Major plant distribution in India
	1. **Himalayan region:** Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Jammu and Kashmir, and Sikkim have steep gradients and perennial rivers, but face seismic and ecological sensitivity.
	2. **North-East:** Arunachal Pradesh has the country's largest untapped potential due to high rainfall, relief and river discharge.
	3. **Western Ghats:** Maharashtra, Karnataka and Kerala support projects on steep west-flowing rivers.
	4. **Central and southern India:** Reservoir-based and multipurpose projects occur on the Narmada, Godavari, Krishna and Mahanadi systems.
4. Ongoing plans-targets
	- Priorities include completing viable projects, pumped-storage plants for balancing solar and wind, modernising old stations, and strengthening inter-state transmission.
	- Pumped storage acts like a water battery by storing surplus electricity and releasing it during peak demand.
5. Benefits
	1. Renewable, flexible and capable of rapid peaking support.
	2. Supports irrigation, drinking water, flood moderation, inland fisheries and regional development.
	3. Low operating emissions and long plant life after construction.
	4. Reduces dependence on imported fossil fuels and supports grid stability.
6. Challenges
	1. Submergence, displacement, forest loss and disruption of river ecology.
	2. Sedimentation reduces reservoir capacity and turbine life.
	3. Landslides, glacial lake outburst floods and earthquakes increase Himalayan risks.
	4. Long gestation, cost overruns, inter-state disputes and downstream flow concerns.
	5. Climate change makes river flows and project economics more uncertain.
7. Key govt initiatives
	- National Hydroelectric Power Corporation and state utilities; National Mission for Clean Ganga; revised dam-safety framework under the Dam Safety Act, 2021; and policy support for pumped-storage projects and hydropower in the renewable-energy portfolio.
8. Conclusion
	- Future hydropower must move from dam-led expansion to basin-based planning that combines safety, ecological flows, rehabilitation and storage for a resilient clean-energy grid.
