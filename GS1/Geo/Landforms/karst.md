### Karst topography
1. Intro
Karst topography is a distinctive landform developed on soluble rocks such as limestone, dolomite, and gypsum, where chemical weathering creates underground drainage and surface depressions. It is a classic example of how geology, water, and time shape the landscape.

2. How formed
   - Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and soil, becoming mildly acidic carbonic acid.
   - This acidic water dissolves calcium carbonate in limestone, creating joints, cracks, and cavities.
   - Over time, continuous dissolution enlarges underground channels, caves, sinkholes, and dolines.
   - When the roof of a cave collapses, surface depressions or swallow holes may form.
   - The process is enhanced in regions with abundant rainfall, permeable rock, and well-developed fracture systems.
   - Source: Geomorphology texts; Indian geomorphology references.

3. importance/significance
   - Karst regions often have underground drainage systems, which make surface rivers scarce but groundwater resources significant.
   - They create unique landforms like caves, stalactites, stalagmites, sinkholes, and uvalas, which are important for tourism and scientific study.
   - Karst aquifers are vital sources of groundwater for drinking and irrigation in many regions.
   - However, they are highly vulnerable to pollution because water moves quickly through subterranean channels with limited filtration.
   - In India, karst landscapes are found in parts of the Himalaya, peninsular plateau, and some cave-bearing regions, where they influence local hydrology and ecology.

4. Conclusion
Karst topography shows the dynamic role of chemical weathering in shaping the Earth’s surface, while also highlighting the need for sustainable groundwater protection in fragile limestone terrains. Source: geomorphology texts and hydrology studies.
