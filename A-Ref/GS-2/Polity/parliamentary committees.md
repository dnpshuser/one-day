# Parliamentary Committees

## 1. Intro

* Parliamentary Committees are the specialized, sub-legislative bodies established by the Parliament to scrutinize legislation, oversee executive functioning, and ensure financial accountability in greater detail than is possible on the floor of the House.
* They function as non-partisan "mini-parliaments" away from the glare of cameras and public posturing, forming the backbone of the legislative process and upholding the principle that the executive remains continuously accountable to the legislature.

## 2. Sub-components of the topic [most imp]

* **Departmentally Related Standing Committees (DRSCs):** Currently 24 in number (16 under Lok Sabha, 8 under Rajya Sabha). Each consists of 31 members (21 LS, 10 RS). They examine Demands for Grants, scrutinize bills referred to them, and review annual reports of respective ministries.
* **Public Accounts Committee (PAC):** The oldest financial committee, traditionally headed by a senior leader of the principal opposition party. It audits the government’s revenue and expenditure, relying heavily on the CAG's reports to detect financial impropriety.
* **Estimates Committee:** Consists exclusively of 30 members from the Lok Sabha. It examines if the funds provided in the estimates are laid out effectively and suggests alternative policies to bring about efficiency and economy in administration.
* **Committee on Public Undertakings (CoPU):** Examines the reports and accounts of Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) and the CAG reports on them, ensuring commercial efficiency without interfering in day-to-day administration.
* **Ad-Hoc vs. Standing:** Standing Committees are permanent and reconstituted annually, while Ad-Hoc committees (like Select Committees or Joint Parliamentary Committees - JPCs) are formed for a specific purpose (e.g., investigating a scam or a highly contentious bill) and dissolve once their task is over.
* **In-Camera Proceedings:** Committee meetings are held behind closed doors. This crucial feature allows MPs to cross party lines, question bureaucrats directly, and debate policies objectively without the pressure of party whips or media grandstanding.

## 3. Evolution timeline

* **1921 (The Inception):** The Public Accounts Committee (PAC) was established in the Central Legislative Assembly under the Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms, marking the genesis of financial oversight in India.
* **1950 (Estimates Committee):** Formed post-independence on the recommendation of then Finance Minister John Mathai to specifically scrutinize the government's budget estimates.
* **1964 (CoPU Creation):** The Committee on Public Undertakings was carved out based on the Krishna Menon Committee report to provide dedicated oversight of India's growing public sector.
* **1993 (The DRSC Era):** Parliament formally instituted 17 Departmentally Related Standing Committees to ensure dedicated micro-scrutiny of every specific ministry's legislation and budget.
* **2004 (Expansion):** The number of DRSCs was expanded from 17 to 24 to cover newer and more complex ministries, creating the modern committee architecture.
* **2009-2014 (The Golden Age of Scrutiny):** During the 15th Lok Sabha, an impressive 71% of all bills introduced were referred to committees for detailed scrutiny.
* **2014-2026 (The Steep Decline):** Committee referrals plummeted to 25% (16th LS) and then a mere 16% (17th LS). PRS Legislative Research data shows this trend of bypassing committees continued into the 18th Lok Sabha, with over 50% of bills in initial sessions passing without committee review.

## 5. Committee or reports [latest is best or some report in the past with very large impact]

* **PRS Legislative Research (17th and 18th Lok Sabha Vital Stats):** Repeatedly flags the systemic decline in committee scrutiny. Their 2026 reports reveal that a staggering number of bills are passed within a week of introduction, completely circumventing DRSCs, which leads to poorly drafted laws facing immediate judicial challenge.
* **National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (NCRWC, 2002):** Strongly recommended that referring major legislative bills to the DRSCs should be made mandatory, rather than leaving it to the absolute discretion of the Speaker/Chairman.
* **Rules Committee of the Rajya Sabha:** Periodically reviews the functioning of committees. It has previously highlighted absenteeism among MPs during committee meetings and recommended disciplinary measures against members with chronically low attendance.

## 6. Indices [better which shows India's ranking]

* **Democracy Index (Economist Intelligence Unit):** India's "Functioning of Government" parameter is negatively impacted when the executive bypasses committee scrutiny, reducing the legislature's ability to check executive power.
* **V-Dem (Varieties of Democracy) Deliberative Component Index:** Measures the depth of respect for opposing views and the quality of debate. Passing laws without committee scrutiny directly lowers this index, as it reflects a majoritarian bulldozing of legislation.
* **Rule of Law Index (World Justice Project):** The "Constraints on Government Powers" and "Open Government" metrics suffer when committees (which often invite public/expert feedback) are bypassed in the law-making process.

## 7. Global comparison

* **United States (Powerful Gatekeepers):** In the US Senate and House, standing committees are exceptionally powerful. A committee can completely rewrite, indefinitely stall ("pigeonhole"), or kill an executive bill. The executive cannot force a vote on the floor without committee approval.
* **United Kingdom (Pre-Legislative Scrutiny):** The UK utilizes Joint Committees extensively for scrutinizing "draft bills" (Green and White Papers) long before they are formally introduced on the floor, ensuring policy consensus and removing drafting errors early on.
* **Australia (Strict Adherence):** The Australian Senate’s Scrutiny of Bills Committee evaluates *every single bill* against a set of strict civil liberty and delegation principles, regardless of the government's urgency, a practice completely absent in India.

## 9. Flow chart

**The Journey of a Bill through a Committee**

1. **Introduction:** Bill is introduced in the Lok Sabha or Rajya Sabha.
2. **Referral:** The Speaker/Chairman uses their discretion to refer the Bill to the relevant DRSC.
3. **Internal Deliberation:** The Committee reads the Bill clause-by-clause in-camera.
4. **Stakeholder Consultation:** Committee invites subject experts, NGOs, industry leaders, and citizens for written and oral evidence.
5. **Bureaucratic Examination:** Ministry secretaries are summoned to explain and defend the Bill's provisions.
6. **Drafting the Report:** The Committee drafts a report containing recommended amendments (dissent notes by opposing MPs are recorded).
7. **Submission:** The report is tabled in Parliament.
8. **Final Passage:** The government may accept or reject the recommendations before passing the Bill on the floor. (Recommendations are persuasive, not binding).

## 10. Recent occurrence - debate

* **18th Lok Sabha Committee Constitution (Late 2024):** The reconstitution of 24 DRSCs and financial committees occurred in September 2024. Debates highlighted the partisan allocation of chairmanships, though conventions were somewhat maintained (e.g., Congress's K.C. Venugopal heading the PAC, and opposition MPs heading specific DRSCs like External Affairs and IT).
* **Bypassing the DRSCs for Crucial Laws:** The massive debate surrounding the passage of the new Criminal Codes (BNS, BNSS, BSA) and the Telecommunications Act. While some initial drafts went to committees, final dramatically altered versions were passed during the mass suspension of 146 MPs without fresh committee scrutiny.
* **The Joint Parliamentary Committee (JPC) Demand:** Continual opposition demands for JPCs on massive financial controversies (like the Adani-Hindenburg issue or the SEBI chairperson allegations) were outright rejected by the government, sparking debates that the executive fears cross-examination by multi-party committees.

## 11. Advantage/Merits/Role

* **Micro-Scrutiny and Expertise:** Committees have the time and mandate to break down complex legislation (like data protection or financial regulation) line-by-line, utilizing domain experts—a task impossible on the chaotic floor of the House.
* **Bipartisan Consensus:** Because proceedings are held in-camera (no media) and the anti-defection whip does not apply, MPs often rise above party lines, analyzing issues objectively and reaching unanimous consensus on technical policy matters.
* **Executive Accountability:** Committees regularly summon high-ranking IAS officers (Secretaries) to answer for budgetary lapses, unspent funds, and policy failures, ensuring the permanent bureaucracy remains answerable to the people's representatives.
* **Stakeholder Participation:** Committees provide the only formal platform for civil society, industry leaders, and citizens to directly influence the legislative drafting process through evidence sessions.
* **Financial Prudence:** Financial committees (PAC, Estimates) act as watchdogs of the public purse, identifying wasteful expenditure, recovering lost revenue, and ensuring funds are spent exactly as authorized by Parliament.

## 12. Disadvantage/De-Merits

* **Non-Binding Recommendations:** The biggest structural weakness is that the government is not legally bound to accept DRSC recommendations. Often, extensive committee reports are simply ignored by the executive.
* **Absenteeism and Apathy:** Committee meetings frequently suffer from low attendance. Many MPs view committee work as unglamorous (since it doesn't get media coverage) and skip crucial meetings, breaking the quorum.
* **Lack of Specialized Support:** Unlike US Congressional committees that have massive, dedicated research staffs, Indian committees rely on generalist Parliament Secretariat staff, lacking the deep technical capacity needed to scrutinize modern, highly complex legislation.
* **Post-Mortem Nature (PAC):** The Public Accounts Committee only examines expenditure *after* it has been incurred (based on CAG reports). It acts as a post-mortem tool rather than a preventive mechanism against ongoing financial mismanagement.

## 13. Challenges

* **Discretion of the Speaker:** There is no rule mandating that every bill must go to a committee. The absolute discretion of the Speaker/Chairman allows the ruling party to deliberately bypass committees for controversial bills under the guise of "urgency."
* **Short Tenure:** Members and Chairpersons are nominated for only one year. This short tenure prevents MPs from developing deep domain expertise in the specific ministry their committee oversees.
* **Politicization of Chairmanships:** The allocation of committee chairmanships has increasingly become a tool of political patronage or punishment, rather than being based on an MP's specific subject-matter expertise.
* **Information Asymmetry:** Committees depend entirely on the respective Ministries to provide data and files. The executive often delays or redacts critical information, stonewalling the committee's investigation.

## 14. Way forward / solution / positive outlook

* **Mandatory Referral Rule (NCRWC):** The Rules of Procedure must be amended to make DRSC referrals mandatory for all bills (except Money Bills or explicit national security emergencies). Any exemption should require a 2/3rd majority vote on the floor of the House.
* **Action Taken Reports (ATR) Enforcement:** The government must be made statutorily bound to table an "Action Taken Report" within 60 days, explaining precisely *why* it rejected specific committee recommendations on a bill.
* **Extended Tenure and Research Support:** The tenure of DRSC members should be extended to a minimum of 2.5 to 3 years to build domain expertise. Committees must be provided with independent, specialized research staffs and dedicated budgets.
* **Public Broadcasting:** While internal deliberations should remain in-camera to protect bipartisanship, the final evidence-gathering sessions (where experts and bureaucrats testify) should be broadcasted publicly, enhancing transparency and civic engagement.

## 15. Best practices

* **United Kingdom (Pre-Legislative Scrutiny):** The UK extensively utilizes Ad-Hoc Joint Committees to scrutinize "Draft Bills." This allows structural changes to be made *before* the government stakes its political prestige on formally introducing the bill on the floor.
* **Australia (Scrutiny of Bills Committee):** An independent committee that evaluates every single introduced bill purely against human rights and civil liberty principles, regardless of the politics behind the bill, ensuring a baseline of constitutional morality.
* **India (The Personal Data Protection Bill, 2019):** A rare but excellent domestic example. The bill was sent to a Joint Parliamentary Committee, which spent over two years consulting global tech giants, privacy activists, and legal experts. While the bill was ultimately withdrawn, the exhaustive scrutiny process itself was a textbook example of how complex legislation should be handled.

## 16. Conclusion

* Parliamentary Committees are the quiet workhorses of Indian democracy. They are the crucial filters that transform hasty executive drafts into robust, well-rounded national laws.
* The alarming trend of bypassing these committees threatens to hollow out the legislative process. Reinstating their primacy—by making scrutiny mandatory and empowering them with research capacities—is non-negotiable for preserving the institutional depth of Parliament.
