# States' Decreased Financial Power

## 1. Intro

* Fiscal federalism in India is structurally designed with a vertical imbalance: the Centre has the most buoyant taxation powers (Income Tax, Corporate Tax), while the States shoulder the bulk of developmental and welfare expenditure responsibilities.
* In recent years, states have increasingly voiced grievances that their fiscal autonomy is being systematically eroded through the Centre's heavy reliance on non-divisible cesses, rigid borrowing ceilings, and the proliferation of Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS).

## 2. Sub-components of the topic [most imp]

* **The Divisible Pool vs. Cesses/Surcharges:** Article 270 mandates the sharing of net central taxes with states (the Divisible Pool). However, Article 271 allows the Centre to levy cesses and surcharges which are *exclusive* to the Centre. The Centre has increasingly relied on these to raise revenue, shrinking the actual pool of money shared with states.
* **Borrowing Limits (Article 293):** States cannot borrow without the Centre's consent if they owe any debt to the Centre (which all states do). The Centre enforces a strict Net Borrowing Ceiling (NBC)—currently capped at around 3% of GSDP—and uses this to control state fiscal behavior, even restricting off-budget borrowings.
* **Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS):** Schemes designed by the Centre but implemented by the States, requiring states to provide matching grants (usually 40%). With over 130 such schemes, states are forced to divert their limited funds to central priorities, undermining their own localized electoral mandates.
* **GST Compensation Cess Expiry:** The transition to the Goods and Services Tax (GST) in 2017 removed the states' autonomy to set indirect tax rates. The 5-year guaranteed compensation for revenue loss expired in 2022, leaving many states facing severe fiscal cliffs.
* **Terms of Reference of Finance Commissions:** Recent commissions (15th and 16th) have introduced conditionalities for local body grants and shifted horizontal devolution formulas (e.g., giving weight to "Contribution to GDP"), which states argue encroaches upon their fiscal planning.

## 3. Evolution timeline

* 1950: Constitutional Design
The Constitution granted the Centre the power to levy surcharges (Article 271) explicitly for specific, temporary purposes, not as a permanent revenue stream.


* 2012: Cess Share at 9.4%
Cesses and surcharges constituted a relatively modest 9.4% of the Centre's Gross Tax Revenue.


* 2015: 14th Finance Commission
Recommended a historic leap in the states' share of the divisible pool from 32% to 42%. However, the Centre immediately began increasing cesses to offset this loss.


* 2017: Implementation of GST
States surrendered their independent power to levy sales tax/VAT, pooling their sovereignty into the GST Council in exchange for a 5-year compensation guarantee.


* 2020-2022: Peak Cess Reliance & GST End
The share of cesses and surcharges in the Centre's gross tax revenue spiked to over 15%, effectively bypassing the Finance Commission's devolution mandate. Concurrently, the GST compensation period ended.


* 2024: Kerala vs. Union of India (Article 293)
The Kerala government sued the Centre in the Supreme Court over the imposition of strict Net Borrowing Ceilings (NBC), challenging the Centre's absolute power to regulate state open-market borrowing. The SC referred it to a Constitution Bench.


* 2026: 16th Finance Commission Report
The 16th FC maintained the divisible pool share at 41%. It introduced a new "Contribution to GDP" (10% weight) parameter, and explicitly flagged the excessive use of non-shareable cesses by the Centre as shrinking the actual pool for states.

## 5. Committee or Reports

* **16th Finance Commission Report (2026):** Maintained the vertical devolution at 41%. Critically, it *flagged the excessive use of cesses and surcharges* by the Centre, noting that it shrinks the actual net proceeds available for states. It also recommended capping the state fiscal deficit at 3% of GSDP, enforcing strict fiscal discipline.
* **Sarkaria Commission (1988):** Warned against the proliferation of Centrally Sponsored Schemes, recommending that their number should be kept to an absolute minimum to preserve the autonomy of state expenditure.
* **Reserve Bank of India (State Finances: A Study of Budgets):** Routinely highlights that states' own tax revenues have stagnated at around 6% of GDP post-GST. It points out that states pay a "differential interest rate" (borrowing at ~10% while the Centre borrows at ~7%), increasing their debt servicing burden.

## 6. Indices

* **Gross Tax Revenue (GTR) Composition:** The most critical metric. The non-divisible pool (cesses/surcharges) rose from ~9.4% of the Centre's GTR in 2012 to over 15.7% recently. This means that out of every ₹100 collected by the Centre, ₹15 is completely kept out of the sharing formula.
* **Tax-to-GDP Ratio:** India's overall tax-to-GDP ratio remains low (around 17%). Because the overall pie is small, the battle between the Centre and States over the slice is highly contentious.
* **State-wise Per Capita GSDP:** Disparities here fuel the horizontal devolution fight. Southern states (higher per capita) argue they are penalized for their economic success to fund poorer Northern states, while their own borrowing limits are capped.

## 7. Global comparison

* **United States:** States have immense fiscal autonomy. They independently levy income taxes, sales taxes, and property taxes. The federal government cannot impose borrowing ceilings on states; state debt is regulated by the market and individual state constitutions.
* **Canada:** Highly decentralized. Provinces collect a significant portion of their own revenue and receive unconditional "Equalization Payments" from the federal government, allowing them total freedom in expenditure.
* **Australia:** Similar to India, there is a Vertical Fiscal Imbalance where the Commonwealth (Centre) collects most taxes (like GST) and distributes it. However, the distribution is managed by an independent Commonwealth Grants Commission, which operates with strict neutrality.

## 9. Flow chart

**The Centrally Sponsored Scheme (CSS) Trap**

1. **Centre's Action:** Centre designs a new CSS (e.g., PM Awas Yojana) leveraging its buoyant tax revenue.
2. **State's Dilemma:** To receive central funds, the State must agree to implement the scheme.
3. **The Matching Grant:** The Centre mandates a 60:40 funding ratio.
4. **Fiscal Diversion:** The State is forced to divert its own scarce funds to meet the 40% matching requirement.
5. **The Autonomy Loss:**
* State is unable to fund its own locally mandated election promises.
* A "one-size-fits-all" policy is imposed on a diverse demographic.


6. **Result:** State acts merely as an implementation agency rather than an autonomous policy-maker.

## 10. Recent occurrence - debate

* **Kerala's Supreme Court Suit (2024):** Kerala filed a historic original suit (Article 131) against the Union, arguing that the Centre's imposition of a strict Net Borrowing Ceiling (NBC)—and counting off-budget borrowings of state-owned enterprises within that limit—violates the state's executive power under Article 293. The SC referred this uncharted constitutional question to a 5-judge Constitution Bench.
* **The 16th Finance Commission Devolution:** Despite states demanding a 50% share, the 16th FC retained the 41% formula for 2026-31. Southern states are heavily debating the new "Contribution to GDP" parameter (10% weight) and the reduced "Income Distance" parameter, arguing it still doesn't adequately reward their demographic and economic efforts.
* **Withholding of Funds:** States like West Bengal have frequently accused the Centre of withholding MGNREGA and PM Awas Yojana funds over "compliance" issues, weaponizing financial transfers for political leverage.

## 11. Challenges

* **Subversion of the Finance Commission:** The constitutional intent of the Finance Commission is defeated when the Centre raises revenue through Article 271 (cesses) instead of regular taxes, legally keeping those funds out of the devolution formula.
* **Erosion of State Priorities:** The proliferation of CSS forces states to abandon their own localized welfare priorities to co-fund the Centre's vision, turning the federal structure into a unitary administrative system.
* **Infrastructure Stagnation:** With a hard 3% fiscal deficit cap and restrictions on off-budget borrowing (like the Kerala Infrastructure Investment Fund Board), states lack the capital to build long-term infrastructure, hindering overall national GDP growth.
* **The Trust Deficit in the GST Council:** The cessation of GST compensation has led states to feel they surrendered their taxation sovereignty without a permanent safety net, eroding the spirit of "cooperative federalism" within the GST Council.

## 12. Way forward / Solution / Positive Outlook

* **Capping Cesses and Surcharges (16th FC Echo):** The Constitution should be amended to place a hard cap (e.g., 5-10% of Gross Tax Revenue) on the amount the Centre can raise via cesses, ensuring the divisible pool remains robust.
* **Rationalization of CSS:** Following the recommendations of various NITI Aayog sub-groups, the number of CSS should be drastically reduced. Central transfers should move toward "untied block grants," allowing states the flexibility to design schemes suited to their local topography and demographics.
* **Reviewing Article 293:** The impending Supreme Court Constitution Bench decision on Kerala's suit must clearly delineate the boundaries of the Centre's "consent" under Article 293, ensuring it cannot be used arbitrarily to suffocate state finances.
* **Strengthening the Inter-State Council:** Financial disputes should be proactively mediated through the Inter-State Council (Article 263) rather than forcing states to drag the Union to the Supreme Court.

## 13. Best practices

* **Australia's GST Distribution:** Australia distributes its entire GST revenue to the states using a principle of "Horizontal Fiscal Equalization" managed by an independent commission, ensuring states have robust, untied revenue without constant central interference.
* **India's 16th FC "Urbanisation Premium":** The 16th FC's approach to local body grants—tying performance metrics strictly to public disclosure of audited accounts and property tax collection—is a positive practice to force fiscal discipline at the local tier, which state governments should emulate.
* **Canada's Equalization Program:** Provides unconditional transfers to less wealthy provinces, allowing them to provide public services at comparable levels to wealthier provinces without the federal government dictating *how* the money is spent (unlike India's CSS).

## 14. Conclusion

* A strong Centre cannot exist without financially resilient States, as the actual delivery of governance, health, and infrastructure happens at the state level.
* Continual fiscal centralization—through cesses, stringent borrowing caps, and tied grants—threatens to reduce state governments to mere glorified municipalities. Restoring fiscal equilibrium is imperative to uphold the constitutional promise of a truly cooperative federal republic.
